Isaias is the first Atlantic hurricane of 2026 and the latest first hurricane ever recorded. It has already taken 511,000 barrels a day off the market

Energy & grid Science & public health New thread Independent corroboration

Isaias became a hurricane on 6 October over the southwestern Gulf. The National Hurricane Center’s 2am outlook this morning carries advisories on it and expects no other formation for seven days. Forecasters describe it as the latest first-forming Atlantic hurricane in reliable records, by a wide margin — the season’s first hurricane arriving in October rather than August.

The track: roughly 400 miles south-southwest of New Orleans, moving east-northeast and expected to turn north, with landfall most likely between the western Florida Panhandle and the Mississippi coast late Friday or early Saturday. One forecast has it peaking at Category 3 with weakening before landfall; another puts Category 2 at landfall. Surge up to 7 feet along about 300 miles of coast, and 3 to 7 inches of rain.

The number that reaches a reader nowhere near the Gulf is production. As of Wednesday operators had shut in about 25% of the region’s oil output — roughly 511,000 barrels a day. Shell is shutting in Mars, Olympus, Ursa, Vito and Appomattox; Chevron four of its nine operated facilities. Only 8 of 371 manned platforms had been evacuated, which shows how little the platform count tells you about the barrels.

The risk that matters is refineries, not platforms. Offshore production returns within days of a storm passing; a damaged refinery is out for weeks, and refining is where diesel is made. An analyst this week called the oil-market risk “moderate, but rising”, the larger concern being a strong storm near refineries rather than over wells.

On 4 October this brief recorded the NHC giving a southwestern Gulf disturbance a 30% chance of formation over seven days. It formed.

What to watch: the landfall point against the refinery corridor from Pascagoula to Lake Charles; BSEE’s daily shut-in figures, which are the honest measure; and next Wednesday’s EIA weekly print, which is where a refinery outage would reverse seventeen days of falling diesel.

The President suspended the highway tax on farm diesel three days ago. This brief led on diesel prices twice since and did not mention it

Correction — omission

An executive order signed on 5 October suspends enforcement of the federal excise tax on dyed diesel used on public roads through 31 December. This brief ran the pump-price chain as its eighth lead on 7 October and updated the thread on the 6th, and mentioned the order in neither. It was reported by CNN on 5 October and by NPR on the 7th.

The failure is specific and worth naming: on both days this brief read AAA and EIA price series and reasoned about why diesel was falling, without checking whether any policy had changed the price of diesel. A price series tells you what happened; it does not tell you what was done. The rule now recorded: when a thread is about a price, search for policy actions on that price, not only for the price.

Energy & grid US politics & policy Independent corroboration

Dyed diesel is chemically ordinary diesel marked with red dye to show that the federal highway excise tax — 24.4 cents a gallon — has not been paid on it. It is sold for farms, construction, generators and heating, and using it on a public road is an offence carrying a penalty under Internal Revenue Code § 6715.

The order, covering 5 October to 31 December, directs the Treasury Secretary to defer collection of the highway tax on dyed diesel without interest or penalties, and directs the IRS to say it will not impose § 6715 penalties on dyed fuel sold for or used on the highway in that window. It invites state governors to use enforcement discretion and halt inspections.

Three things it does not do. It does not make red diesel legal on highways permanently. It defers rather than forgives the tax — the liability is postponed, and the order asks officials to explore forgiving it, possibly by legislation. And it does not create supply: dyed diesel is not sold at most filling stations, and the delivery infrastructure for it is limited.

On savings the claims diverge sharply. One estimate puts the combined federal-and-state saving at up to 60 cents a gallon; the White House projects over $100 per fill-up for a truck taking 250 gallons — but only where the driver’s state also suspends its own diesel tax, which no state is obliged to do. Against a diesel price $2.61 above a year ago, 60 cents recovers less than a quarter of the increase.

What to watch: whether the IRS has actually published guidance, since sources disagree on whether the order is in effect without it; which states suspend their own tax; and whether the deferred liability is ever forgiven, because a deferral that is later collected is a loan, not a cut.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether the order lowers anybody’s fuel bill. The administration’s case: suspending a 24.4-cent federal tax, plus state taxes where governors cooperate, is up to 60 cents a gallon and over $100 on a truck fill. The sceptical case, which the energy analysts quoted this week make: dyed diesel is not available at most pumps, the distribution network is built for farms and construction rather than truck stops, and a tax exemption on a fuel you cannot buy is worth nothing.

Cause. The disagreement is about physical logistics rather than tax policy, and the logistics argument is the stronger one in the short run. Dyed and undyed diesel are the same product distinguished by a marker added downstream, so the supply exists — but the retail infrastructure does not: truck stops hold undyed fuel in their tanks, and switching a tank over is a commercial decision with a December deadline attached. Note also the asymmetry in who benefits. A fleet with its own bulk tank can order dyed fuel by the tanker-load tomorrow; an owner-operator buying at a truck stop cannot. So the saving, where it is real, accrues to the operators who were already cheapest to serve.

What it means for you. Do not expect this at a retail pump, and treat any advertised saving as conditional on your state. The practical warning is the one nobody is emphasising: the tax is deferred, not cancelled, so a trucker who fills with dyed diesel on the strength of this order has a federal excise liability unless Congress forgives it, and the order itself contemplates that forgiveness as something still to be arranged. If you drive a diesel vehicle and are tempted, the question to ask the seller is not whether the fuel is cheaper but who is recorded as liable for the deferred tax. For everyone else the useful observation is in the chart: diesel has fallen for seventeen days straight, the order lands three days from the end of that run, and nothing in the line marks it.

Confidence: Independent corroboration; the executive order itself was NOT read — it has not appeared in the Federal Register, and this brief has no IRS guidance confirming it is operative. The 24.4-cent rate is this brief’s own. Sources: NPR · CNN · Farm Policy News · AAA Fuel Prices, 8 October · corrects 7 October

DHS wants schools to pay $70,000 for every foreign student who takes a first job — and says it may end the programme otherwise

US politics & policy AI & tech industry New thread Underreported Primary source

Homeland Security has proposed a fee on Optional Practical Training, the programme that lets an F-1 student work in a job related to their degree. The amounts: $70,000 for initial OPT and $30,000 for any subsequent OPT. The school pays, before its designated official enters the recommendation in SEVIS, and must show proof of payment; it may pass the cost to students, employers or both. Refunds apply if no work authorisation issues. General comments close 9 November.

The scale: 194,554 F-1 students reported OPT employment in 2024, up from 160,627 in 2023, of whom 165,524 were on STEM OPT, across about 6,186 SEVP-certified schools. At $70,000 a head the 2024 cohort would have cost roughly $13.6bn — this brief’s arithmetic, and the point: no university can absorb it and no ordinary student can pay it.

DHS says so itself. The justification cites fraud — shell companies, “pay-to-stay” schemes, fake worksites, an investigation identifying more than 10,000 students with “highly suspect” employers — plus arguments that OPT bypasses the H-1B cap and displaces US workers. The fee is set to roughly match H-1B fees, and DHS states that without it it may be unable to run OPT and may end the programme entirely.

The legal footing is INA 214(a), the authority over the time and conditions of admission, read with the D.C. Circuit’s 2022 Washington Alliance of Technology Workers decision — the case that upheld OPT’s existence. Fees go to the Treasury as miscellaneous receipts, not to ICE: a fee that does not fund the programme it charges for is not cost recovery but a price set to change behaviour, which is what DHS says it is.

What to watch: the comment file on 9 November, where the universities and the technology employers will appear; whether any final rule keeps the figure; and the litigation, since a fee at this level invites a challenge that it is a prohibition in the form of a price.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether this is a fee or an ending. DHS frames it as both: a charge that makes schools selective and deters bad actors, and an alternative to terminating a programme it says it cannot otherwise administer. Critics will frame it as termination by arithmetic. The document supports both readings, which is unusual and worth noticing — an agency rarely writes that the alternative to its proposal is abolition.

Cause. The fraud findings are real and specific, and the honest position is that nobody defending OPT should wave away ten thousand students placed with highly suspect employers. But a fee is a strange instrument for that problem. Fraud is concentrated in a small number of schools and employers, and the remedies that match it are SEVP decertification, employer audits and worksite verification — all of which DHS already has. A flat per-student charge falls hardest on the institutions with the most students rather than the most fraud, which means the large public universities and not the storefront operations. Note what the proposal does not claim: there is no estimate in what this brief read of how many of the 194,554 are fraudulent, and no figure for how many students the fee would deter.

What it means for you. If you are an international student, a university administrator, or an employer who hires one, assume the programme is at risk rather than merely dearer, and read the comment deadline as the only remaining input. For everyone else the channel is the technology labour market: OPT is how a large share of foreign STEM graduates of American universities take their first American job, and the H-1B cap is why the alternative route is a lottery. Pricing OPT at H-1B levels does not move those people into H-1Bs — the cap is a quantity limit, not a price — it moves them out of the country, with the degree the United States just taught them. The observation that would tell you how serious the department is about the fraud rationale rather than the displacement one is whether the final rule exempts or discounts schools with clean compliance records. A flat fee says the target is the programme.

The $13.6bn arithmetic is this brief’s own. Sources: DHS, Optional Practical Training Fees, full text

Customs has proposed rebuilding the rules for 103 million small parcels a year, a third of which currently pay no duty at all

World economy US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

Customs and Border Protection has proposed a rewrite of the entry rules for low-value shipments — docket USCBP-2026-0298, comments to 7 December. The context: the $800 de minimis exemption has been suspended since 29 August 2025, a 10% duty was imposed on postal shipments in February, and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act terminates it outright on 1 July 2027. This rule builds the machinery for what comes after.

The volumes are the story. Between 1 September 2025 and 31 May 2026: 24,427,685 postal entries and 52,450,418 informal Type 11 entries, annualising to roughly 103 million shipments. And the finding that explains the rest of the rule: only 67% of postal shipments went through any entry process at all — the other third were, in CBP’s words, likely shipped without duties paid.

What would change. Electronic filing becomes mandatory for Type 11 entries, due on or before the date of importation rather than within fifteen days. A new Entry Type 13 replaces the postal process for mail of $2,500 or less, requiring the sender’s identity and the UPU S-10 tracking number, which carriers must also put on the inward manifest — today they report only weight. Non-owner consignees such as postal operators and forwarders would need a licensed customs broker as importer of record. Every Type 11 and Type 13 entry would need a bond, with $1,000 minimum liquidated damages per breach, and mail not entered within fifteen days is deemed abandoned.

The enforcement case is one comparison: FY2024 narcotics seizure rates were 277.8 per million postal de minimis shipments against 15.8 for Type 11 informal entries and 2.6 for formal. CBP’s projected net cost, 2026–2035, is $9.30bn at a 3% discount rate and $7.72bn at 7% — partial, it says, excluding warehousing and returns of abandoned mail.

What to watch: the 7 December comment file; the Entry Type 13 test that began 22 September; and whether the broker requirement survives, since it would most change who can ship into the United States.

A woman who worked at a Siberian anti-plague laboratory has died of suspected pneumonic plague. Two hundred contacts are under observation

Science & public health Geopolitics Independent corroboration

Darya Shipilova, 28, who worked at an anti-plague laboratory in Russia’s Irkutsk region, has died of a suspected case of pneumonic plague. At least five area hospitals are under quarantine and almost 200 contacts are under medical observation. Russia has dismissed reports of a second case as false; the WHO has urged Moscow to respond formally to its inquiry.

The CDC says it is “aware of and closely monitoring” the case, puts the risk to the United States at “extremely low”, and is working with Homeland Security, State and Defense to monitor travellers from the region; it has a health station at JFK able to screen arrivals from Siberia. Asked whether a bioweapon could be behind the infection, the President said he does not think so, and said the US would help Russia.

The detail that makes this a lab-safety story rather than an outbreak story is the victim’s employment. She worked at a facility whose purpose is studying and containing plague. A laboratory-acquired infection with a select agent is a containment failure, and it is the category this brief has kept as a standing thread since September.

Why pneumonic plague is treated differently from the bubonic form: it is in the lungs, transmits person to person by droplet, and is close to uniformly fatal without antibiotics started within about a day. That is why two hundred contacts are watched rather than treated as bystanders — and why, since ordinary antibiotics work, the CDC’s “extremely low” is credible rather than reassurance.

What to watch: whether Russia responds to the WHO formally, which is the test of whether this is being handled as a notifiable event; whether any contact develops disease, which is the only measure of transmission; and whether any account establishes how the infection occurred.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. How many cases there are, and whether the laboratory is the source. Reports of a second suspected case have been dismissed by Russian officials as false information. The WHO is asking Moscow for a formal response, which means it does not have one. So the factual record outside Russia consists of one death, a quarantine, a contact-tracing figure, and a denial.

Cause. Information about outbreaks inside Russia reaches the outside world through Russian official statements and little else, and a laboratory-acquired infection is institutionally embarrassing in a way that a natural case is not — plague is endemic in Siberian rodent populations, so a natural exposure is entirely plausible and is also the account that requires no admission. That is not a reason to assume concealment; it is a reason to treat a single-source denial as weaker evidence than a single-source report. The bioweapon question, raised and dismissed at presidential level, is the least likely explanation and the one that will travel furthest — an anti-plague institute holds plague because studying plague is its job.

What it means for you. Nothing yet, and the honest advice is to discount this unless a contact falls ill. The reason to carry it anyway is that the observable sequence — a worker at a containment laboratory dies of the agent that laboratory handles, the host government denies a second case, the WHO cannot get an answer — is the sequence that precedes every disputed outbreak, and it is far easier to assess as it happens than in retrospect. The specific thing worth knowing as a traveller is that the CDC screening is at JFK only and applies to arrivals from the Siberian region, which is a narrow filter for a disease with a two-to-four-day incubation. If a contact outside the original cluster is confirmed, that filter stops being adequate and the story changes character.

The Army will shoot Nidal Hasan on 3 December — the first US military firing squad since 1945

US politics & policy Independent corroboration

The Pentagon announced on Monday 5 October that Defense Secretary Hegseth recommended execution by firing squad for Nidal Hasan and that the President approved it. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle has set the execution for 3 December at 2pm Eastern at Fort Hood.

Hasan, an Army major, was convicted by a military jury in July 2013 of 13 counts of premeditated murder and 32 of attempted murder for the 2009 shooting at Fort Hood; a panel of thirteen officers unanimously recommended death. The Army Court of Criminal Appeals upheld the sentence in 2020, the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces in 2023, and the Supreme Court denied review in March 2025.

Be precise about the superlative, because outlets are not. This would be the first military firing squad execution since 1945, and the first US military execution by any method since 1961. Both are true and they are different claims.

The method is the part with forward consequences. Firing squad has the shortest documented time to death and the fewest recorded failures; it is also the one jurisdictions have avoided as visibly violent. The military choosing it five days after Tennessee’s lethal injection of Christa Pike failed in public — today’s next story — is the clearest institutional signal yet about which method officials actually trust.

The execution is not certain. It remains unclear whether Hasan will seek to challenge or delay it in federal court, and a capital case with a fixed date ordinarily generates filings.

What to watch: any federal habeas filing before 3 December; whether the Army publishes its execution protocol, which it has never had occasion to use in the modern era; and whether states cite the military’s choice in their own method litigation.

Confidence: Independent corroboration across six accounts; no Pentagon statement, Army order or protocol was read, and the execution time is reported as 2pm Eastern by some outlets and 1pm Central by others — the same moment. Christa Pike is conscious and speaking. Her lawyers thought she was brain-dead, and a judge has ordered the state to preserve the needles

US politics & policy Science & public health Independent corroboration

Judge I’Ashea Myles of the Davidson County Chancery Court in Nashville ordered Tennessee on 7 October to preserve all evidence from the failed execution of Christa Pike: log books, medical waste, restraints, photographs, audio and video, any 911 calls, the protocols referenced, and every message officials sent or received about the attempt. “Anything in the actual room needs to be preserved.” Where evidence is already lost she directed the Department of Correction to take steps to recover it.

And the fact that changes the case: Pike is conscious and speaking. Her attorneys say they had believed she was brain-dead before she woke, in their words, to the shock of everyone. The judge also granted them “liberal access” to her, requiring prison officials to answer requests for in-person visits within two hours.

The state opposed the order on the ground that existing duties already required preservation. It also argued it may no longer control some of the evidence: the IV lines may have been cut and left inside Pike when she was taken to hospital, and the department cannot preserve what it does not hold.

That exchange is the problem in miniature: the physical record of what went wrong is partly inside the surviving prisoner, in a hospital’s custody, and partly in a death chamber controlled by the agency whose conduct is in question. Pike’s lawyers say they emailed the state at 1:09am on 1 October and had no reply by the time they filed on the 2nd.

Her counsel called the order “just a first step of many”. They are not yet asking for the evidence to be handed over — only that it survive.

What to watch: whether the IV lines are recovered and by whom; whether a second execution attempt is ever sought, which a conscious survivor makes a live constitutional question; the independent review commissioned after the commissioner’s resignation; and whether any of the preserved material becomes public.

Confidence: Independent corroboration across five accounts; the court order itself was not read and outlets differ on its precise scope, as noted. Sources: Washington Post · NPR via HPPR · NewsNation · CNN · Newsweek · continues 4 October and 3 October

Nine exchange filings in one day answer a question nobody asked out loud: what happens when the market halts at 3am

US financials New thread Underreported Primary source

Nine SEC notices published today cover the same subject at seven venues: trading resumption after a Level 3 market-wide circuit-breaker halt, in the context of 23-hour trading. The filings are from Nasdaq Texas (Rule 4121), the Nasdaq Stock Market (4121), Nasdaq PHLX (3101), and Cboe EDGX (11.24(b)(2)), EDGA (11.23), BZX (11.30) and BYX (11.29), with four related amendments to Cboe Rules 13.8(d) and 11.22(g).

Translate the mechanics. Market-wide circuit breakers halt all US equity trading when the S&P 500 falls far enough: Level 1 at 7% and Level 2 at 13% pause trading for fifteen minutes; Level 3 at 20% closes the market for the rest of the day.

“The rest of the day” is a coherent instruction when the day ends at 4pm. It is not coherent when exchanges trade 23 hours. If the S&P falls 20% at 3am, a rule that closes the market until tomorrow has to decide what tomorrow means, when the session restarts, and whether a venue trading overnight may reopen before the primary listing market does.

That is what seven exchanges filed on in one morning, and it is the most consequential plumbing question in US equities: the near-24-hour session is being built, and the rules written for a shorter day are being retrofitted venue by venue, in filings that are individually unreadable and collectively a redesign of when American markets are closed.

This brief has not read the filings and cannot say what resumption standard any adopts, or whether the seven agree — which is the point that matters, since a circuit breaker only works if every venue halts and resumes together.

What to watch: whether the seven venues adopt a common resumption standard or diverge; whether the SEC treats them as one proceeding; and whether the Level 3 threshold itself is revisited, since a 20% move is easier to reach in a thin overnight session than in a liquid one.

Macro & markets The Labor Department has set the 2027 minimum wage for federal contractors — one of the few wage floors that moves without Congress.

The Labor Department published the federal contractor minimum wage rate for 2027. The rate is set annually by the Secretary under executive order and indexed to inflation, and it binds employees working on covered federal contracts regardless of what the state or federal statutory minimum is.

The mechanism is why it is worth a line. The federal statutory minimum wage has been $7.25 since 2009, and Congress has not moved it in seventeen years. The contractor rate is the one federal wage floor that rises on a schedule, because it rests on procurement authority rather than on the Fair Labor Standards Act — the government setting terms as a purchaser rather than as a legislator.

It reaches a real population: construction and services workers on federal contracts, including cleaners, security guards, food service and call-centre staff at federal buildings and installations.

This brief has not read the notice and does not have the 2027 figure or the 2026 comparison, which is the entire content.

Below the line because the document was not read and the number is the story. It leads with the rate and the increase.

World economy USTR has asked American industry what the EU’s carbon border tax is doing to it — the opening move in a trade fight over climate policy.

The US Trade Representative published a request for comments on the effects of the EU’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism. CBAM charges importers into the EU for the carbon embedded in steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, electricity and hydrogen, at a price tied to what EU producers pay under the bloc’s emissions trading system.

A USTR comment request of this kind is how a trade action begins. It builds the record of injury that a later section 301 investigation, a WTO complaint or a retaliation list would rest on, and the questions asked shape what the record can support.

The legal collision is real. The EU says CBAM is not a tariff but an equalisation of a domestic carbon price applied identically to imports and domestic goods; the US has argued that a border charge based on production method is a trade barrier whatever it is called. Both are respectable and the WTO has never resolved one squarely.

For US exporters of steel and aluminium the practical question is whether American carbon costs count as an offset, which depends on a carbon price the United States does not have.

Below the line because the notice was not read, there is no deadline or question list in hand, and a comment request commits nobody. It leads on a section 301 initiation or a retaliation proposal.

Energy & grid Three nuclear licensing notices in one day, including limited work authorisations for small modular reactors at Palisades.

Science & public health CMS has proposed changing who may run a clinical laboratory, and how cervical-cancer screening competence is tested.

CMS published a proposed rule amending the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments of 1988 regime, covering virtual access, gynecologic cytology proficiency testing, personnel qualifications and other changes.

CLIA is the federal law that sets who may perform a clinical test and under what supervision — it governs essentially every laboratory result an American doctor relies on. Personnel qualifications are its most contested provision, because they determine whether a laboratory can be staffed in a rural county at all.

Gynecologic cytology proficiency testing is the narrower and more interesting item: CLIA requires individual cytologists who read cervical-cancer screening slides to pass an annual test, a requirement unique in laboratory medicine and imposed by Congress after reporting in the 1980s on missed cancers at high-volume “Pap mills”. Changing it means revisiting a statutory response to a specific scandal.

“Virtual access” presumably concerns remote supervision or remote slide review, which the pathology workforce shortage makes attractive and which cuts against the premise that supervision is physical.

Florida & Tampa Bay A Camp David-style presidential retreat has been proposed at the President’s own Florida golf club.

The President has proposed a Camp David-style presidential retreat at his private Florida golf club, according to NPR. This brief has not read the piece and has no detail on location, cost, funding mechanism, or whether any federal construction or lease is contemplated.

The distinction that would decide whether this is a story or an anecdote is ownership. Camp David is a naval support facility — government-owned land, operated by the Navy, with the costs of presidential use borne by the government and no private party enriched. A presidential retreat established at a privately owned club owned by the President is a different arrangement, and the questions are the ordinary ones: who owns the land, who pays for improvements, who receives the money, and under what authority.

The precedent would outlast any occupant, which is the reason to track it rather than treat it as a personality item. It is tagged Florida because the state would bear the security, traffic and airspace consequences, as it already does for presidential visits.

US politics & policy The Education Department has finalised changes to Impact Aid, the programme that pays districts for land the federal government does not tax.

A final rule published today amends the Impact Aid programme regulations, effective 9 November.

Impact Aid is one of the oldest federal education programmes and works on a logic nothing else in the department shares: where federal activity removes property from local tax rolls — military installations, Indian trust land, national parks, federal laboratories — the local school district loses the revenue that would otherwise fund its schools, and Impact Aid substitutes for the lost tax base.

That makes it structurally unlike a grant. It is compensation for a taking rather than assistance, which is why it goes to districts with no poverty test and why the districts that depend on it most are those serving military families and tribal lands. For some of them it is a double-digit share of the operating budget.

Changes to the regulations therefore move money between categories of district rather than in or out of the system, and the losers in any reallocation are usually small and rural.

This brief has not read the rule and cannot say what it changes.

US politics & policy The Indian Health Service deleted two sets of obsolete regulations, including the rules for treating people it is not required to treat.

The Indian Health Service published two final rules today, both effective 7 December: removal of an obsolete portion of the regulation on the care and treatment of ineligible individuals, and removal of obsolete or suspended regulations generally.

The first title is the interesting one. IHS provides care to members of federally recognised tribes, and the “ineligible individuals” regulation governed when a facility may treat somebody outside that category — a non-Native spouse, a visitor, an emergency case walking into the only hospital for sixty miles.

Removing an obsolete portion of that rule is housekeeping if the authority now sits elsewhere in statute, and is not housekeeping if it removes the stated basis on which a remote IHS facility treats whoever arrives. The index entry does not distinguish, and this brief did not read either rule.

It is here rather than discarded because IHS facilities are frequently the only hospital in their area, and a rule about whom they may treat is a rule about emergency access for everyone nearby.

US financials The IRS is collecting on the stock buyback excise tax — a levy that raised the cost of the main way US companies return cash.

The IRS published an information collection notice for the excise tax on stock repurchases. The tax, 1% of the value of shares a public company buys back, was created by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 and took effect in 2023.

An information collection notice is paperwork, but it is the visible part of an administrative apparatus that is new. The buyback tax was the first federal levy on a corporate capital-return decision, and its design question — what counts as a repurchase, and what nets against it — is where the money actually is, because large companies issue and retire shares continuously.

It settled nothing about the policy argument: buybacks and dividends are economically similar ways of returning cash, taxed differently, and a 1% charge on one changes the relative price rather than the total returned. Whether that moved any company toward investment is an empirical question nobody has answered. For an index-fund holder it is a small permanent drag on the return of capital.
