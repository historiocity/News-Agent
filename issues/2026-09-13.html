Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s chief executives both called for a slowdown yesterday, and the IPO moved to 2027

AI & tech industry New subject Single-sourced

Yesterday, Saturday, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei published an essay urging companies and governments to “pace the frontier” of AI development. Per NPR’s account, it calls for international cooperation and argues that labs should embed third-party evaluators with standing access — people inside the building who report incidents and track safety practice, rather than auditors who arrive afterwards. Sam Altman posted on X shortly after that he agreed and that OpenAI would follow suit.

Then the part with a number attached. Altman told Fortune, in an interview released the same day, that OpenAI’s initial public offering is delayed to 2027, and gave safety as the reason.

Take the sequence seriously before taking the sincerity question. The two firms that are furthest ahead, and that compete directly, published converging positions within hours of each other on a Saturday. That is coordination or it is contagion, and either one is a change in the industry’s public posture from where it stood a week ago.

What makes this reach beyond a press cycle is the third-party-evaluator proposal, because it is the one piece that could be written into something. California enacted the first statutes in the country requiring independent AI audits on 9 September — SB 813 and AB 1405, both of which Anthropic and OpenAI backed. A voluntary embedded-evaluator norm and a state auditor registry are the same idea arriving from two directions.

What to watch: whether either lab names an evaluator and a scope; whether the essay is cited in the DOJ’s preemption litigation; and Governor Newsom’s 30 September deadline on SB 1000 and SB 947.

Disclosure, as this brief’s standing rule requires: this brief is researched and written using an Anthropic model. Treat everything above about Anthropic with the scepticism you would want applied.
Dig deeper — what “single-sourced” means here, and the two readings of the IPO delay

The sourcing, stated plainly. This item appeared on dozens of public radio station websites yesterday and this morning. That is one NPR story syndicated to member stations, not dozens of newsrooms, and the difference matters: nobody has independently checked it. NPR is describing three primary artefacts — Amodei’s essay, Altman’s post, the Fortune interview — all of which are public and none of which this brief has read directly this morning. So the confidence marker is Single-sourced even though the underlying documents are primary and, being public statements by named executives, are unusually cheap to verify. If any characterisation above is wrong, the likely error is in emphasis rather than in fact.

The IPO delay has two readings, and only one of them is the one being offered. The stated reason is safety. The alternative is ordinary: this is a bad autumn to price a listing. The 10-year Treasury is at the cusp of 5%, the Fed is being priced roughly 90% to hike on Wednesday, and the global long end is at multi-decade highs. Discount rates are what kill growth-equity valuations, and a company whose value sits almost entirely in distant cash flows is the most rate-sensitive thing on any calendar. A delay announced with a safety rationale in that environment costs nothing and buys goodwill.

The honest position is that both can be true and the evidence available today cannot separate them. What would separate them: if the delay survives a rate rally — if the long end falls back and the listing still does not come — the safety rationale gains weight. If it prices in the first friendly window of 2027, the rate reading was the real one. That is a testable prediction and this brief will hold it.

The part that is not rhetoric. “Slow down” from a frontier lab is cheap, because nobody can verify a pace. An embedded third-party evaluator is not cheap, because it creates a person with standing access who can say something the lab did not want said. Anthropic already has one live instance of this: METR holds a signed eight-week investigation with access beyond the incident window of the cyber-evaluation disclosures reported on 11 September. Whether METR’s report is published in full is the concrete test of whether the essay describes a practice or proposes one, and it lands before any legislature acts.

Note also what neither executive is reported to have proposed: a capability threshold, a compute ceiling, a pause on a named class of training run, or a date. Absent one of those, “pace the frontier” is a disposition rather than a commitment, and dispositions are not auditable.

A court created in 1996 and never used has now removed a lawful permanent resident to Afghanistan

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Single-sourced

The Justice Department has, for the first time, brought a case before the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, and used it to deport a lawful permanent resident to Afghanistan. Nazira Haji Zada, 47, entered the United States as a permanent resident in 2018. She conceded that she is an “alien terrorist” within the statute’s meaning, and was removed.

The government alleged she supported a plan by her son and son-in-law to carry out an Islamic State-inspired mass shooting on Election Day 2024; that as the family’s matriarch she worked to radicalise it; that she pledged loyalty to ISIS; and that the family raised money for the attack by selling furniture, computers, a phone and two vehicles. Her son Abdullah Haji Zada, 19, was sentenced to 15 years.

The reason this is a structural story rather than a criminal one is the forum. This court exists to remove people using evidence the person and their lawyer are never shown. Civil-liberties objectors quoted in the reporting called that a plain violation of due process. It sat unused for thirty years, through two decades in which the government had every incentive to use it.

It has now been used once, and the precedent it sets is narrower than the headline implies — see below.

What to watch: whether a second referral follows within weeks, whether any future respondent contests rather than concedes, and whether the court’s classified-evidence machinery is ever actually exercised.

Dig deeper — what the statute does, why it went unused, and why a concession is a thin precedent

The machinery. The court was created by the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 and lives at 8 U.S.C. §§1531–1537. Its judges are federal district judges designated by the Chief Justice. The Attorney General applies to it in camera and ex parte; if the court finds probable cause, a removal hearing follows in which the government may use classified information withheld from the respondent. A lawful permanent resident — unlike other noncitizens — gets a security-cleared special attorney who may see the classified evidence but may not discuss it with the client. That is the design, and it is the reason the constitutional objection is not rhetorical: the person answering the case cannot be told what the case is. This paragraph is background from the statute rather than from yesterday’s reporting, and is flagged as such.

Why thirty years of disuse. The usual explanation among immigration practitioners is not squeamishness. It is that the government has never needed it. Ordinary removal proceedings already allow terrorism-related charges, and prosecutors have generally preferred either a criminal indictment or a garden-variety immigration charge that avoids litigating a novel and vulnerable statute. Using a never-tested procedure invites the first appellate ruling on it, and a first ruling can take the tool away entirely. That calculation is the thing that changed here, and nothing in the available reporting explains why.

Why the precedent is thinner than it looks. She conceded. A concession means the classified-evidence provisions were never exercised, never contested, and therefore never reviewed. No court has held that the special-attorney arrangement satisfies due process, because no court was asked. What the government has actually established is that the forum can be opened and produce a removal — an operational proof, not a legal one. The constitutional question is exactly as open this morning as it was on Friday.

That cuts both ways, and the second way is the one worth holding onto. An untested statute with one successful use is more attractive to a prosecutor than an untested statute with none. The marginal cost of the second referral is lower than the first was. If a second case appears soon and the respondent contests it, that is when this becomes a constitutional story rather than an administrative one.

What would change the reading. A published opinion from the court, an appeal to the relevant circuit, or a congressional oversight letter. Any of the three would put the special-attorney procedure in front of someone empowered to strike it down.

Sources: NPR, 12 September; statutory background from 8 U.S.C. §§1531–1537 (AEDPA 1996)

Half of Venezuela’s oil output is going to American refineries, on contracts written under US law

Macro & markets Energy & grid New subject Underreported Independent corroboration

While this brief has spent a week on two blocked chokepoints, roughly 500,000 barrels a day of Venezuelan crude has been arriving at US refineries — about half of the country’s output, according to Under Secretary of Energy Kyle Haustveit, speaking in Houston last month. Venezuelan production is back to about 1.25m b/d, from 1.117m in July.

The legal plumbing appeared in Friday’s Federal Register, which codified four Venezuela general licences. The licences themselves are not new — they were issued in February and March — but their terms are worth reading now that the volumes are real. General Licence 50A authorises oil-and-gas operations by six named energy companies and their subsidiaries on two conditions: any contract with the Venezuelan government or PdVSA must specify that US law governs it and that disputes are resolved in the United States, and any payment to a blocked person must go into a Treasury deposit account rather than to the recipient.

That is not sanctions relief in the ordinary sense. It is a licensing regime that routes a foreign state’s oil revenue through American courts and American accounts.

The US channel is the one this reader has been paying since August. Venezuelan crude is heavy and sour, which is precisely what Gulf Coast refineries are configured to run and precisely what makes diesel. Distillate is where the shortage is.

What to watch: monthly EIA import data for Venezuelan crude; whether the diesel crack narrows as those barrels land; and any move to add a seventh company to GL 50.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a dispute about the tonnage — the ~500,000 b/d figure and the ~1.25m b/d output figure come from the same US official and are not contradicted anywhere this morning. The dispute is over what happened in January, and it is a dispute about characterisation, which then drives a dispute about significance. American accounts say US forces “captured” Nicolás Maduro on 3 January and that he was arraigned in New York on narco-terrorism and cocaine-importation charges. Al Jazeera’s formulation is that Washington “abducted” him and “installed an interim government”. Same events, two words, and the word chosen decides whether the oil arrangement reads as post-conflict reconstruction or as the proceeds of a seizure.

Cause. Vantage, mostly, and it is the mundane explanation rather than the motivated one. A US outlet reports an indictment in a US court as a legal fact, because it is one. A Gulf-based outlet writing for readers across the region reports the removal of a head of state by a foreign military as the operative fact, because for that audience it is. Both are describing something real and each is omitting what the other centres. Note also the timing asymmetry: the “capture” framing was set in January when the arraignment was the news, and the “installed” framing is being applied now, eight months on, when the durability of the interim government is what is actually in question.

What it means for you. Neither framing changes a barrel, and for a reader whose exposure is the pump price, the framing is close to irrelevant — the crude arrives either way. Where it stops being irrelevant is legal risk. If the interim government is later held to have lacked capacity to contract, the US-law-and-US-forum clause in GL 50A becomes the thing that decides who owns the loss, and it was plainly drafted by someone who had that thought first. Read the choice of law as the tell: Washington priced this risk before the volumes moved. The observation that would settle the durability question is whether any of the six licensed companies commits capital to a multi-year project, rather than merely lifting cargoes.

Sources: Al Jazeera, 19 August · Federal Register, 11 September · OFAC Venezuela-related sanctions · Morgan Lewis and Hogan Lovells on the licence terms · EIA imports series

Iran and the Gulf states sit down in Oman tomorrow, with Saudi Arabia’s only bypass of Hormuz shut

Geopolitics Macro & markets Independent corroboration

Tomorrow in Salalah, southern Oman, foreign ministers from the Gulf Cooperation Council are due to meet Iran — the first such gathering since the war began more than six months ago. Iran has widened the guest list to include Iraq. The subject is safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz, which Tehran has kept disrupted since the US–Israeli strikes began at the end of February.

They meet with the alternative route to Hormuz out of service. Saudi Arabia shut the East–West pipeline — the 1,200km line to Yanbu that had been carrying roughly 5m b/d of rerouted crude — after drone strikes launched from Maysan province in Iraq. Iraq is now both the origin of the strikes and a participant in tomorrow’s talks.

This morning, roughly 3am Eastern, an Iranian container ship was hit by an unidentified projectile near Hengam Island, inside the strait. One crew member was killed and four injured. Nobody has claimed it.

President Trump spoke to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman on Friday and said afterwards it would “work out fine and dandy”, declining to say what was discussed.

The named trigger this brief has been waiting on — published coordinates for Iran’s restricted zone — has still not fired.

What to watch: whether Salalah produces a text or a photograph; the coordinates; and Tuesday’s EIA weekly distillate print.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. On Friday Brent settled down 2.8% at $104.61 and WTI down 2.4% at $100.05, on a day when Saudi Arabia’s only Hormuz bypass was shut by drones and the Houthis consolidated on Perim Island. The physical picture got materially worse and the price fell. This is a dispute about significance, not about facts: the wire coverage attributes the fall to the Oman diplomacy headline, and the physical-market reporting describes a supply position that deteriorated the same day. Both descriptions are accurate.

Cause. The two are pricing different things. The futures tape prices the distribution of outcomes, and the first Iran–GCC meeting in six months genuinely shifts probability mass towards a reopening — that is worth several dollars even if nothing else changed. Physical traders price barrels that must move this week, and for them the pipeline outage is unambiguously worse. Timing matters here too: the Oman headline landed during the session and the pipeline confirmation had largely been absorbed the day before, so the tape was reacting to the newer of the two facts, not weighing them. Note also that Brent still finished the week up 8.7%, which is the number that describes the week rather than the day.

What it means for you. Do not read Friday’s 2.8% as relief, and in particular do not read it into the pump. Diesel set a record on Thursday on a day crude fell 3%, because US diesel is now set by refining capacity and distillate inventory rather than by the headline barrel. A crude rally that fails on a Salalah communiqué would still leave the diesel crack where it is. The observation that would settle which market is right: transit counts through Hormuz in the week after the meeting. Barrels moving, not ministers meeting, is the thing that changes a price you pay.

The AI coding agents write a file inside the sandbox, and something outside the sandbox runs it

AI & tech industry New subject Independent corroboration

Security researchers have documented a class of vulnerability affecting most of the widely used AI coding agents — Claude Code, OpenAI’s Codex CLI, Cursor, Gemini CLI and others. The mechanism is the same each time and it is more interesting than a normal bug.

The agent runs in a sandbox. It cannot escape the sandbox. So it does not try: it writes an ordinary configuration file inside the sandbox, and some trusted program on the host — the editor, a shell, git — reads that file later and executes what it says. Cymulate named the pattern Configuration-Based Sandbox Escape. One variant abuses git’s legitimate core.fsmonitor setting, which tells git to run a named program; a malicious .git config in a cloned repository is therefore enough. That variant reaches Codex, Claude Code, Cursor, goose, Qwen Code and others.

In Cursor, a hooks configuration file could execute commands outside the sandbox — CVE-2026-48124, fixed in version 3.0.0. Two further Cursor issues involved a Python virtual environment the editor auto-executes and alternative git metadata that defeated its path-based checks.

The disclosure timeline is the part with teeth. The startup Accomplish reported findings to Anthropic, Cursor and OpenAI; one fix took 50 days.

What to watch: whether any vendor moves from patching instances to changing the trust model, and whether an enterprise breach gets attributed to this class.

Disclosure: Claude Code is named in this story and this brief is written using an Anthropic model. Applied where it is uncomfortable: a 50-day fix on a reported sandbox issue is slow, and the trust-boundary design that produced this class of bug is the vendors’ own.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A narrow, real disagreement about whether these are “sandbox escapes” at all. BleepingComputer and the CVE record treat them as escapes. Techzine is explicit the other way: the attacks “do not directly breach the sandbox” — they exploit a file the agent legitimately wrote, which a trusted host program then executes. This is a dispute about characterisation, and both descriptions of the mechanism are identical. The disagreement is over the label.

Cause. Not interest, and not access — everyone is reading the same Cymulate write-up and the same CVE. It is vantage. From an attacker’s desk, the result is code running on the host, so it is an escape and the label is honest. From a vendor’s or a sandbox-designer’s desk, the containment boundary held exactly as specified and the failure is in what the host does with files afterwards, so calling it an escape misdirects the fix. Both are defending a genuine engineering distinction rather than a position.

What it means for you. The vendor reading is technically correct and operationally the more dangerous one, so adopt the attacker’s label and the vendor’s diagnosis. If containment is not the broken part, then hardening the sandbox will not fix this — and hardening the sandbox is what the patches so far have largely done. The real defect is a trust handoff: an artefact written by a semi-trusted process is later read by a fully trusted one with no revalidation. That is a category, not a bug, so expect more instances. The practical consequence today: cloning an untrusted repository and pointing an agent at it is an execution risk, not a reading risk. The observation that would settle it is whether the next round of fixes touches the handoff — validating agent-written config before the host acts on it — or just closes the specific files named above.

Sources: Cymulate on Configuration-Based Sandbox Escape · BleepingComputer · Techzine · The Hacker News on core.fsmonitor · Upstarts Media on the Accomplish disclosures

The data-centre housing number everyone is about to quote is not a data-centre effect

Energy & grid AI & tech industry New subject Primary source

The National Association of Realtors published its 2026 Data Center Impact Report this week, and it contains a number that is going to be misused: median home value in US counties with more than ten data centres is $431,750, against $174,500 in counties with none.

That is a ratio of nearly two and a half to one, and the report says plainly that data centres did not cause it. The counties with the most data centres were already richer, younger and better educated before the buildout — median household income around $89,000 against $64,000 in counties with none. Data centres are sited where the fibre, the power and the educated workforce already are. The house prices and the data centres have a common cause.

The concentration is extreme. 92% of US counties have no mapped data centre at all, and 1% have ten or more. Loudoun and Prince William counties in northern Virginia alone account for roughly 19% of every mapped data centre in the country.

NAR’s principal economist, Nadia Evangelou, put the finding as: they are not “one category”. The report’s actual conclusion is that there is no single data-centre effect on housing — which is a harder thing to say in a headline than a number is.

What to watch: whether this figure turns up in a state legislature’s data-centre siting debate stripped of the caveat.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A dispute about facts, and therefore adjudicable. Commercial Observer’s headline on the same report reads that ten counties hold 42% of US data centres and “home prices rise faster there” — a causal claim about rate of change. NPR’s treatment, and the report itself, say there is no single effect and that the level difference is confounded. These cannot both be the report’s finding.

Cause. Almost certainly the ordinary one rather than the motivated one: a level difference and a growth difference are different statistics, and a report that contains both is easy to compress wrongly under a headline. It is also worth naming whose report this is. NAR represents realtors, whose members benefit from rising home values; a finding that data centres do not straightforwardly raise prices cuts against the simplest version of that interest, which is a mild reason to credit the caveat rather than doubt it.

What it means for you. Believe the confounding, and treat any “data centres raise home values” claim built on the $431,750 figure as unsupported — the report you would be citing says so itself. The open question the report does not settle is the one that actually matters to a homeowner near a proposed site: what happens to prices within a county after a data centre is built, at the neighbourhood level, controlling for what was already there. County medians cannot answer that at any level of care. Until someone runs it at parcel level with a pre-trend, the honest answer for a Pasco or Hillsborough homeowner reading a siting notice is that nobody knows, and the two-and-a-half-to-one figure is not evidence either way.

Sources: NAR 2026 Data Center Impact Report · NPR · Commercial Observer

Florida & Tampa Bay: the window to object to the school vaccine rule closes tomorrow

Florida & Tampa Bay Science & public health Independent corroboration

Comments and requests for a public hearing on Florida’s proposed rewrite of rule 64D-3.046 are due tomorrow, Monday 14 September. The proposal was published on 24 August.

The rule drops hepatitis B, varicella, Hib and pneumococcal vaccination from the requirements for school entry. Measles, polio, diphtheria, pertussis, mumps and tetanus are required by statute rather than by rule and survive it — but as this brief has said before, the widened exemption language in the same rewrite applies to those too, which is why both framings you will see are correct.

The Florida Chapter of the American College of Physicians has formally requested a rule hearing. A timely request inside the statutory window obliges the agency to hold one, so the practical question tomorrow is not whether objections exist but whether the request is honoured with a date.

One caution, repeated because it keeps surfacing in search results: the 12 December Panama City hearing date traces to a November 2025 notice and is not a granted 2026 hearing. Do not rely on it.

What to watch: whether a hearing is granted with a date, adoption after the window closes, or a challenge at the Division of Administrative Hearings.

US financials The doubled bank examination threshold appears in tomorrow’s Federal Register as an interim final rule, which is the document behind Saturday’s story rather than a new development.

Geopolitics The Iran aviation wind-down published in the same issue expires on 23 September, and that one is a real clock.

US debt & fiscal Treasury proposed information-reporting rules for Opportunity Zones on Friday, which is the first sign the permanent regime is being built rather than debated.

The IRS published proposed regulations on 11 September implementing statutory information-return and investor-statement requirements for qualified opportunity funds, together with updated certification and decertification procedures.

The significance is procedural and therefore easy to miss. Opportunity Zones as originally enacted had famously thin reporting, which made it impossible to evaluate whether the deferral was buying anything. Building the reporting rules first, before the next round of designations bites, is the difference between a programme that can be audited and one that cannot.

The fiscal channel for this reader is indirect but real: OZ deferrals are a revenue cost carried on the same balance sheet as the net interest line, and a programme with reporting is one whose cost can eventually be scored against its effect.

Below the line because it is a proposed rule about forms, with no comment deadline yet reported and no dollar figure attached. It would move up if the reporting requirements draw organised opposition from fund sponsors, which is the usual signal that they bite.
