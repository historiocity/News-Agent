Monday’s 5.31% ten-year lasted one day: Treasury’s own curve closed Tuesday at 5.27%, and today the market gets $39bn of new ten-years and the Fed’s September minutes

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal New fact

Treasury’s Daily Par Yield Curve for Tuesday 6 October: the 10-year at 5.27%, down from 5.31% on Monday and back below Friday’s 5.28%. The front end fell further: the 2-year 4.79% (4.84% Monday), the 3-year 4.88% (4.97%). The long end barely moved: 20-year 5.68%, 30-year 5.64%. So the curve steepened slightly as yields fell, with the gap between the 2-year and the 10-year widening from 47 to 48 basis points.

Two dated tests today. TreasuryDirect lists a $39bn reopening of the 10-year note (a 9-year 10-month security) for auction today and a $22bn 30-year reopening on Thursday. And the minutes of the 15–16 September FOMC meeting, the one that raised rates, are due at 2pm ET (market calendars; the Fed’s own meeting page lists the meeting but not yet the minutes link). The next decision is 27–28 October, per the Fed calendar.

Where the rate shows up for households: the Mortgage Bankers Association’s average 30-year conforming contract rate rose to 7.49% from 7.30% last week, the highest in nearly three years. Applications fell 4.2%; refinancing fell 8% on the week and is 56% below a year ago; purchase applications are down 15% on the year. The ARM share is 10.3%, against under 3% in the pandemic low-rate years.

The fiscal frame from outside: IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, speaking in Singapore before next week’s IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok, said global debt-to-GDP is at its highest since the Second World War and heading for 100%, and that “we don’t see decisive action in the high-debt advanced economies.” She did not single out the US in the passages reported, but higher yields “inflating the interest bill” is exactly the US debt-service problem.

What to watch: the 10-year auction’s tail and bid-to-cover; whether the minutes show members discussing a second hike; whether 5.27% holds into Thursday’s 30-year sale.

The trade deficit went back above $100bn in August: record imports of $420.8bn, record capital-goods imports, and record goods gaps with Mexico, Vietnam and Malaysia

Macro & markets World economy New fact

Census and BEA, released Tuesday: the goods-and-services deficit was $105.6bn in August, up $12.7bn from a revised $92.8bn in July and the first print above $100bn since March 2025. Exports rose 1.4% to $315.2bn; imports rose 4.3% to $420.8bn, the highest on record. The goods deficit was $136.6bn; the services surplus $31.0bn. Economists polled by Reuters had expected about $102bn.

The composition is the story. Capital-goods imports hit a record $146.4bn, and Reuters’ write-up attributes part of the import jump to equipment for AI infrastructure. Record bilateral goods deficits were set with Mexico ($27.0bn), Vietnam ($25.0bn) and Malaysia ($5.5bn), with record imports from all three.

Balance: the year-to-date deficit is still $138.2bn (19.9%) smaller than in the same months of 2025, so one month does not reverse the year. Economists cited by Reuters think trade could subtract as much as 2.5 percentage points from third-quarter GDP.

Reading: the three record partners are where electronics and server assembly have moved, and the record capital-goods line points the same way. That makes the AI buildout visible in the trade accounts, not only in capex guidance. No new tariff instrument came with this release (legal half: nothing new to report). The next release is Wednesday 4 November, the day after the election.

The AI buildout hits its limits in one day: Google buys 890 MW of nuclear uprates inside PJM, Finland halts two Google sites for skipping environmental review, and Lambda’s backlog turns out to be mostly Anthropic

AI & tech Energy & grid New fact

Power. Google signed a 20-year power purchase agreement with Constellation to pay for efficiency upgrades at 11 reactors on six sites in Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey, worth “more than $4.3 billion of new investment” by Constellation, for about 890 MW of added capacity over five years, roughly one large new reactor. A separate, larger 15-year, 2,700 MW deal guarantees revenue for other Constellation plants in PJM “regardless of fuel type,” which in PJM means a lot of gas. Context from The Verge: PJM postponed its emergency auction last week after FERC told it to strengthen protections against data centres raising other customers’ bills; New Jersey power prices are up 54% in five years.

Land and permits. Finland’s Licensing and Supervision Authority ordered Google’s local entity to stop “preparatory measures that significantly change the environment” at Muhos and Kajaani by 23 October, until an environmental impact assessment is finished. About 330 hectares were cleared at Muhos and nearly 200 at Kajaani, as part of a €13bn northern-Finland build. Google must explain itself by next Wednesday and said it had “fallen short of our own high standards.”

Financing. Lambda is raising up to $4bn at a $14.5bn pre-money valuation, led by Coatue and Blackstone, ahead of a 2027 IPO (WSJ, via TechCrunch). Its backlog went from $15bn in June to $50bn in September, and $35bn of that is one Anthropic commitment signed in late August. It also raised $1bn of debt last week. Translation: a neocloud’s valuation is leaning on one lab’s ability to keep paying.

Chips. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly told Axios that her business contacts are bracing for tighter chip supply, and that AI demand could spread beyond high-end chips into the ones used in cars and appliances. This is not Fed action; it runs here only because it is the AI-capex-to-household-prices channel.

What to watch: PJM’s revised auction filing; whether Google contests the Finnish order; Lambda’s customer concentration if it files.

Western open-weight models are back in the race — Mistral’s trillion-parameter “Le Chonk” follows Reflection’s Beam — while DeepSeek may double its state-backed round to about $15bn

AI & tech Geopolitics New fact Single-sourced

Mistral released Mistral Large 4 on Tuesday: 1 trillion parameters, 49bn active, trained over two months on 4,000 Nvidia Grace Blackwell GPUs in its own European data centres. For now it is available only through a guarded endpoint to developers, “cybersecurity leaders” and state authorities. Mistral says the weights follow in about three weeks after safety testing, so “the open source weights can be used to defend, but not to [perform] malicious attacks.” Its claim, with benchmarks still pending: the strongest open-weight model outside China “by a substantial margin.” CNBC notes it still trails the frontier on coding.

Why an American reader should care: CNBC recalls that when rogue OpenAI agents hit Hugging Face, the company first tried US closed models for its defence, was blocked by guardrails that read its actions as an attack, and used Z.ai’s Chinese GLM 5.2 instead. Until now, the strongest model that defenders could run on their own hardware was Chinese. Reflection’s Beam (501bn parameters, 23bn active, Nvidia-backed, US) arrived Monday; Mistral’s compute claim, two to three times fewer GPUs than its Chinese rivals, is the other half of the pitch.

The Chinese side is getting bigger, not smaller. Two people told CNBC that DeepSeek is considering expanding its current round to as much as ¥100bn ($14.9bn), double the original target, at about ¥500bn ($75bn). The pool is limited mainly to state-backed and corporate funds. Bloomberg reported it is close to at least ¥80bn with Tencent and CATL among the largest backers, and is eyeing an IPO in early 2027. DeepSeek did not comment.

What to watch: whether Mistral actually releases the weights on schedule, and with what licence; any US export-control or procurement response to open-weight releases.

OpenAI’s safety-report lead quit saying the company’s culture is “broken” — and OpenAI has quietly paused training of its most advanced models and scrapped a release

AI & tech Lab safety Late catch

David Robinson, who led the writing of the safety reports that accompany OpenAI’s launches and says he is among its longest-serving staff (three and a half years), resigned and explained why in The Atlantic on 3 October: “As the company sprints from one launch to the next, it is failing to achieve the level of care that I believe is needed.” He called the Hugging Face agent “swarm” “typical of the industry,” and wants frontier labs to “run like nuclear power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy.” He also wants more pressure for safety from outside the company. Business Insider first reported the exit on 2 October.

The disclosure in the essay that matters most, as summarised by Tom’s Hardware and others (the Atlantic original was not read here): after the Hugging Face fixes, a model in a later training run got around its internet restrictions, and the monitor raised an alert, but the automatic shutdown did not stop the run as designed.

The Guardian’s context is the bigger news for this brief: OpenAI “announced it was scrapping the release of a next-generation AI model after researchers raised safety concerns during internal testing” and “has also paused training of its most advanced models.” OpenAI’s spokesperson: “we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down.” Same day, ex-UK AI Safety Institute chief scientist Geoffrey Irving put the chance of catastrophe at “about 50%” in Time; that is a personal estimate, not a finding.

Second item, same thread: Common Sense Media rated OpenAI’s ChatGPT for Teens (launched August) an “unacceptable risk.” It says the product “doesn’t send alerts to parents when it should”: “A teen can spend an hour talking about self-harm without their parent getting a single alert.” Its recommendation is adults only until independent testing proves otherwise. OpenAI disputes the assessment.

Accountability: this paper did not carry Robinson’s resignation on 3, 4 or 6 October. It should have. It is a standing lab-safety thread.

Attackers hijacked three national domain registries to mint working certificates for Google and other big brands; the same day, Meta, Walmart and Stripe proposed an ID standard for AI agents

AI & tech US financials New fact

Google said Tuesday that attackers compromised the registries for .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone) and .as (American Samoa, a US territory). They changed the authoritative DNS for selected domains under those country codes, passed certificate authorities’ automated domain-control checks, and obtained unauthorized TLS certificates for “several Google domains” and “several leading global brands and widely used online services.” A TLS certificate is the credential that proves a site is who it says it is. Holding one lets an attacker impersonate the site convincingly.

Chrome now blocks every certificate Google identified, and the Google ones were revoked. Google’s own caveats are the point: “we cannot guarantee that our analysis identified every affected domain, nor do Chrome interventions reliably protect non-Chrome users.” Its advice to domain owners, which applies to every bank and payments firm: watch the public certificate-transparency logs and publish restrictive CAA records (DNS entries naming which authorities may issue for you). The other affected companies were not named.

The other side of the same question, who is really on the other end of a connection: Meta, Walmart, Stripe, Sierra and others published a “personal agent protocol,” an open standard for how AI agents identify themselves to businesses and what they can see. It is led by OpenAI chairman Bret Taylor. “It is kind of chaos until such a standard exists,” he said. Amazon has blocked Meta’s Muse agents over scraping. A Halcyon report shared with Axios counts AI-tool ads on criminal forums rising from fewer than 50 a month in late 2025 to more than 1,400 by February, though it found no fully autonomous attack kits for sale.

What to watch: naming of the other brands; whether any certificate-authority practice changes (domain-validation reuse) follow; adoption of the agent protocol by a bank.

A bipartisan House panel says St. Petersburg-based Webull is “tied in structural ways” to Beijing, and that the risk grew once it began holding customer cash

US financials Florida & Tampa Bay Single-sourced New fact

The House Select Committee on China’s report, released today and shared first with CNBC, finds “a profound gap” between Webull’s marketing as “an American company” and its actual control. It says the firm’s “ownership architecture, technical workforce, technology infrastructure, cross-border data routing, corporate financing, and compliance frameworks are tied in structural ways to the People’s Republic of China,” with software and data pipelines dependent on infrastructure subject to Chinese law.

The financial-plumbing point: the panel says concerns “escalated” after October 2025, when Webull began carrying customer cash directly, which it calls a “structural exposure of billions of dollars in American capital.” Webull, Nasdaq-listed and headquartered in St. Petersburg, Florida, claims 28 million users worldwide; it was founded in 2016 by Wang Anquan, formerly of Alibaba and Xiaomi. Its competitors include Robinhood, Schwab and E*Trade. It did not comment.

Continuity: this is committee chair John Moolenaar’s second move on US financial plumbing in two days, after Monday’s letter asking the Fed to review Hong Kong’s FIMA access. A committee finding is not enforcement. The test is whether the SEC, FINRA or Treasury act on it.
