Iran will reopen Hormuz in seven days for seven conditions — from a memorandum nobody has read. Meanwhile Iraq moved two million barrels through it

Geopolitics Energy & grid Independent corroboration

Iran’s parliament speaker Mohammad Baqer Ghalibaf said on Sunday that the strait “will not be opened” until Iran’s seven conditions, based on the Islamabad memorandum of understanding, are realised. Foreign minister Abbas Araghchi has put a clock on the other side of it — the strait could reopen within seven days if the conditions are accepted: “The choice now is with the US.”

The Islamabad memorandum is dated 17 June, is described as a framework for talks on a durable peace reached with Pakistani and Qatari mediation, and has not been published. Qatar has been carrying messages between Araghchi and the US envoys Witkoff and Kushner, who met in New York in September; Tehran says Washington’s reply came back through the Qataris, and no reachable account says what it was.

Nor is there a published list of the seven. Two accounts each give part of one, and together they name seven distinct demands — this enumeration is assembled by this brief, not quoted from a document: end the fighting on all fronts; return Iran’s frozen funds; lift the US naval blockade of Iranian ports; exempt Iranian oil exports from sanctions; stop interfering in Iran’s internal affairs; stop cooperating with Iran’s adversaries; halt all attacks on Iranian territory. Items one and seven may be one condition counted twice, in which case something else is missing.

Then the fact that cuts across all of it. Iraq’s state Oil Tankers Company reported moving two million barrels of crude through the strait on Saturday. “Closed” is a claim about permission, not about water: the strait is a licensing regime with a shooting enforcement mechanism, and cargoes are moving through it. At the pump the easing continues — AAA diesel $6.3434, a thirteenth consecutive daily fall and 12.75 cents below a week ago; petrol $4.3697, down 11.01.

What to watch: publication of the memorandum or of a numbered list by either government; any US answer that is not relayed through Qatar; and whether transits rise, which is the only measure of whether the closure is a regime or a deterrent.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether the strait is closed. Iran’s parliament speaker says it will not be opened; Iraq’s state tanker company says it moved two million barrels through it the day before. Both are first-person claims by parties in a position to know their own business, and they are not actually contradictory once you see that they describe different things — a declared permission regime, and the cargoes that regime has licensed or failed to stop. The dispute is about what the word means, and it matters because the word is doing the work in every market report.

Cause. Each party is describing the quantity that serves it. Iran is asserting authority over a waterway whose legal status it disputes — it signed but never ratified UNCLOS and rejects the transit-passage regime — so a closure it can lift on conditions is worth more to Tehran than a blockade it merely enforces, because the first establishes a right and the second is only a fact. Iraq, which has to sell oil, is reporting throughput. The US, which maintains its own naval blockade of Iranian ports, has an interest in neither party’s framing and has said nothing reachable. Note what nobody has produced in twenty-six days: the coordinates of the restricted zone. A closure with no published boundary cannot be complied with or tested, which is itself informative about its purpose.

What it means for you. Read “closed” as a price on passage rather than an absence of passage, and watch volumes rather than declarations — the pump price is the honest instrument here and it has fallen thirteen days running, which is not what a closing strait does. The thing that should worry a reader is not the closure but the conditions, because four of the seven named above (frozen funds, sanctions exemptions for oil exports, lifting the naval blockade, ending cooperation with adversaries) are permanent changes to US policy being traded for a temporary restoration of shipping. That is an exchange of durable concessions for a reversible one, and it is the structure of the deal rather than its contents that determines whether it can be accepted. The single observation that would clarify everything is publication of the 17 June memorandum: a war is being prolonged over a text that neither government will show, and both sides’ claims about what it requires are currently unfalsifiable.

The Supreme Court’s term opens tomorrow, and the first case asks whether a Colorado county can sue oil companies over the climate

US politics & policy New thread Independent corroboration

The October 2026 term opens Monday 5 October, the first Monday in October. The justices will hear seven cases over five days in the October sitting, which runs 5–14 October. First up, Monday, is Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) v. County Commissioners of Boulder County: whether federal law forecloses state tort claims over interstate and international greenhouse gas emissions — in plainer terms, whether a Colorado county may pursue oil and gas companies for millions over their alleged role in worsening climate change.

That is the question behind roughly two dozen similar suits by cities, counties and states. The mechanism matters more than the subject: if federal law preempts the field, every one of those cases ends regardless of merit and the only route left is legislation. If not, discovery proceeds in state courts nationwide. A preemption ruling disposes of litigation wholesale, which is why this case rather than any verdict is the one to watch.

Also on the term’s granted docket, with dates not established here: Johnson v. United States Congress, on whether the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act stripped federal district courts of jurisdiction over constitutional challenges to veterans’ benefits laws; a Second Amendment challenge to bans on AR-15-style rifles; whether Colorado may apply its non-discrimination requirements to religious schools; and whether the National Voter Registration Act or a federal consent decree displaces state documentary proof-of-citizenship requirements for voter registration. The ICE mandatory-detention case this brief reported on 2 October is also on the docket.

Four of those five touch machinery rather than policy — who may sue, in which court, under whose law.

What to watch: Monday’s argument in Suncor, and specifically whether the questioning is about preemption doctrine or about the climate claims themselves; the argument calendar for November and December; and whether any emergency application displaces the scheduled work, as happened repeatedly last term.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a factual dispute — the calendar is published — but a real disagreement about what kind of term this is. One framing, and the one this brief took from the headline it could not read, is that the term is “jam-packed with controversy”: guns, religion, voting, immigration. The other, visible in the preview material and in the calendar itself, is that the October sitting is unusually technical, led by a preemption question and a jurisdictional question about veterans’ benefits. Both describe the same docket.

Cause. The two framings count different things. A term’s controversy is usually measured by its granted cases across the whole year; its first sitting is assembled from whatever was granted earliest and is fully briefed, which skews technical. There is also a sourcing asymmetry worth naming: the NPR piece that prompted this story returned HTTP 503 on two attempts, so the “controversy” framing is known here only from its own headline, while the technical framing comes from material this brief actually read. That is not a reason to prefer one — it is a reason to tell you which is which.

What it means for you. Expect the consequential rulings to arrive in June and the arguments that signal them to happen now, and read the October sitting for doctrine rather than for drama. The practically important point for an ordinary reader is that Suncor is not really about climate change: it is about whether state courts can be used to litigate harms with national and international causes, and the answer will govern opioid-style mass litigation over anything diffuse — emissions, microplastics, forever chemicals. If you want one thing to listen for in Monday’s argument, it is whether the justices treat the Clean Air Act as occupying the field by itself or look for an express preemption clause, because the first reasoning reaches much further than this case.

The clocks go back on 1 November unless the Senate moves, and the bill would make the mornings darker, not the evenings lighter

US politics & policy New thread Independent corroboration

The President spent Saturday pressing Senate Republicans to pass the Sunshine Protection Act — which he prefers to call the “No More Changing of Clocks Act” — and named Senator Tom Cotton as the man holding it up, publishing Cotton’s mobile number and urging people to call it. The House passed the bill in July, 308 to 117. Majority leader John Thune has questioned whether it can reach the 60 votes it needs.

The substance is in a distinction almost every account blurs. The bill makes daylight saving time permanent, not standard time. Clocks would stay an hour ahead through the winter rather than falling back — which moves both sunrise and sunset an hour later by the clock. Evenings get lighter in December; mornings get darker, and that is not a side effect but the same fact stated twice.

That is precisely Cotton’s objection, and it is a substantive one rather than an obstruction: keeping the clock advanced through winter means more schoolchildren and workers travelling before dawn. The alternative reform — permanent standard time, which is what sleep researchers and most medical bodies have favoured — would end the switching without darkening the mornings, and is not what is before the Senate.

The deadline is close: clocks fall back on Sunday 1 November 2026, and absent Senate action in the next four weeks they fall back whatever happens later.

Note what the bill does not do: states may already opt out and sit on permanent standard time, as Arizona and Hawaii do. What federal law forbids is a state choosing permanent daylight time — so the Act is a national answer to a question states are currently only half-allowed to settle.

What to watch: whether Thune brings it to the floor before 1 November; whether a permanent-standard-time amendment appears, which would reframe the whole fight; and the vote count against the House’s 308–117, which suggests the issue is not partisan so much as geographic.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether the bill delivers what its supporters say. “Stop changing the clocks” is a position almost everyone agrees with; “permanent daylight saving time” is one of two opposite ways to do it, and the coverage treats them as the same thing. The accounts also diverge on feasibility: the President describes a bill being blocked by one senator, while the majority leader describes a bill that lacks sixty votes. Those are different problems with different remedies, and only one of them can be solved by phoning Tom Cotton.

Cause. The conflation is partly linguistic — “daylight saving” sounds like more daylight, when it only relocates it — and partly that the two camps are not actually arguing about the same hour. Evening-light advocates (retail, golf, recreation industries, and most people’s stated preference in polls) win under permanent DST. Morning-light advocates (school districts, farm states, sleep medicine, and the northern latitudes where December sunrise would slip past 8:30am) lose. Arkansas sunrise on 1 January under permanent DST would be after 8am; in parts of the upper Midwest it approaches 9. That is why this splits by geography rather than party, and why a 308–117 House majority does not predict sixty Senate votes: the Senate over-weights exactly the states that lose the mornings.

What it means for you. Plan on the clocks going back on 1 November, and read any “Congress to end the time change” headline for which direction it ends in, because the two versions have opposite effects on your winter. If you are in Florida, permanent DST is the version the state legislature has already endorsed and the one that would suit the latitude — a Tampa sunrise in January would move from about 7:15am to about 8:15am, which is tolerable in a way that the same shift in Minnesota is not. The thing to watch for is an amendment substituting permanent standard time: that is the version that passes medical scrutiny and the version that would make the evening-light coalition abandon the bill, so its appearance would tell you the effort has turned from passing something into passing anything.

A co-pilot grounded by Oman for extremist views was hired by FlyDubai, and took a crash axe to the captain at altitude

Geopolitics US politics & policy Independent corroboration

Emirati investigators have concluded that the co-pilot of FlyDubai flight FZ1073 attempted “to carry out a terrorist act”. On Wednesday, on a Boeing 737 MAX 8 flying Dubai to Tel Aviv with 174 passengers, he attacked the captain with the cockpit’s crash axe and tried to seize the controls. The aircraft fell roughly 17,000 feet before a second crew regained control, after passengers and crew subdued him. The captain, Smit Machchhar, an Indian national, was wounded opening the cockpit to seek help.

The co-pilot is named as Hamam al-Hammami, an Omani national. The load-bearing fact is what preceded his hiring: Oman Air had barred him from flying over concerns he had adopted extremist views, reportedly after government surveillance logged visits to radical websites. FlyDubai hired him afterwards.

That sequence is the story, and it is a records problem rather than a screening one. Aviation security is built on keeping weapons away from passengers; the crash axe is required equipment, stowed in the cockpit, on the assumption that whoever can reach it is the last line of defence rather than a threat. The hardened cockpit door built after 2001 works equally well at keeping help out.

ICAO’s Annex 17, as amended, already requires background checks, vulnerability assessments, staff screening and information sharing between states. The question for the investigation is whether Oman’s finding was available to FlyDubai and ignored, or was never shareable — an intelligence-derived concern that never became a record an employer could lawfully see.

The US channel is direct: American carriers hire from the same international pilot pool, the FAA accepts foreign licence conversions, and this brief has an open thread on falsified US pilot training records. A finding that one state’s disqualification does not travel is a finding about every state’s hiring.

What to watch: the preliminary investigation report and whether it names a records failure; any FAA or EASA airworthiness or operational directive on cockpit axe stowage; and whether ICAO moves on cross-border sharing of adverse pilot findings.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not whether it happened — the UAE has said so officially — but what kind of failure it was, and the accounts split cleanly. One framing is a vetting failure by FlyDubai: a flagged pilot was hired, so somebody did not check. The other is an information-sharing failure between states: Oman’s concern was reportedly intelligence-derived and may never have existed in any form a Dubai airline could request or receive. The first has a culprit; the second has a structure, and the remedies are completely different.

Cause. The two framings trace to the two sets of people talking. Regulators and airlines have every reason to describe a systemic gap, because a systemic gap is nobody’s negligence. Reporting on the co-pilot’s history naturally emphasises that the warning existed, because it did. And both can be true at once: a warning can be real, recorded somewhere, and simultaneously unavailable to the party that needed it. Note also that this brief has not read the UAE investigators’ statement in full — it is known here through four wire accounts — and the detail about surveillance of website visits is attributed to a report rather than to any official source.

What it means for you. Treat the systemic reading as the likelier one and the consequential one, because it is the version that implicates airlines you fly. The concrete exposure for an American traveller is the foreign licence conversion and the wet-lease: US carriers and their codeshare partners draw crew from a global pool in which adverse findings are held by national authorities under their own privacy and intelligence rules, and there is no equivalent of a criminal-records check that crosses borders reliably. If the investigation concludes the Omani finding was unshareable, the fix is a multilateral records regime, which takes years; if it concludes FlyDubai could have seen it, the fix is one airline’s procedure and nothing else changes. Watch which one the report says, and treat any proposal to remove the crash axe as the cosmetic response — the axe was not the vulnerability.

Tennessee’s prisons chief is resigning. He ran Arizona’s department when it botched three executions in one year

US politics & policy Science & public health Independent corroboration

Governor Bill Lee announced on Saturday 3 October that Tennessee Department of Correction commissioner Frank Strada will step down later this month, “in the best interest of Tennesseans”, while an independent review examines the failed execution of Christa Gail Pike on 30 September. Pike remains critically ill, unconscious and on a ventilator.

Two facts about Strada turn this from a resignation into a pattern. He led Arizona’s department of corrections when it botched three executions in 2022, leaving shortly afterwards, and was then hired to run Tennessee’s. And Pike’s was the second botched execution he oversaw in Tennessee this year.

That is four failed executions under one administrator across two states. The significance is not personal culpability — a commissioner does not insert the needle — but that the same failure mode followed the same person through a change of state, statute, drug supplier and execution team. Venous access failing repeatedly is a training and protocol problem, and protocol is exactly what a commissioner owns.

It also tells you something about the hiring. Arizona’s 2022 failures were public and documented when Tennessee recruited him, which means his record was either not disqualifying to Tennessee or not examined — a question for the governor’s office rather than for the review.

Yesterday this brief wrote that the governor’s halt was the strongest available evidence that something had gone seriously wrong, because a state does not suspend its own executions over a disputed account. A commissioner’s departure and an independent review, within four days, is stronger.

What to watch: the review’s terms of reference and whether it can compel disclosure of the drugs, doses and the team’s qualifications — the material Tennessee’s secrecy statutes shield; whether the earlier 2026 failure is within its scope; and whether Pike survives, which governs whether a second attempt is ever litigated.

The Houthis say they hit Aramco in Riyadh with missiles and drones. The Saudi coalition says that is “misleading”; there was a fire

Energy & grid Geopolitics New thread Independent corroboration

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group ran “a military operation with ballistic missiles and drones” against Aramco in Riyadh on Saturday, claiming strikes that were “precise and direct” and “led to fires breaking out”. A large plume of smoke and fire was seen near an Aramco facility in the capital. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki called the claim of targeting Riyadh “misleading”. No Saudi authority has confirmed the fire or given a cause.

The fighting has intensified sharply. The coalition said that early Sunday its forces carried out 97 “precise targeting operations” against Houthi reinforcements on the Tor al-Baha front and the Taiz axis, using warplanes, drones, artillery, missiles and snipers. Yemeni government forces struck targets in Sanaa and Saada. A Saudi-backed Yemeni army claim of 700 Houthis killed in 24 hours is a belligerent’s figure and should not be treated as a casualty count.

Why this is a US story rather than a Yemen story: Riyadh is reported to be preparing an offensive to retake the Bab el-Mandeb, the second chokepoint on the route out of the Gulf, and a strike on Saudi refining or export infrastructure is the one event that would reverse the thirteen-day fall in American diesel prices. The 2019 Abqaiq attack took roughly 5% of world supply offline in a morning. Riyadh is also the party being asked to raise Hormuz shipments to compensate for its own damaged east–west pipeline.

What to watch: any Aramco or Saudi energy ministry statement identifying the facility and whether throughput was affected; satellite or flight-tracking corroboration; and the EIA weekly print on Wednesday, which is where a genuine supply event would appear.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether an Aramco facility in Riyadh was struck. The Houthis claim a precise hit causing fires; the Saudi coalition calls the Riyadh claim “misleading”; and a fire was visibly burning. Note the exact shape of the denial — al-Maliki disputed the claim of targeting Riyadh, not the existence of the fire, and no Saudi body has said what the fire was. That is a narrow denial doing the work of a broad one.

Cause. Both parties have strong and opposite incentives on this specific question, which is why neither statement is worth much alone. A Houthi claim of reaching the Saudi capital’s oil infrastructure is worth more to the Houthis than the damage itself, so claims run ahead of capability; this is a group that has claimed strikes that did not occur. Saudi Arabia has an equally strong interest in denial, because confirmed vulnerability at Aramco moves crude prices and insurance rates and invites more attempts — Riyadh underplayed the extent of the 2019 Abqaiq damage for days. The asymmetry worth noting is that a fire is observable from the ground and a target is not, which is why the visible fact and the contested fact are different facts.

What it means for you. Do not price this yet, and specifically do not read the fire as confirmation of the claim. What would change your fuel bill is not a strike on an Aramco office or storage depot in Riyadh but a strike on processing, stabilisation or export capacity — Abqaiq, Ras Tanura, Yanbu — and Riyadh is not where those are. The reason to pay attention anyway is cumulative: the American diesel price has fallen thirteen days on a G7 reserve release and partial Hormuz flows, and that entire improvement rests on Saudi Arabia being able to pump and ship more than usual. A second front that makes Saudi infrastructure a target removes the one piece of slack in the system. The observation to wait for is a throughput number, not a photograph.

An air ambulance flying a patient from Bermuda to Boston broke up over the Atlantic with six aboard

US politics & policy New thread Independent corroboration

A Gulfstream G100 air ambulance, registration C-GRJP, operated by Latitude Air Ambulance of Hamilton, Ontario, took off from Bermuda at about 11:40pm Eastern on Friday bound for Boston and lost contact with air traffic control after roughly one hour and forty-five minutes. The US Coast Guard has located a debris field off Nantucket and confirmed it is from the missing aircraft. The cutter William Sparling searched overnight.

Six people were aboard: two Canadian pilots, two medical workers, a patient and the patient’s companion — four Canadian nationals and two Bermudian. No survivors have been reported.

The structural point is about the flight itself rather than the accident. A medical transfer from Bermuda to Boston is a 700-mile overwater leg flown at night by a two-pilot crew in a twenty-to-thirty-year-old business jet, because that is how patients are moved from islands without tertiary hospitals. Scheduled aviation’s safety record does not describe this category: air ambulance work in the United States has a fatal accident rate far above airline flying, and it is flown under less demanding rules — Part 135 rather than Part 121 — with fewer crew-rest and dispatch requirements.

Jurisdiction will be its own problem: a Canadian-registered aircraft, departing a British Overseas Territory, lost in international waters en route to the United States. Investigative authority for a Canadian registration in international waters sits with the Transportation Safety Board of Canada, with the NTSB likely assisting as the destination state.

What to watch: whether the recorders are recovered from the seabed; which authority takes the investigation; and whether anything in it touches night overwater single-engine-out performance or crew duty limits, which is where this category’s accidents usually land.

A separately reported Tampa Bay regional figure of 179 confirmed cases across the three counties also circulates and is not reconcilable with the sum from here. The honest statement is that Hillsborough is the outbreak and the regional total is somewhere between the two, and this brief cannot close the gap without the Florida DOH report, which it has been unable to read for ten issues.

The new fact, and it is the one that matters: Hillsborough’s mosquito surveillance is finding dengue virus in the mosquito population in many neighbourhoods, not merely diagnosing it in people. Case counts are a record of transmission that has already happened; virus in trapped mosquitoes is a measure of transmission still available, and it is why the county is expanding treatment rather than waiting for the case curve to turn.

What to watch: 8 October, when both emergencies expire; whether the mosquito-pool positivity is published rather than described; and the Gulf disturbance, which is the only thing in this section that could reverse the response.

Energy & grid Alaska LNG would cost more than double its Gulf Coast rivals, and Seoul says it has not actually agreed to fund it.

The Alaska LNG project, whose South Korean investment the President announced on 30 September as part of a broader $200bn package, is estimated by the US developer Glenpoint at $44.5bn to $54.5bn — more than double the cost of comparable Gulf Coast liquefaction. Seoul said it had not decided whether to fund it and would first review commercial viability. Asked on Friday whether the announcement was premature, the President said: “I didn’t jump the gun.”

The cost gap is geography, not mismanagement. Gulf Coast plants sit beside existing pipelines, shipyards and a petrochemical labour force; Alaska LNG requires an 800-mile pipeline from the North Slope across permafrost and seismic terrain before any liquefaction. The project has been proposed for decades without binding commitments, which is the relevant fact about its economics.

Its advantage is real but narrow: shipping time to Asia without transiting the Panama Canal or the Strait of Malacca. In a year when two chokepoints are contested, that argument is stronger than it has been — and still has to clear a doubled capital cost.

Below the line because it is an estimate and an unsigned commitment. It leads on a final investment decision, a binding offtake agreement, or a federal loan guarantee.

Geopolitics The Coast Guard interdicted a fuel ship bound for Cuba — announced Friday, but the interception was in early September.

The US Coast Guard announced on 2 October that it had interdicted the M/V Grace in the Caribbean while the vessel was sailing toward Cuba, in what it described as a coordinated international enforcement operation against suspected illicit fuel transport. The ship had switched off its transponder, the practice that defines the so-called dark fleet. It was escorted to Progreso, Mexico, where a Mexican Navy inspection team confirmed large quantities of fuel in its ballast tanks.

Read the dates. The interception was in early September; the announcement is now. That is the trap this brief recorded on 11 September about OFAC notices — a publication date is not an event date — and it changes what the item is: not an operation today, but a disclosure today.

Fuel in ballast tanks is the detail carrying the evidence. Ballast tanks hold seawater for trim and are not cargo space; using them to move fuel is both concealment and a pollution risk, and hard to explain innocently. The context is a US fuel cut-off that increasingly resembles a maritime blockade of Cuba, after Venezuela — Cuba’s main supplier — was removed as a source.

Science & public health Philadelphia’s Academy of Natural Sciences has closed — founded 1812, the oldest natural history museum in the Americas, and the first place in the world to display a dinosaur.

The Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia closed at the end of September, a little under two years short of the bicentenary of its public opening. It was founded in 1812, opened to the public in 1828, and in 1868 mounted the world’s first display of a dinosaur skeleton. Drexel University, which assumed financial responsibility in 2011, cited rising post-pandemic costs and falling attendance. Closure was announced on 1 September; a “Save the Academy” petition gathered over 114,000 signatures, and in its final weeks attendance surged, with September revenue more than twenty times the previous year’s.

That last figure is the uncomfortable one: a museum whose closure announcement produced twenty times its normal revenue had a visibility problem as much as a demand problem, which is a different diagnosis from the one given.

The consequential question is the collection — roughly 18 million specimens, many of them type specimens, the single reference organism against which a species is defined. Those cannot be replaced, and their custody and accessibility to researchers are now a live question.

Below the line because the closure was announced a month ago and this brief has no account of where the collection goes. It leads on a disposition plan for the specimens, or a reversal.

Confidence: Independent corroboration; the specimen count is background, not from today’s reporting. Sources: NPR via KPBS · WHYY · OPB

Geopolitics A Russian strike hit a Liberian-flagged ship in Odesa and killed a crew member — the grain corridor’s insurance problem, restated.

A Russian strike hit a Liberian-flagged vessel in the port of Odesa, killing one crew member, according to gCaptain. Separately, Russian forces struck a Kyiv bridge for a third consecutive day.

The flag is the point. Liberia is the world’s largest open registry, and a Liberian-flagged hull in Odesa is almost certainly a third-country commercial ship with a third-country crew carrying Ukrainian cargo — not a Ukrainian asset. Killing a seafarer on a neutral-flagged merchant vessel in port is the event that moves war-risk premiums and charter availability for the whole Black Sea corridor, which is the mechanism by which Ukrainian grain reaches, or fails to reach, world markets and therefore food prices.

The US channel runs through those prices and through the insurance market rather than through policy: Washington has backed the corridor diplomatically, and its viability is an underwriting question settled in London.

World economy Port congestion is tying up 12% of the world’s container shipping capacity — a figure that behaves like a rate rise for importers.

Twelve percent of global container shipping capacity is currently immobilised in port congestion, according to gCaptain. For context, the figure spent most of the pre-2020 decade in the low-to-mid single digits and peaked near 14% at the worst of the 2021–22 disruption.

The arithmetic is why it matters: capacity stuck at anchor is capacity removed from the market, so a 12% congestion rate is functionally a 12% reduction in the fleet without a single ship leaving service. Freight rates respond to available capacity, not nominal capacity, which is how congestion becomes a price that US importers pay and eventually a price on a shelf.

The causes are currently additive rather than singular: Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb rerouting lengthens voyages and bunches arrivals, and bunched arrivals are what congestion is. A longer route does not just cost more fuel; it desynchronises berthing windows that were scheduled around the short route.

This brief has not seen the underlying dataset, the measurement definition, or which ports dominate the figure — all three of which determine whether 12% is a crisis or a seasonal reading.

Below the line because a single aggregate with no methodology and no port breakdown cannot carry a story. It leads with a rate series or a named port’s dwell times.

Confidence: Single-sourced; the historical comparison is this brief’s background, not from the report. Sources: gCaptain front page

Geopolitics Bosnia votes today for a three-member presidency — a constitution the United States wrote and has never been able to put down.

Bosnians vote today for the three members of the state presidency and 42 seats in the House of Representatives, with concurrent elections in the Federation and in Republika Srpska.

The US channel is unusually direct and almost always omitted: that three-member presidency, and the two-entity structure beneath it, exist because the Dayton Agreement — negotiated at a US air force base in Ohio in 1995 and signed under American guarantee — serves as Bosnia’s constitution. Washington is not an observer of this system; it is its author and one of its guarantors. American sanctions remain the main external instrument against Republika Srpska secessionist steps.

So a Bosnian election is a periodic report on whether a thirty-one-year-old American peace settlement is still holding, which is a different question from who wins. The structural issue is that Dayton stopped a war by entrenching the ethnic divisions that caused it, and every election since has been fought within that design.

This brief has no candidates, polling or result.

Below the line because it is an election day with no result, and the foreign-elections bar requires a changed US relationship. It leads on a secession step, a sanctions action, or a result that puts the Dayton structure in question.
