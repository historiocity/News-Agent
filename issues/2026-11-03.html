The Supreme Court ended it in a few hours, the night before the election

Courts US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

The Justice Department asked the Supreme Court on Monday for emergency relief requiring California to set aside the ballots of people it says are likely non-citizens. The Court denied it on Monday evening, hours after it was filed. Polls open this morning.

That is the fourth court to reject the same theory in nine days. Judge David Carter refused it in the district court on 28 October. Judge Sharon Gleason threw out the parallel Alaska suit on Sunday. A Ninth Circuit panel rejected the emergency request on Friday, two to one. And now the Supreme Court, without taking the appeal up.

The Ninth Circuit's composition is the detail worth holding on to. The two judges in the majority were Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, and Gabriel Sanchez, a Biden appointee; the dissenter, Patrick Bumatay, is also a Trump appointee. This did not divide on the lines it would have been expected to. Bennett and Sanchez wrote that "there is a material risk that granting the United States its requested relief would result in disenfranchisement and could jeopardize the County's entire election."

Three legal grounds have now been named across the rulings, and each is independent of the others. The Elections Clause, which gives states authority to run elections. The Purcell principle, that courts should not change election rules on the eve of an election. And the National Voter Registration Act's bar on systematically removing ineligible voters within 90 days of a federal election — the same statute whose preamble the Department had relied on to bring the case.

Judge Carter's characterisation last week, quoted in the reporting, is the sharpest thing any of the judges said: the Department's filings formed "a broad, ongoing pattern of attempted election interference by federal officials."

What remains is not the lawsuits. The grand jury subpoenas to Los Angeles registrar staff are returnable tomorrow. The Department's letters warning county employees that tabulating flagged ballots risks making them "aiders and abettors to a federal crime" have not been withdrawn. And the number in dispute was always soft: county officials found many of the 20,000 were not registered at all, and a UCLA analysis filed Saturday put 42 per cent of the names nowhere in California's voter files.

What to watch: whether the subpoenas proceed tomorrow; whether any new filing appears tonight or tomorrow in a state where a result is close; and whether the Department's letters are retracted.

The denial, the Ninth Circuit quotation and Judge Carter's phrase are all as NPR reported them; this brief read no order, opinion or filing in this case at any stage, which over nine days of leading on it is a real limitation and is stated here rather than in a footnote. Correction: the Treasury hump was wider three weeks ago than at any point this brief called a record

Macro & markets Correction Primary source

Since early October this brief has tracked the gap between the twenty-year and ten-year Treasury yields and built an argument on it: that the gap was unusually stable, that +37 basis points was "the widest reading recorded here", that thirteen sessions inside three basis points made it structural, and that +40 on 30 October was a breakout. The series says otherwise, and the series was in a file this brief downloads every morning.

The month in full: the gap opened October at +40, rose to +43 on 7 October, fell steadily to +34 on 21 October, and has climbed back to +40. That is a round trip, not a narrow range with a breakout at the end. The +37 described as a record on 26 October was lower than nine readings earlier in the same month.

How the error happened is the useful part. This brief started printing the figure in the third week of October and treated what it had printed as what the series had done. The phrase "recorded here" was literally true and functioned as a claim about the market, which is the worst kind of true. Every day since, the twenty-year and ten-year columns have been read out of the same CSV; computing the difference for the preceding three weeks took one pass over a file already on disk.

This is the same failure as the refinery utilisation story on 29 October, which called 92.8 per cent a constraint without checking that it was a four-year high for the date. The rule written into the configuration on 30 October was "always read a level against its own history before calling it low." It was not applied to the series this brief looks at most often, for four days after it was written.

What survives. The gap is at +40 and has risen nine basis points in eleven sessions, which is a real move. Yesterday's print: ten-year 4.91 per cent, down 3; twenty-year 5.31, down 3; two-year 4.59, up 3. The structural claim does not survive, and nothing in a 22-day series can establish it either way.

What to watch: the quarterly refunding tomorrow, which is what this month of curve-watching was pointed at; and whether the gap goes through +43, which would be the first reading this brief could honestly call a high.

Confidence: Primary, computed this morning from the Treasury file for every business day since 1 October. The series before 1 October was not pulled, so even +43 is only the highest since then and is not described as more than that. The NRC has shortened its hearings for the second time in eight days

Energy & grid Regulatory Continuation Primary document Underreported

A final rule published this morning revises the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's rules of practice and procedure to "streamline contested adjudications in NRC licensing proceedings". It implements the ADVANCE Act of 2024 and Executive Order 14300, "Ordering the Reform of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission". It takes effect 3 December 2026 and applies to proceedings noticed on or after that date.

A contested adjudication is the hearing in which somebody who is not the applicant — a state, a tribe, a local group — gets to put a safety or environmental contention to a licensing board and have it decided on a record. It is the only part of nuclear licensing where a member of the public has standing to make the Commission answer a question.

This is the second rule in eight days to reduce that component. On 26 October this brief led on a final rule adding §50.43(f), which lets a commercial licence applicant reference a prior Department of Energy or Department of War authorisation as the means of showing its design performs its safety functions — importing a finding made in a process with no hearing and no intervenor. Today's rule makes the hearings that remain shorter. Taken together: less of the safety case is made in a proceeding the public can join, and the proceeding itself is quicker.

The case for it is real and this brief made it eight days ago. The Commission has more applications than it can process; repetition is waste; the ADVANCE Act is Congress's own instruction to speed deployment. The case against is that "streamlining" and "narrowing" are the same operation described from different ends, and which one it is depends on specifics this brief has not read — what gets cut, whether contention admissibility standards move, and whether the time limits bind the board or the parties.

What to watch: the first proceeding noticed after 3 December and whether anyone is admitted as an intervenor; whether the contention admissibility rules changed, which is the provision that actually decides who gets heard; and whether the pending microreactor framework carries the same approach.

Confidence: Primary document, summary and dates only. Published today and read at source, but the regulatory text, the response to comments and the specific procedural changes were not read — so this brief cannot say what was streamlined, only that the Commission says it streamlined it. The characterisation of contested adjudications is general knowledge. Sources: FR 2026-22443 · GovInfo PDF · 26 October

HHS has written gender dysphoria out of the disability rule

Health Regulatory New subject Primary document

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 prohibits disability discrimination by anyone receiving federal money — which, in health care, means almost every hospital, clinic and insurer that touches Medicare or Medicaid. A final rule from the HHS Office for Civil Rights, published this morning, changes who it covers.

The statute excludes "gender identity disorders not resulting from physical impairments" from the definition of disability. The question has been whether gender dysphoria, a distinct and later diagnosis, falls inside that exclusion. The May 2024 final rule left the matter ambiguous in its preamble. Today's rule resolves it: HHS now interprets the exclusion "to encompass gender dysphoria not resulting from a physical impairment." The Department says the clarification is needed "to ensure compliance with the best reading of the governing statute." It takes effect 4 January 2027.

What this does in practice is remove a legal route, not a service. Section 504 is an anti-discrimination statute: a person covered by it who is refused care, or given worse care, because of their condition has a federal claim. Someone outside it does not, under this statute. The rule does not say what care may be provided; it says who can complain about being denied it under section 504, which is a narrower and more consequential thing than most coverage of such rules suggests.

It is also a contested legal question rather than a settled one. Federal courts have divided on whether gender dysphoria falls within the statutory exclusion, with at least one court of appeals having held that it can be a disability under a companion statute. An agency adopting one side of a live circuit question by regulation is making a choice that litigation will test, and the rule's own language — "the best reading of the governing statute" — is the formula used when an agency expects to defend its interpretation in court.

What to watch: whether the rule is challenged before 4 January; whether the companion regulations at other departments follow; and whether HHS applies the same reading in enforcement of section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, which is the other main route.

Confidence: Primary document, summary read; the response to public comments and the regulatory text were not. The statement that federal courts have divided on this question is this brief's general knowledge and was not verified against any case this morning — it is the kind of claim this brief has twice this week been wrong about when working from memory, and it is flagged accordingly. Sources: FR 2026-22380 · GovInfo PDF

The rules for who collects America's organs have been rewritten

Health Regulatory New subject Primary document Underreported

Fifty-odd organ procurement organisations hold federally granted monopolies over defined geographic areas. Each is the only body that may approach families about donation and recover organs in its territory, and performance between them varies enormously. CMS has published a final rule revising their conditions for coverage, effective 4 January 2027.

The scale, in the rule's own opening: at least 100,000 people are on the waiting list for a transplant at any time, and another person joins every eight minutes. Many wait years; some die waiting.

The provision that matters is about competition. The rule "clarifies when an OPO's service area is open for competition", revises the designation requirements, revises definitions, and updates the appeals process. An OPO's service area opening to competition is the only real consequence a failing one faces — the sanction is losing the territory to a better operator — so the rules on when that happens are the enforcement mechanism of the whole system.

CMS is explicit about what it is not changing, and the sentence reads as a pre-emptive answer to the obvious worry: "this final rule does not revise the focus on improving the volume of donors and transplants assessed in the outcome measures or the tier structure used for re-certification and de-certification." The tiers, introduced after years of evidence that some OPOs recovered far fewer organs than comparable ones, stay. What changes is the procedure around them.

That combination — keep the measures, add "operational flexibility" and clarify the appeals — can be read two ways, and this brief cannot tell which without the regulatory text. Clearer rules about when a territory opens could make de-certification more usable; a better appeals process could make it slower. The outcome measures are only as strong as the procedure for acting on them.

What to watch: whether any service area actually opens for competition under the new procedure; how long an appeal takes compared with the old process; and the next round of OPO tier assignments.

Confidence: Primary document, executive summary only — the regulatory text, the comment responses and the severability discussion were not read, and the two readings offered above are stated as unresolved because they are. The waiting-list figures are CMS's own. The description of OPO monopolies and the history of the tier system is general knowledge, not from the document. Sources: FR 2026-22381 · GovInfo PDF

Regulatory The FCC has revised its broadcast and cable equal employment opportunity rules.

A final rule in today's edition revises the FCC's broadcast and cable equal employment opportunity rules. These are the obligations that require stations and cable operators to recruit widely for vacancies, keep records of their outreach, and file reports the Commission can audit — a regime that has existed in some form since the 1960s and has been litigated and rewritten repeatedly since.

Which direction this rule goes is the entire story and this brief cannot say it. "Review of the Commission's rules" in a title is compatible with tightening the reporting, loosening it, or deleting the Form 395-B data collection that has been the recurring fight. That last one would be the significant version, because the data collection is what makes the rest auditable.

Below the line because it was identified by title and not read, on a morning when five other documents were. It is today's negative check: the largest thing here left unopened, and it is on the owed list.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: FR 2026-22408

Regulatory Two corrections in one edition, one of which lowers a campaign-finance penalty by ten dollars.

Today's edition carries two corrections of previously published regulatory text, and the pair is a decent illustration of how the Code of Federal Regulations is actually maintained.

The first is issued by the Office of the Federal Register itself, not by the agency, to fix an error in the most recent annual revision of the CFR. In 11 CFR 111.24, the Federal Election Commission's compliance-procedure penalties: "$84,852" becomes "$87,056", and "$7,455" becomes "$7,445". One figure rises by $2,204 and the other falls by $10 — the second being the signature of a transposed digit surviving into print and then being caught a year later. The correction publishes on election day, which is coincidence rather than meaning.

The second is EPA correcting an "inadvertent error in the regulatory text" of the Lime Manufacturing hazardous air pollutants rule — finalised on 16 July 2024, corrected today, effective immediately. Two years and four months between publishing a binding emission standard and fixing the words in it.

Below the line because corrections are not events. They are here because this brief reads the Federal Register daily and the maintenance layer — who fixes what, how long it takes, who notices — is part of understanding what a published rule actually is.

Confidence: Primary documents, both read in full. Sources: FR 2026-22402 · FR 2026-22404

Energy & grid Pipeline safety rules for liquefied natural gas facilities are being amended.

The Transportation Department has proposed amendments to the pipeline safety regulations for liquefied natural gas facilities. The existing standards at 49 CFR part 193 date substantially from 1980 and have been criticised from both directions for years — as outdated by industry, and as inadequate to the scale of modern export terminals by safety advocates.

The timing places it in a run this brief has been tracking. In the past week: a Defense Production Act determination declaring petroleum refining and logistics capacity a defence-essential industrial resource, section 232 findings on wind turbines and on robotics, and helium added to the critical materials list partly for gas-infrastructure leak testing. LNG export capacity is the part of American energy infrastructure that has grown fastest, and its safety rules are the part that has moved least.

Below the line because it is a proposed rule identified by title and not read, so this brief cannot say whether the amendments raise the standards, align them with international codes, or relax them — which is, again, the only thing a reader needs. No comment deadline is given here because none was read.

Confidence: Identified, not read. The characterisation of part 193's age and the criticism of it is general knowledge, not from the document. Sources: FR 2026-22396

Energy & grid The diesel premium set another record, the day after this brief said the streak had ended.

Yesterday this brief reported that the diesel-minus-petrol spread had narrowed for the first time in nine days, ending a run of eight consecutive records at a peak of $2.1307. It added: "One day is one day, and this brief has spent the past fortnight learning that lesson expensively on this exact thread."

This morning: diesel $6.2875, down 0.46 cents; regular $4.1493, down 2.01. The spread is $2.1382 — above the old peak. Petrol is falling faster than diesel again, which is the whole mechanism: both fuels are getting cheaper and the gap keeps widening because the distillate side is sticky.

The caution was the right call and it is worth noting why. A single narrowing print after eight widenings is exactly the shape of noise, and treating it as a turn would have been the third wrong mechanism on this thread in a fortnight. The honest version yesterday was "one day is one day", and today is why.

Below the line because it is one more reading in a series, and because the question this thread existed to answer — why — was settled on Saturday and Sunday. The next thing that could change the picture is tomorrow's EIA release, which reaches this brief on Thursday.

Confidence: Primary, read at source. Sources: AAA · 2 November
