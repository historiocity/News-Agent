The CFTC is taking sports betting out of its own statute on Monday, and putting prediction markets in

US financials US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

Two Commodity Futures Trading Commission documents publish on Monday and must be read together. An interim final rule, effective 19 October, excludes casino-style gambling products from the definition of a "swap". A proposed rule does the opposite for something else: it explicitly includes event contracts — sports, politics, cultural and weather events — in that same definition. Comments on both close 18 November.

The operative text is a two-part test. A product escapes the swap definition only if it is offered by someone licensed to offer gambling under state law or under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, acting within that licence and regulated as gambling by that state or tribe — and it is not traded on an exchange, a board of trade, a swap execution facility or any other multilateral or negotiated market. Sports wagers and casino games are then named as excluded.

So the line is venue and licence, not substance. The same bet on the same game is gambling at a sportsbook and a federally regulated swap on an exchange.

Why now is in the preamble, unusually plainly. The Sixth Circuit, in KalshiEX LLC v. Schuler, found sports event contracts legally indistinguishable from casino-style wagers, and worried that if they are swaps then every sports wager — in a casino, on an online book, between two friends in a bar — falls under the Commodity Exchange Act. The Ninth Circuit reasoned the same way in KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad. Thirty-nine states and the District of Columbia told the Commission courts are "hopelessly confused and divided". Kentucky said treating event contracts as swaps would transfer every ounce of authority over sports wagering to the CFTC; New Jersey told the Supreme Court nothing in the statute makes the CFTC the sole regulator of American sports gambling.

What to watch: a challenge to the good-cause exception; what the states say by 18 November, having won the carve-out they wanted; and whether the inclusions proposal is finalised as drafted, since that is the half that claims territory.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A dispute about law between the courts and the agency, now visible in the text of a rule. Two federal appellate courts have held that what matters is what a contract is — a sports event contract is sports gambling "regardless of whether the exchange calls them swaps", as the Ninth Circuit put it. Monday's rule holds that what matters is where it trades and who is licensed. Those cannot both be the governing test, and the rule is written so that the identical economic exposure falls on either side of the line depending only on the venue.

The cause. The agency is not confused; it is cornered, and the preamble nearly says so. If substance governs, the CFTC becomes the national regulator of sports betting by accident, against the objection of 39 states, against tribal gaming revenue protected by its own federal statute, and with a case pending at the Supreme Court. The venue test is the only line that lets it keep the exchanges and disclaim the sportsbooks. The states' vantage is the mirror image: they wrote gaming law, they collect the tax, and they have no interest in the substance argument winning.

What it means for you. Read Monday's pair as a jurisdictional settlement offer, not a clarification. The CFTC is giving the states everything outside the exchange in order to keep everything on it — and the thing on it is the fast-growing part. For the question this brief left open yesterday, the answer arrived in two days: the CFTC has now spoken, and it is claiming event contracts rather than ceding them, which puts it on a collision course with the SEC's own claim to the same ground. What would settle it is the Supreme Court taking the Kalshi question, or the two agencies filing against each other. Neither has happened.

Both were read in full from GovInfo's PDFs of the FR-2026-10-19 package, including the operative regulatory text, the good-cause discussion and the voting summaries. On each, the voting summary records Chairman Selig voting in the affirmative and names no other commissioner — consistent with this brief's reporting that the Commission has been operating with one voting member. Kenya has a third Ebola case, and the US entry order does not cover Kenya

Science & public health US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Kenya's Health Ministry confirmed on Friday 16 October a third case of Bundibugyo virus disease: a 65-year-old woman, a primary contact and close relative of the first two patients, in quarantine since 6 October. She developed fever, sore throat and malaise, was isolated and tested positive. Of 130 listed contacts, 127 have been traced and are in quarantine. The ministry says there is no evidence of community spread and the public risk remains low.

The sequence is the point. Kenya's first patient had been ill in the DRC for about a month, travelled by road into Uganda, flew to Nairobi on 3 October and died on the 5th. Medication, the health minister said, may have masked his symptoms at airport screening. The second case was a family member already in quarantine; so is the third.

Now set that against the American instrument. On 15 October this brief reported the CDC order under 42 U.S.C. 265 (91 FR 65556) suspending entry of people travelling from the DRC, Uganda and South Sudan — expressly including lawful permanent residents. Kenya is not on that list. Nairobi is one of the largest aviation hubs in Africa and a principal connecting point for US-bound traffic, and the one case that moved internationally did so by crossing a land border and then boarding a plane in a country the order does not name.

That is not an argument for adding Kenya. It is the observation that a country-of-departure rule tracks borders while an outbreak tracks journeys, and the gap is now demonstrated rather than hypothetical. The DRC epidemic is the second-largest on record, with more than 4,300 deaths since May. The Bundibugyo strain has no licensed vaccine; candidates are in early human trials.

What to watch: the order's 10 November expiry, when it lapses unless renewed — the live question is whether the country list is extended, and Kenya is the test; and whether any Kenyan case appears outside the traced contact group, which is what "no community spread" currently rests on.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. The third case is reported by Al Jazeera quoting the Health Ministry's own statement; the first two cases, the 128–130 contact figures and the travel sequence are corroborated across earlier reporting from Daily Maverick, Dawan Africa and Australian outlets carrying the wire. The ministry statement itself was not read at source. The CDC order's terms are primary, read in full on 15 October. Of $962m donors say they sent to the Ebola response, barely $200m could be traced

Science & public health Geopolitics New thread Underreported Independent corroboration

At a press conference in Kinshasa on 14 October, the director-general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, Dr Jean Kaseya, said donors report having disbursed about $962m to organisations implementing the Ebola response in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. A joint verification exercise with Congolese health authorities could trace slightly more than $200m of it to the structures actually running that response. More than $700m could not be followed to a destination.

The precise claim matters and is narrower than the headline figure suggests. This is a tracing failure, not a finding of theft: money that donors recorded as disbursed to implementing partners could not be matched, in a joint exercise, to the clinics, surveillance teams and burial units on the other end. Some of that gap will be overheads, procurement held upstream, and partners' own accounting. How much is the question nobody has answered, and Kinshasa has said it will demand answers from the organisations concerned.

The American channel is direct. The United States is historically the largest single funder of outbreak response, this outbreak has produced a US entry suspension now in force, and the money moves through implementing partners that are substantially American NGOs and contractors. If more than two-thirds of disbursed funds cannot be traced to delivery, the US cannot currently say what its own contribution bought.

Context on scale, from Africa CDC's own earlier statements: roughly $2.997bn pledged as of September, which Kaseya was careful to call pledges rather than money moved; about $452m cited as disbursed in August. Those are different snapshots on different bases and should not be reconciled into a series.

What to watch: whether any named donor publishes a reconciliation; whether USAID's successor arrangements or Congress ask for one; and whether the verification exercise is repeated, because a second pass is what would separate slow accounting from missing money.

Confidence: Independent corroboration for the figures, which appear consistently across Africanews, francophone outlets reporting the Kinshasa press conference, and an Al Jazeera report citing Reuters. No primary document was read — neither the verification exercise nor an Africa CDC release — and no donor has been named. Sources: Africanews · Afrik.com (paraphrased from French) · Al Jazeera

Three rules on Monday rewrite how federal employees are promoted, rated and let go

US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

The Office of Personnel Management has three final rules in Monday's issue. Separately each reads as housekeeping. Together they are a single programme, and this brief would have missed it by reading any one of them.

Elimination of time-in-grade. Until now a candidate generally could not advance to a competitive-service General Schedule position at GS-6 or above without 52 weeks in a position one grade lower — two grades for certain two-grade-interval occupations. That requirement is removed. Occupational qualification standards still apply; the waiting period does not. Promotion speed inside the civil service has just been deregulated.

Administrative leave for workforce realignment, effective 18 November. This one addresses, by name, deferred resignation programmes — the mechanism used across government in 2025 — and clarifies when an agency may grant administrative leave during a reduction-in-force notice period. It also settles something sharper: when an agency may accept or deny an employee's request to withdraw a resignation tendered under such a programme. An agency may refuse to let you un-resign.

Managing senior professional performance, effective 18 November, issued jointly with the Office of Management and Budget. It covers senior-level and scientific-or-professional employees — the government's senior scientists and technical specialists — and aligns their appraisal with the Senior Executive Service. It also removes the prohibition on a standardised distribution of performance rating levels. Forced ranking of federal scientists is now permitted where it was barred.

What to watch: whether the time-in-grade repeal shows up as faster promotions or as faster hiring of outsiders into mid-grade posts, which are different things; the first denial of a resignation withdrawal to be litigated; and whether any agency actually adopts a forced distribution.

Confidence: Primary source, subject to the same caveat as every document in this issue: dated Monday 19 October, not yet published. All three were read from GovInfo's PDFs. The administrative-leave rule drew 30 comments, including from two unions; the time-in-grade rule was adopted substantially as proposed. Sources: OPM, Elimination of Time-in-Grade · OPM, Administrative Leave for Workforce Realignment · OPM and OMB, Managing Senior Professional Performance

From Monday you can owe foreign customs charges on a parcel that never arrived

World economy US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

The Postal Service is revising the International Mail Manual, effective 19 October, to set out who pays when a foreign customs authority bills a charge on something you posted — including after you have already bought the postage. Comments close 18 November, after the rule is in force.

The trigger is named in the document: on 1 July 2026 the European Commission eliminated its de minimis exemption for most low-value goods entering the EU customs territory. The American de minimis repeal, which this brief has tracked all autumn, has a mirror image, and this is it arriving at the retail counter.

The mechanics. Where duties, taxes, import fees or other charges are assessed on a mailpiece and have not been paid in full, the mailer is responsible for paying them, or for reimbursing the Postal Service. Destination posts may add a handling or delivery fee on top of the duty, to cover clearing the item and collecting at the door — and the new text warns those processing charges may apply even where no duty or tax is due.

Then the sentence that makes this a story. The obligation holds "regardless of whether the item is delivered, lost, damaged, or returned." You can post a parcel, pay for postage, have the parcel lost in transit, and still owe the destination country's charges on it. The Postal Service also disclaims responsibility for the accuracy of what a foreign customs authority, postal operator or tax authority calculates.

What to watch: the first reimbursement demands reaching ordinary senders and small exporters, which is when this becomes visible; whether the comment period after the fact changes anything; and whether other destination countries follow the EU, because the rule is written generally and is not EU-specific.

Confidence: Primary source; dated Monday, not yet published. The quoted phrase and the liability provisions were read from the amended manual text at new section 424, Unpaid Destination Customs Charges. Sources: USPS, Customs Requirements and Payment Procedures for Certain Customs Charges

The diesel premium went through two dollars, a day after this brief said to watch for it

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

AAA's national averages this morning: diesel $6.3276, up 0.15 cents overnight and a fifth consecutive daily rise. Regular petrol $4.3085, down 1.86 cents on the day and 5.84 cents on the week. The two are moving apart faster than diesel alone is rising, because petrol is falling while diesel climbs.

So the premium is now $2.0191, through two dollars. Yesterday this brief put the number at $1.999, called it effectively two dollars, and named the threshold to watch: whether it would hold above $2.00, because that is the level that passes into freight rates and then into shelf prices. It went through within a day.

The year-on-year comparison is the one that shows what kind of move this is. Diesel is +73.7% against a year ago; regular is +41.2%. Twelve months ago the premium was 59 cents, which is close to the ordinary relationship between the two fuels. It has more than tripled.

A caution about what this does and does not measure. The premium is a daily card-reader average, which is the right instrument for a level and the wrong one for a trend — the weekly EIA survey, this brief's trend instrument, last read $6.179 for the week ending 12 October and was falling. Both remain true, for the reasons set out in yesterday's divergence box. Tuesday 20 October brings the first weekly reading that contains this week's rises, and that is the number that will say whether the daily move is a trend or a wobble.

What to watch: Tuesday's EIA print; whether petrol keeps falling, since half of this widening is coming from the petrol side and that is a different story about demand; and the first freight surcharge filings to move.

The emergency this brief could not confirm on Friday is real, extended to 22 October

Florida & Tampa Bay Science & public health Continuation Primary source

Yesterday this brief reported that the last Tampa Bay dengue emergency order it could find ran to 15 October, that no later order was reachable, and that it would not infer a lapse from a missing document. That was the right call and the answer is now on the county's own site.

Pinellas County's state of local emergency for dengue is in force and has been extended to 22 October, by County Administrator Barry Burton under authority delegated by the Board of County Commissioners, which adopted the declaring resolution on 24 September. The county's dengue page states plainly that Pinellas is under both a mosquito-borne illness alert and a state of local emergency.

What the declaration is for is the part worth knowing, and the county says it: the delegation lets the Administrator approve mosquito-control expenditure quickly, and it preserves eligibility for state reimbursement if state funding becomes available. It is a procurement and money instrument, not a restriction on anyone.

One concrete measure for residents. Pinellas Solid Waste has waived the residential tire disposal fee: up to four tires per resident, none more than 36 inches in diameter, free at the Solid Waste Disposal Complex, 3095 114th Avenue North, St Petersburg. Tires must be separated from other waste; rims may stay attached; a valid driver's licence is needed as proof of residency. Stored tires hold water and breed the mosquitoes that carry this virus, which is why the fee is the lever.

The county cites the Florida Department of Health report released 15 October — the Week 40 report this brief read yesterday — for 37 confirmed Pinellas cases and 351 across the Tampa Bay region. Those are the same figures published here, from the same document.

What to watch: the 22 October expiry, which under Florida's seven-day rule means another decision next Thursday; the Week 41 report, not yet posted, which is the first that can carry October onsets; and Hillsborough, whose own order status this brief still cannot establish.

Geopolitics Three national emergencies are continued for another year on Monday, and one of them is the legal floor under the Ebola response.

Monday's issue carries exactly three presidential documents, all of them annual continuations of national emergencies declared under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act: the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Sudan, and significant narcotics traffickers centred in Colombia. All three notices are dated 15 October.

The DRC emergency rests on Executive Order 13413 of 27 October 2006, declared over violence and atrocities threatening regional stability. Twenty years on it is also, incidentally, part of the legal architecture standing behind an American government response to an Ebola epidemic in the same country — sanctions authority and public-health authority running in parallel over the same territory, for entirely unrelated reasons.

An annual continuation is the least newsworthy document a president signs and the most reliable: it is how you can tell, without any announcement, that a twenty-year-old emergency is still switched on. There are dozens of them.

US financials The IRS will take your credit card directly from Monday, and may charge you a fee for it.

Final Treasury and IRS regulations on payment of tax by commercially acceptable means take effect 19 October. They implement a change made by the Taxpayer First Act allowing the IRS to accept credit and debit card payments directly, rather than routing taxpayers to third-party payment processors as it does now.

The regulations do two things at once. They remove the restriction on the IRS paying a fee under contracts related to card payments, and they remove the restriction on the IRS imposing a fee or charge on people who pay by card. Both limits go.

Today a taxpayer paying by card pays a processor's fee of roughly two per cent, which on a large balance is a serious sum. Cutting the processor out could reduce that. It could also simply move the fee from a private intermediary to the government, and the regulation is permissive rather than prescriptive on which happens.

Below the line because the rule authorises a change without setting a fee, and until the IRS publishes what it will charge, nobody can say whether paying tax by card gets cheaper or not.

Energy & grid Fuel additives are not fuel, the product safety regulator decides, so their containers need no flame arrester.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission is issuing an interpretive rule, effective 19 October, reading the phrase "flammable liquid fuels" in the Portable Fuel Container Safety Act of 2020 not to cover fuel additives.

The reasoning is textual. An additive is intended to be added to fuel for a purpose unrelated to creating heat or power — improving engine performance, cleaning components, extending engine life, stabilising fuel, preventing weather effects. On the Commission's reading, that puts it outside the statute even though the liquid in the bottle is itself flammable.

The practical effect is that containers of fuel additive do not require the flame mitigation devices the Act imposes on portable fuel containers — the flame arresters put there after a run of injuries from vapour ignition when petrol cans were poured near a flame. Whether an additive bottle poses the same risk is a factual question the interpretive rule does not reach, because it did not have to.

Below the line because it is an interpretive rule, which binds nobody by itself, and no injury data was presented or read.

US politics & policy The Park Service is restoring the 1981 Alaska hunting framework it spent the last decade replacing.

A National Park Service final rule in Monday's issue amends the regulations for hunting, fishing, trapping and other harvest activities in Alaska park areas, and the procedures for restricting public use and access, including for subsistence.

The rule's own description of itself is the news: it "restores the regulatory framework adopted to implement the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 in 1981" — the framework that governed for more than three decades — with targeted updates after public comment. Restoring a 1981 rule means undoing what replaced it, and what replaced it was a decade of contested rulemaking over predator control and state-versus-federal authority on park lands.

The restored framework is described as recognising the distinct roles of the State of Alaska, the Federal Subsistence Management Program and the Park Service. A companion proposed rule in the same issue would allow off-road vehicles for subsistence use in Gates of the Arctic.

US politics & policy Commerce is trimming the rules on when its employees testify, and insists nothing changes.

A Commerce Department final rule in Monday's issue amends the regulations governing testimony by current and former employees and the production of departmental documents in legal proceedings — the department's Touhy regulations, which control whether a litigant can compel a federal official to testify or hand over records.

The department says it is removing language that is redundant, references other authorities, or offers unnecessary elaboration, to "promote efficiency and simplicity", and states that it does so without significantly altering any entitlements, obligations, or processes.

That disclaimer is the thing worth filing. Touhy rules are what a plaintiff runs into when they need a government witness, and the elaboration being removed is, in litigation, often the part that tells you what the department must consider before refusing. A simplification that removes criteria and promises to change nothing is a claim that can only be tested the next time someone is refused.

Below the line because the redline was not read, so whether anything of substance went with the surplusage is not established here.

AI & tech industry Two upper C-band rules quietly switch on for real on Monday, their paperwork finally cleared.

The FCC is announcing that the Office of Management and Budget approved, on 21 September, the information-collection requirements in two rules from its upper C-band order — 47 CFR 27.1412(e) and 27.1419. Those amendatory instructions were published on 31 July and become effective 19 October.

The underlying proceeding is the expansion of flexible use in the 3.98–4.2 GHz band, the upper portion of C-band. A Paperwork Reduction Act notice is the dullest document the FCC publishes and it is a reliable signal of commencement: a rule with an unapproved collection cannot operate, so the approval date is when the machinery actually starts.
