Correction: Xi summit deliverables landed — $60 bn tariff recommendations, 20 mt of US coal, a standing Board of Trade

Both governments have now published the product of last week’s Washington visit. Under the US–China Board of Trade (chartered in Beijing in May, operationalised this week), the two sides recommend more favourable tariff treatment for about $30 billion of non-sensitive goods in each direction — $60 billion two-way. USTR Ambassador Jamieson Greer’s 27 Sep statement: the US list covers agricultural products through medical devices and unlocks improved access for “about 30 percent of U.S. exports to China”; the China-to-US list covers household goods, toys and similar consumer items. A White House fact sheet adds the energy half this brief under-weighted: China will import at least 10 million metric tons of US coal in 2027 and again in 2028 (20 mt across the two years). The Board of Trade also stood up an agriculture working group; a separate Board of Investment was established; and the two sides agreed a “Super Intelligence” dialogue with a next exchange by November 2026 plus a bilateral incident channel — the AI half Sunday’s Grok already carried.

What is still not published: the actual tariff rates, the size of each cut, or an effective date. Greer and the White House both use “recommendations” / “may qualify” language that still has to clear domestic procedures. Handelsblatt’s front page carried the €/$60 billion package ahead of reachable US wire fronts — the second time this month a European business daily led the China trade story here.

What to watch: whether the 30-for-30 lists harden into schedules with rates and dates; whether the coal tonnage has enforcement or purchase contracts attached; the Board of Trade’s first quarterly meeting; any interaction with the remaining Section 301 exclusions.

Divergence — recommendation vs deal

What is disputed. Not the existence of the lists — USTR, the White House and Chinese commerce releases all carry them. The dispute is whether a Board-of-Trade “recommendation” subject to domestic process is a summit deliverable or a press release.

Why they differ. A recommendation without rates is not a tariff cut; a 20 mt coal commitment without published contracts is not a cargo. But an institution that meets quarterly, with reciprocal product lists and a named energy tonnage, is also not “nothing both sides would sign.” The error this brief made was treating the missing schedule as proof of a missing agreement.

What it means for you. Hold the legal-mechanism half and the market-effect half separately (settled preference). Mechanism: Board of Trade + lists + coal tonnage + SI dialogue. Market effect: not priced until rates and dates exist. Do not re-infer absence from the next quiet day.

FAA rescinds 13 of 14 CE-500 single-pilot exemptions, citing falsified training records

Federal Register document 2026-19843, published Monday, is a policy statement: the FAA will stop issuing exemptions that let transport-category Cessna Citation CE-500-series jets (type-certificated for two pilots) be flown by one, and it is rescinding the exemptions still outstanding. Effective 29 September 2026. Pilots holding single-pilot logbook endorsements under those exemptions must stop; the endorsements are no longer valid relief from §§ 91.9 and 91.531. A § 61.58 proficiency check completed under an exemption can still satisfy PIC requirements for a two-pilot operation.

The review that produced the notice began in 2024. The FAA says it found incomplete records, required training done in an advanced aviation training device contrary to the exemptions’ conditions and limitations, and improper designated-pilot-examiner checks “that included falsified records.” Result: 13 of the 14 Part 61 exemption holders were denied an extension or had the exemption rescinded. Separately, the agency’s accident review finds Part 25 CE-500 aircraft operated single-pilot involved in “approximately twice the rate” of accidents as Part 23 CE-500/510/525 aircraft flown single-pilot — undermining the equivalent-level-of-safety finding every exemption needs. Models affected include Citation/Citation I (500), II (550), S/II (S550), 552, V/Ultra/Bravo/Encore/Encore+ (560). Not affected: Part 23 types already certificated single-pilot (CE-501/551, Mustang 510, CitationJet 525 family).

This came out of the whole-day RULE/PRORULE sweep, not a thread. NBAA and the Citation Jet Pilots association have questioned the no-notice timing even while accepting the safety predicate.

What to watch: the individual rescission actions against remaining holders; any petition for stay; how Part 135 operators rewrite ops specs with their POIs.

Sources: FR 2026-19843 (primary; full text read) · NBAA / CJP response · trade write-ups (AvBrief, AeroCorner) for model list corroboration

DOT’s Monday CAFE text: reset MY2022–2031, kill credit trading from MY2028, ~34.5 mpg by 2031

Sunday’s Grok carried the Oval Office announcement and flagged that the rule did not yet exist. Monday’s substance is on the Department of Transportation briefing-room page for the “Freedom Means Affordable Cars” / SAFE III proposal (and in the December 2025 NPRM the announcement implements). NHTSA proposes to reset CAFE standards for model years 2022–2031, developed “without consideration of electric vehicles and credit trading.” From the reset baseline: passenger-car stringency rises 0.5% per year MY2023–2026, 0.35% in MY2027, 0.25% MY2029–2031; light trucks 0.5% MY2023–2026, 0.7% in MY2027, 0.25% MY2029–2031. Fleetwide average projected at roughly 34.5 mpg in MY2031 (against the prior ~50.4 mpg path). The proposal would eliminate the CAFE credit-trading program starting in MY2028, and reclassify crossovers and small SUVs as passenger automobiles rather than light trucks.

Monday-run rule catch. Reporting (CBS, Washington Post, Washington Times) puts the largest slice near $567 million for HHS refugee and migrant services, plus about $70 million for international education grants, with further cuts touching migrant-student education, housing counseling, minority business development and selected research lines. Underreported Energy & grid New Primary source

EPA proposes reconsidering the 2024 gasoline-distribution air rules — and says emissions will not change

Federal Register 2026-19774 (proposed rule; reconsideration). EPA is reopening pieces of its 8 May 2024 NESHAP/NSPS final rule for gasoline distribution facilities and bulk gasoline terminals: operating limits and continuous-compliance requirements for air emission control devices, vapor-tightness requirements for gasoline cargo tanks, modification criteria in the NSPS, plus technical corrections. Docket EPA-HQ-OAR-2025-0302; comments due 12 November 2026 (PRA comments to OMB by 28 October). The agency’s own abstract claim: it “does not anticipate” VOC or HAP emission impacts because the amendments “do not impact the stringency of the emission standards,” and it “expects… no impact on air quality or overall human health.”

Read that carefully. A reconsideration that loosens monitoring, vapor-tightness or modification triggers while leaving the numeric standards untouched is how stringency erodes in practice without a headline rollback. The FR full text is long (~190 KB); this brief read the summary, dates and the no-impact claim, not every technical appendix.

What to watch: whether industry comments ask to reopen the standards themselves; any state AG intervention; interaction with the still-elevated gasoline and diesel retail prints.

Sources: FR 2026-19774 (primary; full text fetched)
