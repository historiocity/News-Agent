A one-member commission has proposed a framework for the entire American crypto market, and the voting summary says so

US financials AI & tech industry Underreported Primary source

The CFTC has issued an advance notice of proposed rulemaking, “Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions and Regulation Crypto Asset Markets”, RIN 3038-AF80, touching 17 CFR parts 1, 38 and 39. It publishes tomorrow at 91 FR 64811; comments close 14 December 2026. It was issued in Washington on 8 October. The voting summary reads: Chairman Selig voted in the affirmative; no Commissioner voted in the negative. There are no other commissioners. On 9 October this brief reported that the agency is down to one of a statutory five and 551 staff, and wrote that the pending rulemakings were now being written by a one-member commission. This is that, in thirty pages.

The substance is not small. Regulation CTX would say which crypto transactions fall inside CEA section 2(c)(2)(D) — retail commodity transactions on a leveraged, margined or financed basis. An offer counts even if it is not accepted, and may sit in onboarding materials, exchange terms or margin documents. Financing “arranged, endorsed, or materially assisted” through marketing or revenue-sharing can bring a platform in. The escape is actual delivery within 28 days to a wallet the buyer controls, and the Commission would ask who holds the private keys: a book entry where the broker keeps exclusive control is not delivery.

Regulation CAM is the larger half: a purpose-built subcategory of designated contract market for venues listing only crypto, with tailored core principles on listing standards, position limits, trade reporting, execution and operational risk; proof of reserves; mandatory FCM intermediation; leverage only through an FCM or a sponsored qualified banking institution; and open questions on section 4d segregation, part 190 and the Bankruptcy Code, including rehypothecation.

“Crypto asset” means any digital representation of value on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger, excluding payment stablecoins from a permitted issuer under the GENIUS Act.

What to watch: whether any commissioner is nominated before comments close, because an ANPRM issued 1–0 becomes a proposed rule and then a final rule on the same arithmetic; the 14 December comment file, the only adversarial process this rulemaking will get; and whether the SEC’s own Regulation Crypto Assets, proposed 18 August, lines up with this or cuts across it.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. There is no divergence yet, because the document publishes tomorrow and nobody has written about it. The disagreement this brief can already adjudicate is the one inside the document: whether a quorum problem is a legitimacy problem. The CFTC’s own position, implicit in proceeding, is that a commission acts through whoever is seated and the statute sets no floor it is breaching. The contrary case is not about authority but about record: a rulemaking of this size normally produces concurrences and dissents, and those are what a reviewing court reads when the rule is challenged. A 1–0 vote leaves the comment file as the only place an opposing argument can be made, and a comment is not a dissent.

Cause. Mundane and structural, not sinister. Commissioners leave, nominations stall, and an agency with a statutory mandate and a crypto industry waiting does not stop issuing rules while the Senate is busy. The ANPRM form itself is a tell in the agency’s favour: it asks questions rather than proposing text, which is the most procedurally cautious instrument available and buys time for a quorum to be restored before anything binding is written. Against that, note the pacing — the SEC proposed its companion in August, a joint taxonomy was issued in March, and an industry that has waited years is unlikely to accept a pause now.

What it means for you. If you hold crypto on a US exchange with any leverage, margin or financing feature, read the actual-delivery test, because it is the line between a state-regulated spot market and a federal futures market, and it turns on whether you control the private keys. If your platform holds them, the Commission is signalling the transaction may be its business. The second thing worth your attention is the custody question: the notice asks openly whether section 4d segregation and the Bankruptcy Code need modification for crypto held by an FCM, and whether rehypothecation is permissible. That is the FTX question, asked by the regulator, with no answer yet. Anyone with assets on a venue that would become a CAM should want the answer before the rule, not after. The resolving observation is the comment file on 14 December, and specifically whether any bank or clearing house files on segregation.

It has not yet published — it is scheduled for 13 October and was read today through the Federal Register API. Trump announced an energy ceasefire that Moscow has not confirmed and Kyiv had not been told about

Geopolitics Energy & grid US politics & policy Independent corroboration

The President said on Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an energy ceasefire, and told both sides not to break it. What it covers was not stated. A White House official told Axios the proposal is refinery for refinery — attacks on oil refineries only, with power plants and the grid possibly to follow if refinery strikes stop. The same official said Russia was still considering the proposal, which contradicts the announcement. No Kremlin confirmation has been found.

Zelenskyy was reported to be unaware of any agreement, and said Kyiv was prepared to accept a reciprocal energy ceasefire. Russian attacks continued. This follows Saturday, when the President called for Zelenskyy to be replaced and told him to stop striking Russian refineries: “He better damn well stop.”

Note the shape of the week. Friday: OFAC licenses Russian-origin diesel through April 2027, against a statutory import ban nobody has repealed. Saturday: the President calls for the Ukrainian president’s removal. Sunday: he announces a ceasefire one party has not agreed to and the other had not heard of. Each step is presented as a response to the diesel price.

And the diesel price is falling without any of it. AAA diesel is $6.2665, down 1.06 cents — the largest daily fall in a week and a new low. Petrol is $4.3556. The national average is 26.11 cents off the 22 September record, with Saturday’s single-day rise more than reversed.

Ukraine meanwhile struck a Yandex data centre in Vladimir, which the company says is shut down — Russian digital rather than energy infrastructure, in the same twenty-four hours.

What to watch: a Kremlin statement, which is the only thing that would make this an agreement rather than an announcement; whether Ukrainian refinery strikes actually stop, which is observable within days from Russian regional reporting; and 18 October, the Graham Act implementation deadline, now six days away.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A factual dispute with an unusually clean answer: whether an agreement exists. The President says two parties agreed. A White House official, speaking to Axios on the same day, says one of them is still considering it. Ukraine says it was not told but would accept something reciprocal. On the evidence the honest description is a proposal publicly announced as a deal, and the scope is narrower than “energy” implies — refineries only, by the administration’s own account, not power plants or the grid that Russia has been hitting all week.

Cause. The mundane explanation first: announcing a deal is a negotiating tactic, and a public claim that Moscow has agreed raises the cost to Moscow of saying it has not. The administration also has a dated reason to want this resolved before 18 October, when the Graham Act obliges it to act on Russia sanctions. Reach for timing too: the Axios official and the President are describing the same proposal at different stages, hours apart, and an official briefing on background is usually the more accurate of the two because there is no audience to persuade. There is also a precedent that should temper everyone — a 30-day energy truce was announced in March 2025 on the same terms and did not hold.

What it means for you. Do not price anything off this. The thing that would make it real is a Kremlin statement, and the thing that would make it matter is a stop to Ukrainian refinery strikes — which is the actual supply variable, since those strikes are what the President blames for the diesel price. Watch Russian regional reporting of refinery fires rather than any statement from Washington. And note the arithmetic in the paragraph above: the pump price has fallen 26 cents from its record across a week in which the licence, the demand for Zelenskyy’s removal and the ceasefire announcement all happened, and the fall predates all three. If diesel keeps falling, nothing here caused it; if it rises again, nothing here will have prevented it. The one genuinely new fact worth carrying forward is the Yandex strike: whatever is or is not agreed about refineries, Ukraine has just demonstrated it can take down Russian data infrastructure, and no announced ceasefire covers that.

More than one unemployed American in four has now been out of work for over six months

Macro & markets New thread Primary source

From the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ September Employment Situation, released 2 October: unemployment 4.2%, 7.1 million unemployed, nonfarm payrolls +29,000, participation 61.8%, employment-population ratio 59.2%. And the figure the headline rate hides: 1.9 million people have been unemployed for 27 weeks or more — 27.1% of all unemployed.

Put the two together. A 4.2% unemployment rate is historically unremarkable. A long-term share above a quarter is not: it is the signature of a labour market where people who lose a job do not quickly find another, which is a different condition from one where many people are losing jobs. The 29,000 payroll gain against 7.1 million unemployed works out at roughly one new job for every 245 people looking.

The consequence arrives through the benefit system, which is built for short spells. NPR reports that nearly four in ten people now exhaust their unemployment benefits before finding work, and profiles a Colorado worker in a state offering twelve weeks. Twelve weeks against a 27-week-plus share of 27.1% is not a close call: for a quarter of the unemployed, the insurance runs out roughly halfway through the spell.

This is the first time this brief has run the labour market as its own story, and the reason is that the two numbers have separated. The rate is what gets reported and what the Fed is watched on; the duration is what the household experiences. They are now telling different stories, and only one of them is in the headline.

What to watch: the October Employment Situation, due in early November, and specifically whether the 27-week share rises again rather than whether the rate does; weekly continuing claims, which measure the same thing faster; and any state moving to extend benefit duration, which would be the first policy acknowledgement.

Confidence: Primary source for every official figure, read from the BLS release. The benefit-exhaustion share and the Colorado case are from NPR and were not independently verified; the one-job-per-245 arithmetic is this brief’s own. Every passenger on every train crossing the US border would be reported in advance, and the cargo fleet is covered too

US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

Customs and Border Protection has proposed “Advance Traveler Information for Rail”, docket USCBP-2026-0992, RIN 1651-AB60, creating a new subpart K of 19 CFR part 123. It publishes tomorrow at 91 FR 64840; comments close 14 December 2026. Rail carriers would transmit advance electronic information on every individual — passenger and crew — crossing the US border by train, including those in transit.

The data set is a full traveller record: name, date of birth, sex, citizenship, residence, travel document details, status on board, itinerary, US address, seat location and contact details. Carriers would also file a notice of arrival or departure for each train, with route, stops and a 24-hour emergency contact. Timing: the train notice 24 hours before the border, traveller data 60 minutes before departure, a close-out within 30 minutes after.

Two things in the text deserve attention. First, the proposed definition of “train” includes cargo as well as passenger conveyances, and the agency’s own projections are about 3,497 passenger train crossings a year against 70,215 cargo crossings — so a rule titled for travellers reaches a fleet twenty times larger. Second, the enforcement: civil penalties under 19 U.S.C. 1436, criminal penalties for intentional violations, and the conveyance used in a violation is “subject to seizure and forfeiture”. A locomotive is forfeitable for a late manifest.

The economics are small and oddly distributed. Over 2026–2030 at a 3% discount rate CBP projects a net benefit of $36.3m to rail passengers and $1.5m to passenger carriers, against a net cost of $1.7m to cargo carriers. Security benefits are not quantified at all. The one exemption is for active-duty military on orders aboard a Department of War-chartered train.

What to watch: whether Amtrak, VIA Rail and the Class I freight railroads file against the cargo scope, where the cost falls; whether the 60-minute window survives, since it is tight for a train boarding at several stops; and whether the seizure-and-forfeiture language is softened, because it has no proportionality in it.

A company has applied to explore the deep seabed under a 1980 American law, in waters nobody owns

Geopolitics Energy & grid New thread Underreported Primary source

NOAA has announced receipt of an application from Eco Minerals, Inc. for a deep seabed mining exploration licence under the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act, 30 U.S.C. 1426(a)(1). The amended application arrived on 5 December 2025. The notice publishes tomorrow at 91 FR 64880, docket NOAA-NOS-2026-2608, and NOAA has determined the application fully compliant with the Act’s information requirements.

The area is the part that matters: the seabed “beyond national jurisdiction” in the Pacific, in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone and in an area between Guam and Wake Island. No coordinates, no block list and no acreage are given in the notice, and it does not name the minerals or the term sought. The Clarion-Clipperton Zone is the polymetallic nodule province between Hawaii and Mexico that the International Seabed Authority has spent three decades allocating under the Law of the Sea Convention.

That is the story in one sentence: the United States is not a party to the Convention, so it licenses the seabed under its own 1980 statute, and the area it is licensing is the area the Authority administers for everyone else. DSHMRA was written as an interim measure pending a ratification that never came. The notice mentions neither the Convention nor the Authority.

A virtual public hearing is set for 16 November, 3–5pm Eastern, capped at 1,000 attendees, with registration closing 12 November and three minutes per speaker. Written comments close 14 December. NOAA notes that statements in the application are the applicant’s, not the government’s.

What to watch: whether the International Seabed Authority or any state party objects, which would be the first real test of the parallel regime; what the application says the minerals are, which the notice withholds and the docket may not; and whether the hearing fills its thousand seats, because that is the cheapest available measure of whether anyone outside the industry is paying attention.

The Law of the Sea context is this brief’s own and is not in the notice. Sources: NOAA, Deep Seabed Mining: Notice of Receipt of Application, full text, 91 FR 64880

The Gulf crews are back and the oil is not: 8.6% of platforms evacuated, 57.5% of production still shut in

Energy & grid Macro & markets Primary source

BSEE’s Sunday count, as of 11am CDT from 31 company reports: 1,173,863 barrels a day of oil shut in — 57.54% — and 1,051 million cubic feet of gas (49.11%), with 32 of 371 manned platforms still evacuated, one non-dynamically-positioned rig, and no DP rigs off location. The agency will keep updating daily at 1pm CDT.

Hold those two numbers together, because they have come apart. Evacuated platforms have fallen from 129 on Friday to 32 on Sunday — from 34.8% of the Gulf’s manned platforms to 8.6%, a three-quarters reduction. Shut-in oil over the same two days fell only from 71.5% to 57.5%. Crews are back on four platforms in five, and well over half the oil is still off.

That gap is the thing worth watching, and the honest position is that this brief does not know what is in it. The benign reading is ordinary restart sequencing: re-manning is fast, bringing wells and the pipeline and processing chain back is slower, and the gap closes over days. The other reading is that something was found on inspection, offshore or in the onshore infrastructure the platforms feed. Nothing reachable this morning distinguishes them. What can be said is that the standing expectation this brief has carried — that offshore production returns within days while refinery damage lasts weeks — is being tested on its first half.

Four deaths remain attributed to Isaias, two in Escambia County, Florida and two in Alabama. The three refineries named last week — Chevron Pascagoula (356,000 b/d), Vertex at Mobile (88,000), Hunt at Tuscaloosa (50,000) — have still reported no damage.

What to watch: today’s 1pm BSEE count, and specifically whether shut-in oil falls below half while platform evacuations are already under 10%; any operator statement explaining a delayed restart; and the pipeline and processing operators, who report separately and are the likeliest location of the gap.

US financials The overnight circuit breaker gets its reference price moved fifteen minutes earlier, effective on publication.

The SEC has granted summary effectiveness to the Twenty-Ninth Amendment to the LULD Plan — the National Market System Plan to Address Extraordinary Market Volatility. Release 34-106624, File No. 4-631, publishing tomorrow at 91 FR 65016. The change is one number: the Consolidated Price, used with the listing market’s official closing price to set Overnight Price Bands, moves from 7:45pm to 7:30pm Eastern. Nothing else changes.

The reason is operational. Alternative trading systems run overnight sessions from 8pm to 9pm, when the exchanges are closed, and a 7:45pm snapshot leaves fifteen minutes to compute and distribute bands. Moving it to 7:30 gives forty-five. Participants asked for summary effectiveness so the change was certain before the December deadline for systems work.

It is temporary: effective on publication 13 October, for a period “not to exceed February 10, 2027”, with operative date expected 6 December. Comments close 3 November. The Commission identifies no risk.

Below the line because it is a fifteen-minute timing change to a mechanism that has not yet been tested in anger. It is here because this brief opened the 23-hour trading thread on 8 October from nine exchange filings asking what happens when the market halts at 3am, and this is the first piece of that machinery actually being set. It leads at the first overnight halt.

Science & public health After four years, the FDA will let salt substitutes into foods that legally required salt.

The FDA is issuing a final rule amending its standard of identity regulations — the rules that define what a food must contain to be sold under a given name — to permit salt substitutes in standardised foods where salt (sodium chloride) is currently a required or optional ingredient. Docket FDA-2022-N-2226; publishing tomorrow at 91 FR 65040; effective 14 December 2026.

The stated purpose is sodium reduction: less sodium may reduce the risk of hypertension, a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke. The agency frames it as flexibility for industry to reformulate “while maintaining the basic nature and essential characteristics of the foods”.

The mechanism is more interesting than the health claim. Standards of identity are among the oldest and most rigid parts of American food law, written to stop adulteration by specifying recipes — and that rigidity is what has blocked reformulation: a manufacturer removing salt from a standardised food could not legally still call it that food. This rule removes the obstacle for one ingredient class. It requires nothing.

Below the line because it is permissive rather than mandatory — nothing changes until manufacturers choose to reformulate — and the abstract rather than the rule text was read here. It leads if a major category actually reformulates, or if a petition follows for other ingredients.

US financials Four of the big railroads were found revenue adequate for 2025, which is the finding that governs how much they can charge.

The Surface Transportation Board served a decision on 8 October announcing the 2025 revenue adequacy determinations for the nation’s Class I railroads. Four were found revenue adequate: BNSF, CSX, Norfolk Southern and Union Pacific. Docket EP 552 (Sub-No. 30); the notice publishes tomorrow at 91 FR 65025.

Revenue adequacy is not a formality. A railroad is revenue adequate when its return on investment at least equals the industry cost of capital, and the finding is the statutory hinge in rate regulation: it bears on what a shipper must show to challenge a rate as unreasonable, and on how the Board treats differential pricing on captive traffic. Four of the large US railroads clearing that bar in the same year is the condition under which shipper complaints become most arguable.

The determination is backward-looking and annual, and the Board’s methodology has been contested for years by both shippers and carriers for opposite reasons.

Below the line because it is an annual accounting determination with no immediate operative effect, and the decision itself — the one with the figures and the cost-of-capital calculation — was not read here, only the notice abstract. It leads at the first rate case that turns on it.

Confidence: Primary source for the abstract and the names; the 8 October decision was not read, so no return figures or cost-of-capital number is reported. Sources: STB, Railroad Revenue Adequacy—2025 Determination, 91 FR 65025
