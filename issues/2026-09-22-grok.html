Negative check: the FDIC proposes to remake how bank mergers get approved

In today’s Federal Register the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking (RIN 3064-AG18; FR Doc. 2026-19308) that would “fundamentally reform” the FDIC’s processing and evaluation of merger transactions under the Bank Merger Act. Comments close 23 November 2026.

Notable mechanics in the proposal: (1) a new de minimis category with a letter filing and “deemed approval” on a five-business-day clock unless the Attorney General objects on competition grounds; (2) express inclusion of credit-union shares and centrally booked deposits in the initial Herfindahl-Hirschman Index competitive screen; (3) an HHI safe harbor (post-merger HHI ≤1,800, or increase <200 if above 1,800, or corporate reorganizations); (4) mandatory completeness determination within 21 days and tailored decision clocks of 90 or 150 days (extendable to 180/270); (5) a quantitative “merger in substance” definition at 80% of assets over a rolling 12 months; and (6) a new notice/non-objection track for “significant asset transfers” (≥25% asset growth over 12 months that is not itself a BMA merger).

The preamble is explicit about the policy direction: faster, more predictable, less stringency than the current framework, less weight on supervisory ratings alone, and rarer removal from expedited processing for CRA protests. It would also rescind the current Statement of Policy concurrent with a final rule.

What to watch: the comment file from large banks, community-bank trade groups, and DOJ Antitrust; whether the OCC and Federal Reserve Board publish companion frameworks; any congressional reaction to “deemed approval.”

Divergence — speed vs concentration

What is disputed. Whether modernising the BMA screen for fintech/credit-union competition and hard clocks is overdue housekeeping, or a structural tilt toward consolidation.

Why they differ. Credit-union shares in the HHI raise measured competition in rural markets; de minimis deemed approval and longer asset thresholds for expedited processing cut friction for serial acquirers. Both can be true about different deal sizes.

What it means for you. Core US financials. Came from the standing FR RULE/PRORULE sweep — not a beat tip. If finalised, the competitive map and the processing clock both change.

Dig deeper — what “deemed approval” actually buys

De minimis is not a free pass for megadeals. The proposal ties it to assets below the Hart-Scott-Rodino lower threshold and under 5% of the acquirer’s assets (or certain roll-up corporate reorganizations of operating subsidiaries with unchanged legal/financial risk), plus composite/CRA/compliance/capital screens. The load-bearing change for larger deals is the combination of credit-union HHI inclusion, the safe harbor, and the 90/150-day clocks with limited CRA-protest removal — that is the architecture of predictability, not the five-day letter track.

The SEC opens a five-year, conditioned window for tokenized NMS stocks on permissioned AMM venues

Also in today’s Federal Register: an SEC order (Release No. 34-106402; File No. 4-927; FR Doc. 2026-19388), dated 17 September and published 22 Sep, granting temporary conditional exemptive relief under Exchange Act §36(a)(1). Tokenized Securities Venues (TSVs) that provide permissioned automated-market-maker liquidity pools for tokenized NMS stocks get a temporary exemption from the statutory “exchange” definition; certain proprietary liquidity providers get a temporary exemption from the “dealer” definition. The order expires five years after publication and requests comment on modifications.

Scope is tight on purpose: permissioned (not open DeFi) trading; Tokenized NMS Stock paired with another Tokenized NMS Stock, a non-security crypto asset (e.g. a GENIUS Act payment stablecoin), or a tokenized money market fund; volume/symbol caps by LULD tier (Tier 1: ≤75 symbols and 0.25% of prior-month ADV; Tier 2: ≤250 symbols and 2.5% ADV); issuer notice and objection rights for third-party tokenization; U.S.-person TSV requirement; public Notice; books-and-records; stoppage of trading when the listing exchange stops the underlying; no leverage/financing by the TSV. The order sits inside Chairman Atkins’s “Project Crypto” and the January 2025 digital-asset executive-order line — and it is self-consciously interim while the Commission considers rulemaking.

What to watch: first TSV Notices and which issuers object; whether volume caps bind; any FINRA/broker-dealer friction when members trade through TSVs; companion Investment Company Act issues the order expressly does not resolve.

Divergence — sandbox vs fragmentation

What is disputed. Whether a five-year exchange/dealer carve-out for AMM pricing (which generally cannot meet Reg NMS trade-through/quotation rules without gutting the model) is necessary innovation policy, or a supervised fracture of the national market system.

Why they differ. AMMs set prices from pool ratios, not external NBBO. The Commission’s answer is volume caps and time limits while it learns. Critics will say any meaningful TSV volume is already a two-track equity market.

What it means for you. Core financials with a crypto/tech channel. Primary FR notice — not on yesterday’s Grok menu.

Dig deeper — what the volume caps are for

The order is explicit that AMM prices can dislocate from traditional NMS prints because they do not directly consider external pricing. Caps and the issuer-objection right are the investor-protection half of a relief package that also drops Reg NMS obligations for qualifying TSVs. Read the two together: the Commission is allowing the experiment and simultaneously bounding its footprint in the SIP-reported market.

FDIC State Bank Parity: when host-state law does not bind national banks, it should not bind out-of-state State banks either

Companion NPR in today’s FR (RIN 3064-AG34; FR Doc. 2026-19310): the FDIC proposes to amend 12 CFR part 331 so that, for purposes of FDI Act §24(j) (Riegle-Neal), host-state laws apply to any branch or any services provided in the host state by an out-of-state State bank to the same extent those laws apply to an out-of-state national bank. Comments also close 23 November 2026.

The catalyst named in the preamble is Illinois’s Interchange Fee Prohibition Act (IFPA) and the Northern District of Illinois litigation (Ill. Bankers Ass’n v. Raoul). The court enjoined enforcement against national banks and against out-of-state State banks “subject to Riegle-Neal,” but the Illinois AG argued §24(j) reaches only branches physically in Illinois — leaving State banks that serve Illinois customers online without a branch in legal limbo. The FDIC estimates ~3,185 State banks potentially affected once IFPA’s current July 2027 effective date arrives, with ~$308 million in one-time compliance-system costs avoided if parity holds. Eleven other states are described as pursuing similar legislation.

The proposal expressly does not change interest-rate exportation under FDI Act §27, and does not itself decide which particular host-state laws are preempted.

What to watch: IFPA effective-date politics in Springfield; copycat state bills; whether the rule finalises before July 2027.

Divergence — dual banking vs state consumer experiments

What is disputed. Whether §24(j) was always about functional parity in a mobile-banking world, or whether Congress tied the shield to physical branches and left online State banks exposed.

Why they differ. National banks already have a clearer preemption path (OCC IFR + injunction). Without parity, State banks face convert-or-comply pressure — exactly the dual-banking imbalance the FDIC says it is curing.

What it means for you. Core financials + policy. Same day’s FR as the merger remake — package them as a bank-regulatory morning, not two unrelated filings.

Sources: FR 2026-19310 full text (primary)

British Columbia sues OpenAI in California over the Tumbler Ridge school shooting

British Columbia Attorney General Niki Sharma stated Monday that the province has filed suit in California against OpenAI for alleged failure to notify law enforcement of threats made on its platform before the 10 February 2026 mass shooting at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School. The province frames the case as corporate accountability for credible violence threats on an AI platform, seeks answers for families and School District 59, and pairs the filing with letters to Canadian federal ministers proposing Criminal Code amendments for human accountability when AI is implicated. Sharma says OpenAI “has not taken meaningful steps” to address survivor and community concerns.

US channel: the venue is California federal process against a US company and CEO; the liability theory (duty to warn / failure to escalate flagged threats) is the same class of claim US state AGs and plaintiffs will watch as a template. Altitude bar cleared — this is not foreign colour.

What to watch: the complaint’s operative counts once docketed and readable; OpenAI’s response; whether US state AGs file parallel actions; any product-safety or reporting commitment from the lab.

Divergence — platform duty vs speech intermediary

What is disputed. Whether a frontier chatbot provider that flags credible gun-violence content owes an affirmative law-enforcement notice duty, or whether that collapses into a general monitoring obligation the US has historically resisted.

Why they differ. BC analogises to tobacco/opioid public-nuisance suits. OpenAI will likely argue scale, false-positive risk, and First Amendment / Section 230-adjacent theories even in a Canadian-plaintiff California filing.

What it means for you. Core AI + liability. Primary provincial statement fetched. Not on yesterday’s Grok menu.
