Payrolls rose 165,000 against a 59,000 forecast, while the number out of work for six months or more reached 2 million

Macro & markets New subject Primary source

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said Friday that nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 in October. The average monthly gain over the prior twelve months was 48,000. August was revised up to 186,000 from 133,000 and September to 60,000 from 29,000, so the two months together are 84,000 higher than first reported. Unemployment was 4.1%, which BLS describes as "changed little". It has stayed between 4.1% and 4.3% since March. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month to $38.00 and are up 3.1% on the year. Most of the hiring came in professional and business services (+70,000), health care (+42,000), transportation and warehousing (+25,000) and manufacturing (+19,000).

The household survey looks weaker. The number of long-term unemployed, people out of work for 27 weeks or more, held at 2.0 million, or 27.6% of everyone unemployed. People working part time because they could not get full-time hours rose 339,000 to 4.8 million.

CNBC put the Dow Jones consensus at 59,000, and the consensus expected unemployment to stay at 4.2%. On Treasury's own curve the 2-year yield rose from 4.46% to 4.51% on the day and the 10-year from 4.84% to 4.86%. Semafor reports that CME FedWatch odds of a December rate rise jumped 12 points after the release. The Fed held at 3.75–4.00% last week, as this brief reported.

What to watch: the November jobs report on 4 December, and the inflation prints before the December decision.

Divergence — a hot labour market or a stuck one?

What is disputed. This is a dispute about significance. The facts are not in question. Semafor reads the release as strengthening the case for a December rate rise. CNBC leads with a "disturbing" trend: the average spell of unemployment now exceeds 27 weeks, which it says is the longest outside the pandemic since 2016, and the long-term share is the highest since late 2021.

Why they differ. They are reading different surveys from the same release. Semafor's market piece is built on the establishment survey of employers, which drives payrolls, revisions and wages. CNBC's labour feature is built on the household survey and quotes an Indeed economist describing a market where employers neither hire nor fire much. Both readings are accurate. They answer different questions: what the Fed will do, and how a person who loses a job will fare.

What it means for you. For rates, the establishment numbers carry the weight. A 165,000 print, 84,000 of upward revisions and 0.4% wage growth push the Fed toward a rise, and the 2-year yield moved accordingly. The household weakness changes slowly and will not move a December decision on its own. Treat this as a hawkish print. The December 4 report settles it: if payrolls hold up and the long-term share keeps climbing, both readings are right at once.

Dig deeper — why the revisions matter more than the headline

September was first reported at 29,000, a number that suggested the labour market was stalling. It is now 60,000. Add August's upward revision and two months that looked weak look ordinary, which changes the story the Fed has been telling itself since the summer. The prior twelve-month average of 48,000 is the useful baseline. October's print is more than three times that, and the gains are broad: business services, health care, couriers and factories. They are not concentrated in government or one seasonal line.

The wage number matters for the same reason. A 0.4% monthly rise is an annualised pace near 5%, well above the 3.1% year-on-year figure. That is the kind of reading that keeps services inflation sticky while oil is still elevated by the Iran war.

Against that, the 339,000 rise in involuntary part-time work is large for one month. The household survey is noisy, and BLS itself calls most of its October changes "little". Since benefits run out after 26 weeks in most states, a long-term jobless count that rises while initial claims stay low is consistent with CNBC's argument. People are not being laid off in large numbers. Those who are laid off are not being rehired.

The forecasts also differed: Dow Jones had 59,000, and the Reuters poll cited in this brief on 5 November had 65,000. Against either forecast the miss was about 100,000, which is why the 2-year yield moved.

Treasury and the Fed withdrew the 2020 plan to cut the cross-border wire-records threshold from $3,000 to $250, crypto included

US financials New subject Primary source Underreported

A notice on the Federal Register's public-inspection list, to be published Monday 9 November, says FinCEN and the Federal Reserve Board are withdrawing their joint proposal of 27 October 2020 (85 FR 68005). The proposal would have cut the threshold in the Bank Secrecy Act's Recordkeeping Rule and Travel Rule from $3,000 to $250 for transfers that begin or end outside the United States. It would also have stated that both rules cover transactions in convertible virtual currency and in digital assets with legal tender status. The agencies say they "will not take further action" on it.

The notice gives two reasons. More than five years have passed, so the agencies want fresh public comment before adopting anything similar. And crypto markets have changed, including through the GENIUS Act stablecoin statute of July 2025. The notice adds that the withdrawal tells the public no final rule will come without a new chance to comment. It summarises nearly 3,000 comments, most of them form letters. Banks and money transmitters said $250 would be costly and would push remittances into less-regulated channels. Crypto firms opposed the virtual-currency clarification, while some banks and money transmitters supported it.

The practical effect: the $3,000 line set in 1995 stands, and there is no stated federal rule on how the Travel Rule applies to crypto. That comes in the same week OFAC sanctioned eight Canadian money-services firms for moving Iranian funds partly through crypto (see Also today).

What to watch: whether a new proposal follows, and whether it raises the threshold for inflation, as some commenters asked.

Dig deeper — what changes, and what does not

Nothing changes in day-to-day compliance. The $3,000 thresholds in 31 CFR 1020.410(a) and 1010.410(e) and (f), and in the Fed's Regulation S, stay as they are. What changes is the outlook. For six years banks, money transmitters and exchanges have had to plan around a possible twelvefold cut in the threshold for cross-border transfers. That possibility is now formally gone. The notice also describes a split in which crypto service providers opposed applying the rules to virtual currency, while some depository institutions and money transmitters supported it. Banks have long wanted competing crypto rails held to the same record-keeping rules they follow. This withdrawal leaves that question open.

The notice ties the decision to the stablecoin statute and to a White House digital-assets report, and says future changes should be fit for purpose. In practice, any Travel Rule for crypto will now be written alongside the stablecoin regime rather than imported from wire transfers. The tension worth noting is that enforcement and rulemaking are moving in opposite directions. In the same week, OFAC designated Canadian exchanges with listed TRON addresses for laundering Iranian money, while the record-keeping rule that would have reached smaller crypto transfers was withdrawn.

I found no news coverage of the withdrawal in the sources checked. It is on the public-inspection list ahead of Monday's publication. The marker is Primary source because the account above is the agencies' own document and nothing else.

Friday's "minor" strikes on Qeshm, a seafarer killed on a tanker, and Brent near $98 after a double-digit weekly fall

Geopolitics Energy — one causal chain Follow-up Independent corroboration

Trump said on Friday that US forces had carried out "minor" strikes on Iran, destroying in a few minutes what he called an anti-aircraft device (AP, via NBC). Iranian state media reported two explosions in the Suza area of Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz. NBC adds reports of explosions on Hengam and around Bandar Abbas. These were the first strikes since Tuesday's midterms. The US also struck Qeshm on Monday (CNBC). A day earlier, Thursday, the Marshall Islands-flagged products tanker Velos Sapphire was hit by what its Greek manager believes was a missile while sailing unladen toward Bahrain. One seafarer was killed (Reuters, via gCaptain).

Prices are not reflecting the attacks. Trading Economics has Brent at $97.76 on 6 November, down 0.54% on the day and more than 12.5% on the week. Semafor says it briefly neared $100 on Friday. AAA's national average for regular gasoline is $4.037 this morning, down 12 cents on the week. FGE's Fereidun Fesharaki told Bloomberg that Iran's exports are close to zero under the US blockade, with only 200,000–300,000 barrels a day leaving via Iraq. He said a deal is unlikely and fresh strikes likely. On diplomacy, the Guardian reports a Qatari plan under which Iran would admit UN inspectors to its bombed nuclear sites and reopen Hormuz on the second day of implementation. One more risk has surfaced: the FT, via Ars Technica, reports Windward tracking three sanctioned tankers and an Iranian fishing boat near Hormuz subsea cables in October, after Iranian threats against the cables.

What to watch: whether Iran answers the Qatari plan, EIA's weekly retail prices on Monday, and whether insurers reprice Gulf transits after the Velos Sapphire death.

Divergence — what was hit, and how far oil fell

What is disputed. Two factual points. First, the target: Trump says the US destroyed an Iranian anti-aircraft system. Iranian TV, quoted by Al Jazeera, says Iran's air defences shot down a US MQ-9 drone over Qeshm. CNBC adds that Trump never explicitly said his remarks referred to Qeshm. Second, the size of the oil move: Trading Economics has Brent down more than 12.5% on the week, while Bloomberg's FGE piece says about 15%.

Why they differ. On the strike, each government's account serves its own purpose. Washington calls it minor and defensive, which suits a White House that wants a deal. Tehran claims a shootdown, which suits a government under blockade. The two claims can both be true: a drone could be lost in a strike on an air-defence site. CENTCOM's only comment was that the situation was "relatively calm". On oil, the explanation is timing and measurement. Bloomberg's piece was published during Friday's session, and Trading Economics prices a CFD, not the settlement.

What it means for you. The US did strike again, and Iran has not shown a downed MQ-9. Treat the drone claim as unconfirmed until there is wreckage or a US acknowledgement. For prices, take "down 12–15% on the week, near $98" as the honest range. The pump is following crude down with the usual lag. The real risk is the shipping attacks, not the strikes. A dead crew member on a tanker in the Gulf is what moves war-risk premiums.

Dig deeper — the chain from Qeshm to the pump

Qeshm sits in the narrow part of the strait opposite Bandar Abbas, which is why each strike there gets read as a Hormuz event. The supply facts have not changed. Iran's own exports are close to zero under the US blockade, per FGE, so another strike on Iranian air defences removes no barrels. What moves the price is the risk to everyone else's barrels. The Velos Sapphire was unladen and heading for Bahrain, so it was not carrying Iranian oil. That a non-Iranian products tanker could be hit and a crew member killed is the cost that shows up in freight and insurance.

The diplomatic track is more detailed than it was a week ago. The Guardian's account of the Qatari plan says Iran would not have to commit to sending its enriched-uranium stockpile abroad, with other options under discussion, and that Hormuz would open on day two. CNBC quotes Trump saying Iran wants a deal "very badly". FGE thinks Tehran now sees a weakened president and will hold out. These are predictions, not facts. The settling observation is whether inspectors enter.

The subsea-cable report adds a risk that has nothing to do with oil. Six active cables cross the strait, and Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar depend entirely on them, according to Windward via the FT. One corridor the loitering ships visited is used by 2Africa, a cable whose consortium includes Meta. A cable cut would be a data-centre and connectivity problem across the Gulf, not an energy one.

Bessent and Greer told Beijing to deliver on Busan, while 600,000 Nvidia chips wait in Hong Kong for Chinese approval

World economy — US channel AI & tech New subject Primary source

On Friday morning, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer held a videoconference with Vice Premier He Lifeng. USTR's readout says Greer stressed "concrete actions" by China on agricultural purchases, rare earths and other critical minerals. Treasury's readout says Bessent "raised concerns about recent Chinese actions" and that the US will closely assess whether China is implementing the deal. According to Reuters, carried by Nikkei Asia, the Busan arrangement expires on 10 January. It caps Trump-era tariffs on Chinese goods at about 20%, and China pledged 25 million tonnes of soybeans a year for three years plus $17 billion more in farm purchases. Al Jazeera reports that China's Commerce Ministry said on Thursday it would buy more US farm goods only if the two sides carry out their "30 billion for 30 billion" reciprocal tariff cut. Trump and Xi are expected to meet at APEC in Shenzhen on 18–19 November and at the G20 in Miami on 14–15 December.

Chips are a second point of leverage. Reuters, citing two people, reports that about 600,000 Nvidia H200s are sitting in Hong Kong while Beijing withholds approval for large-scale imports. Several Chinese internet companies have each received about 10,000. An eight-card H200 server was offered in China for more than 6 million yuan (about $900,000) at the end of October, roughly triple its price a year earlier. Separately, the chairs of the House China, Foreign Affairs and Intelligence committees have asked Jensen Huang to a closed briefing on 18 November, the day Shenzhen opens. The request follows a New York Times report that US intelligence believes he was targeted by a Chinese spying operation (Ars Technica). Nvidia rejects the claims.

What to watch: whether Shenzhen produces the tariff-cut implementation China says it is waiting for, and whether Huang attends on the 18th.

Divergence — complaint or accusation, and how big the tariff package is

What is disputed. First, how to characterise the call, which is a dispute of significance. Handelsblatt headlines it as Washington accusing China of breaching the deal. DW and Reuters say Washington "urged" or "warned". Second, an apparent conflict over the size of the tariff package: Reuters describes the September proposal as a $60 billion package, while China's ministry calls it "30 billion for 30 billion".

Why they differ. The tariff figures agree once you add them up: $30 billion of cuts on each side makes $60 billion in total. Reuters gives the sum and Beijing gives the reciprocity. On tone, the US readouts never use the word "violation". Handelsblatt, writing for a German business audience that watches whether the trade truce holds, translates Bessent's "concerns about recent Chinese actions" as an accusation. That is a fair reading of the subtext, but it is stronger than the text.

What it means for you. Washington is complaining on the record ahead of Shenzhen without saying the deal is broken. That leaves room to declare success there. China's position is plain: farm purchases depend on tariff cuts. The deal expires on 10 January. A dated tariff-cut implementation, or a rare-earth licensing announcement, would show the truce holding. Silence from Shenzhen would mean the 10 January expiry becomes the real deadline.

Dig deeper — why the chip stockpile belongs in a trade story

The H200 figure rests on a single source. Reuters reported it exclusively, and DCD, Semafor, CNA and The Next Web are all rewrites. The marker on this story reflects the primary readouts, so read the chip number as Reuters' alone. Reuters also cites an earlier report from The Information that Beijing told Alibaba, ByteDance and DeepSeek that total approvals might come to fewer than 200,000 chips, and that it wants Chinese processors used for inference. If that cap holds, most of the Hong Kong stockpile is never going to clear.

For the US this is the reverse of the usual export-control story. Washington licensed the H200 for China, with a 25% export levy according to DCD. Beijing is the side holding back, to limit dependence on US technology. That gives China something to trade in Shenzhen at no cost, because approving imports it already wants to allow can be presented as a concession. For Nvidia shareholders, a widely shared industry summary says the company booked a $0.4 billion charge for excess H200 inventory in its quarter to 26 July. I did not read the 10-Q itself.

The Huang request is a separate matter but lands on the same date. The committees' letter, as quoted by Ars, frames it around PRC access to sensitive US technology. It centres on a Megaspeed executive. Megaspeed is a Singapore cloud company that has bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips.

Justice launched a "Super Intelligence" Task Force that absorbs its AI litigation unit and will advise against "burdensome regulation"

AI & tech — policy Follow-up Primary source

The Justice Department announced the DOJ Super Intelligence Task Force (DOJ-SITF) on Thursday. The founding memo, signed by Attorney General Todd Blanche on 13 October and published with the announcement, cites Executive Order 14434, "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence" (29 September). It names Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward and Associate Deputy Attorney General Stephen Weiss as co-chairs.

The memo gives the task force six jobs. It will organise DOJ's criminal and civil enforcement involving super intelligence, settle conflicts between cases, recommend enforcement priorities, and act as DOJ's point of contact with the presidential Super Intelligence Force, which Jay Clayton chairs. It also absorbs the AI task force Attorney General Bondi announced on 9 January, the preemption-litigation unit this brief has tracked as a thread, and takes over DOJ's affirmative civil AI litigation. Through DOJ's legal-policy and legislative offices it will advise on proposals that promote innovation "without burdensome regulation". Components and US attorneys may be asked to notify the task force before opening investigations or bringing charges in super-intelligence matters. Members come from the Antitrust, Civil, Criminal and National Security divisions, the FBI and the Solicitor General's office.

This creates one office inside DOJ that coordinates AI prosecutions, suits against state AI laws and legislative advice. The Register reports that Pentagon CTO Emil Michael told a WSJ conference that details on the AI regulatory landscape will come later this month.

What to watch: that regulatory-landscape document, and the first state AI law the task force challenges.

Divergence — who runs it

What is disputed. A factual point. The Register reports the task force is "headed up by" Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton alongside Woodward and Weiss. DOJ's release and memo name only Woodward and Weiss, as designees of the Attorney General, and describe Clayton as chair of the separate presidential Super Intelligence Force. Nextgov and Akin's tracker match DOJ.

Why they differ. The explanation is ordinary: there are two bodies with nearly identical names, the presidential "SI Force" and the DOJ "SI Task Force", and the release mentions Clayton in the same paragraph as the co-chairs. The Register also abbreviates it "SINTF", which appears nowhere in DOJ's documents.

What it means for you. Go with the primary documents. DOJ-SITF is run by DOJ officials and liaises with Clayton's body. The difference matters: a task force chaired by an intelligence official would sit outside DOJ's prosecutorial chain, and this one sits inside it. The memo settles the question. It is the controlling text.

Four states sued to avoid running Medicaid work requirements from 1 January, saying the funding threat is unconstitutional coercion

US politics & policy New subject Primary source

Oregon, Minnesota, New Mexico and Washington filed Oregon v. Oz (No. 6:26-cv-2435, D. Or., Eugene Division) on Thursday. The defendants are CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and their agencies. The suit targets the work requirement that H.R. 1 imposes on the Affordable Care Act expansion population, which starts on 1 January 2027. The complaint argues the law turns Medicaid from a health programme into a vehicle for encouraging work, and that Congress did this by amendment instead of repealing the expansion. It argues that the penalty for refusing, the loss of all federal Medicaid funding, is coercion that exceeds the spending power, relying on the Supreme Court's 2012 decision. Bloomberg Law reports the states want an injunction barring the government from withholding funds if they decline to implement the requirement.

The numbers show what is at stake. In Minnesota, 1.2 million people are on Medicaid and 243,000 would have to meet the requirement, according to MPR News. That means showing 80 hours a month of work, school or volunteering, or qualifying for an exemption. The exemptions in the statute include the medically frail, totally disabled veterans and parents of children 13 and under.

STAT reports that the suit comes less than a week after a federal judge, in a separate case, found that implementation would cause irreparable harm. It notes the suit could buy time for the new Democratic Congress, which takes office in January, to change the law.

What to watch: a motion for a preliminary injunction and its hearing date. Any order has to arrive before 1 January to matter.

Divergence — who the requirement covers

What is disputed. A factual point. MPR News says Minnesotans aged 21 to 64 who apply will have to show 80 hours a month. The complaint, quoting the statute, defines the people covered as expansion enrollees aged 19 to 64, minus the exempt groups.

Why they differ. The likely explanation is a reporting error or a state-specific detail that was compressed. MPR is working from the Minnesota attorney general's briefing, while the complaint cites 42 U.S.C. §1396a(xx)(9) directly. Nothing in the material I read suggests Minnesota has a different age floor, and the federal requirement sets the minimum.

What it means for you. Use the statute: 19 to 64. That adds two years of young adults to the count, so Minnesota's 243,000 figure may itself be computed on a different basis. CMS's implementing guidance, or Minnesota's own eligibility notice, would settle it.

CDC counts 5,182 measles cases this year, the most since 1991, and a ruling on US elimination status is due next week

Science & public health New subject Independent corroboration

CDC data show 5,182 confirmed measles cases in the US so far in 2026, the highest annual total since 1991. That is more than double 2025's 2,289 (Al Jazeera, and AFP via France 24). Of these, 4,993 are linked to outbreaks. Children under five make up 18% of cases and those aged five to 19 another 41%. Kindergarten vaccination coverage fell from 95.2% in the 2019–20 school year to 92.4% in 2025–26.

Deaths are rising faster than the national count shows. Wisconsin has reported a baby's death from measles, the state's first in more than 35 years. It was the third measles death reported in the US this week, after a New York child on Monday and an unvaccinated person in Jefferson County, Pennsylvania, on Wednesday (Ars Technica). Ars also reports that experts will decide next week whether the US has lost its measles elimination status.

NBC's headline calls losing elimination status a "huge embarrassment to public health". It is also the clearest scorecard so far for the past year's changes to federal vaccine policy, which this brief has tracked as a thread.

What to watch: next week's elimination decision, and whether CDC's death count catches up with the states'.

Divergence — four deaths or eight?

What is disputed. A factual dispute about the death toll. Al Jazeera reports that CDC counts four measles deaths in 2026. AFP, via France 24, reports that state health agencies count at least eight. Al Jazeera itself says CDC's total runs below the states', particularly Pennsylvania's.

Why they differ. The most likely reason is the reporting process. CDC adds a death once a state's case report is confirmed and transmitted, which takes days or weeks. States announce on their own schedule. Three deaths were announced this week alone, so a federal count published around the same time would trail. There is no sign of deliberate undercounting in the material I read, though Al Jazeera's wording leaves that open.

What it means for you. Treat at least eight as the floor and CDC's four as lagging. A CDC update that rises to match the states would settle it. If it does not rise after the states' reports are filed, that would be a story in itself. One more point: Ars's "first death in over 35 years" refers to Wisconsin, not the country.

The rail regulator restated how far federal law overrides state and local limits on rail-served projects

US politics & policy — regulation New subject Primary source Underreported

The Surface Transportation Board has issued a policy statement in Docket EP 791 on its jurisdiction and on preemption under the ICC Termination Act. It is on the Federal Register's public-inspection list and becomes final on 9 November. It answers a 19 September 2025 letter from six railroad and shipper groups asking for guidance. Board members Fuchs, Hedlund and Schultz replied five days later that a statement was coming. The Board says its precedents are "generally consistent" but scattered, use different terms and are hard for the public to follow. It calls the statement non-exhaustive and says it makes no binding determinations.

FreightWaves highlights two substantive points. First, a modified test for when local rules aimed at a railroad's customer, such as a transload or off-loading facility, are really regulating rail service. The test asks whether the facility exists to receive or tender common-carrier rail service, and whether the government action restricts or dictates that service. Second, an argument that any government or private effort to take exclusive possession of a railroad right-of-way is categorically preempted, with no need for a case-by-case look at the effect on operations.

This affects every county fighting a rail-served terminal or industrial project. Under this framework, a zoning denial aimed at a facility's rail use is easier to challenge before the Board.

What to watch: the first local dispute decided under the new framework, and whether states challenge the categorical rule on rights-of-way.

Divergence — a restatement or a widening?

What is disputed. A dispute about significance. FreightWaves calls the statement "sweeping" and says it signals broader protection for rail-served projects. The Board presents it as a consolidation of long-standing precedent with no binding effect.

Why they differ. Both describe the same text. The Board has a reason to call its guidance continuous with past rulings, because that is what makes it hard to attack in court. A trade outlet writing for shippers and railroads looks at where the framework departs from earlier decisions, and FreightWaves notes the statement questions parts of past rulings on privately owned terminals. The departures are real but narrow.

What it means for you. FreightWaves has the better reading of the effect and the Board has the better reading of the legal form. Nothing binds anyone today. But a statement that frames the analysis in railroads' favour will be cited in every dispute that follows. A Board decision applying it to a contested local denial will show whether it widens preemption in practice.

Dig deeper — why a non-binding statement still matters

Preemption fights under the ICC Termination Act usually turn on one question: is the local government regulating "transportation by rail carrier", or a nearby business that happens to use rail? Under past decisions, a customer-owned terminal often fell outside federal protection, so the county's permit rules applied. The new two-question test moves attention to the rail service itself. If a facility exists to send and receive rail cars, and the local action limits volume, timing or access, the Board now signals the action may be preempted even though the railroad does not own the facility.

The rights-of-way argument is the bigger change. Eminent-domain takings of rail corridors for trails, roads or development have normally been judged by how much they interfere with operations. Treating exclusive-possession takings as categorically preempted would remove that balancing step. That matters to states and cities trying to acquire unused or lightly used corridors.

I found the statement through the public-inspection sweep and corroborated it only against FreightWaves. It runs about 25,000 words, and I read its summary, background and the sections FreightWaves highlights, not every page.
