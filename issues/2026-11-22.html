Federal contractors have been told to clear their answers to Congress with the White House first

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

An executive order signed on Friday requires federal contractors to notify the White House of any congressional request for information about their work and to coordinate their response with the administration. It tells them they must “abide by any instructions received from the White House and contracting agency related to the withholding of communications that the White House or the agency has determined are protected by executive privilege”, and warns that their contracts may be terminated if they do not comply.

The order gives its own reason, and it is worth reading as written: “If the executive branch cannot be assured that those communications will remain confidential, agencies and the White House will be chilled from sharing sensitive information with contractors, which will stymie the operation of critical Government programs.” It also accuses unnamed congressional leaders of “harassing private companies that contract with the federal government” and reminds them that an inquiry must have a “valid legislative purpose”.

The timing is six weeks before the committee gavels change. Rep. Jamie Raskin, in line to chair House Judiciary, called the order “unprecedented and blatantly unlawful”: “Federal contractors are paid with taxpayer dollars, not Trump's personal billions. Public contractors have both a duty and a right to speak truthfully when Congress calls.” Senator Richard Blumenthal, who will chair a Senate investigative panel, wrote to a set of companies earlier this month asking them to cooperate with a planned inquiry into the White House ballroom, having previously asked the builder about the anticipated cost and how it was chosen. Democrats have also signalled inquiries into the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool repairs, the Kennedy Center renovation, Homeland Security's purchases of aircraft and its contracts for ICE deportation operations and border wall construction in Big Bend National Park.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is a privilege-protection measure or an obstruction instrument. The order's position is that executive privilege attaches to executive-branch communications wherever they sit, including inside a contractor, and that the government may instruct its own contractors how to handle them. Raskin's position is that a private company paid with public money has an independent duty to answer Congress truthfully, which no executive order can override. A dispute about law, and it will be settled in court rather than argued to a conclusion.

Cause. There is a real legal question underneath, and it is worth separating from the politics. Executive privilege is genuinely held by the executive, not by the contractor, and a contractor handed privileged material does not acquire the right to waive it — that much is uncontroversial. What is new is the machinery: advance notification of every congressional request, coordination of the response, and termination as the sanction. Those are not privilege assertions; they are a reporting line. And a privilege claim is ordinarily made document by document, to the committee, by the executive. This order moves the decision upstream and makes the contractor's commercial survival the enforcement mechanism. Reach for the privilege rationale before assuming bad faith — but note that the order itself does not stop at privileged material: it requires notification of any congressional request.

What it means for you. If a contractor complies and Congress subpoenas anyway, the contractor is caught between a subpoena and a termination clause, and that is the position the order creates. Believe the text quoted above, the termination warning and the named reactions — two newsrooms have the order. This brief has not read the executive order itself and does not know its section structure, its effective date, or whether it carves out subpoenas as opposed to requests. That last distinction is the whole case and it is the first thing to check when the text is available. What would settle it: the order's own language on compelled process; the first contractor to receive a January subpoena and what it does; whether any company sues or seeks a declaratory judgment; and whether the administration actually terminates a contract over this.

What to watch: publication of the order's full text; whether it distinguishes requests from subpoenas; the first January subpoena to a contractor; litigation by a contractor or by Congress; and the ballroom inquiry, which is the nearest live test.

An appeals court has held that a $1.7 trillion spending law was never validly passed

Courts New subject Underreported Single source

A federal appeals court ruled on Friday, 9 to 8, that the 2022 omnibus spending law was unconstitutional because it passed the House on proxy votes. The bill passed 225 to 201 with one member voting present; according to the opinion, 226 lawmakers voted by proxy. The majority held that proxy votes do not count toward a quorum under the Constitution's Quorum Clause, so the House lacked one. It also held that the government had not established “the existence of an emergency” when it adopted proxy voting. A district judge reached the same conclusion in 2022.

The case arrived by an unusual route. Texas's attorney general brought it in 2023 to stop the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act — which requires certain employers to accommodate pregnant workers — from being enforced in Texas. Friday's ruling grants that request. The majority opinion, by a Trump appointee, stresses that the ruling has a “narrow scope”, applying only to enforcement of that provision against Texas. The dissent, by an Obama appointee, argued that no member objected that a quorum was lacking at the time, and that requiring physical presence “shoehorns a much larger requirement into the Constitution”.

What the 2022 bill did, besides the pregnancy provision: it funded government operations across federal agencies and carried roughly $45 billion in emergency assistance to Ukraine and NATO allies and around $40 billion for disaster relief.

Divergence

The point in dispute. How far this reaches. The majority says it is narrow: one provision, one state. A workers'-rights lawyer quoted in the reporting says the opposite — that a holding the law was unconstitutionally enacted “goes far beyond the specific fact patterns of the case”, and that a district judge anywhere in the circuit would now point to this opinion. A dispute about consequences, and both readings can be true at once: the judgment is narrow, the reasoning is not.

Cause. The mechanism to understand is the difference between a holding and a precedent. A court can only grant the relief sought — here, blocking enforcement of one statute against one state — so the disposition is genuinely narrow and the majority is not being disingenuous. But the ground of decision is that the House had no quorum on 23 December 2022, and that fact does not change from statute to statute. Every provision in a $1.7 trillion omnibus rests on the same vote. Reach for that before either dismissing this as a technicality or treating it as the law's repeal: nothing is repealed, no money is clawed back, and the ruling binds one circuit. What has changed is that any litigant in that circuit challenging anything in that bill now has an appellate holding to cite, and the government has to win the quorum question over and over. The 9–8 margin is the other thing to hold on to: a one-vote en banc majority is the least stable kind, and the obvious next step is a petition to the Supreme Court.

What it means for you. Nothing changes today for anyone outside Texas, and the pregnancy-accommodation right remains law everywhere else. Believe the vote counts, the 226 proxy votes, the 9–8 split and the quoted reasoning. This brief has not read the opinion or the dissent and is relying on one newsroom's account of both. What would settle the reach: whether the government seeks Supreme Court review and whether the Court takes it; whether any other provision of the 2022 omnibus is challenged on the same ground in the same circuit; and whether other circuits, which have not ruled, split. One structural note worth keeping: the House's proxy-voting accommodation was used for a great deal more than this bill, and the opinion's logic is indifferent to which bill it was.

What to watch: a certiorari petition; challenges to other provisions of the same bill; whether another circuit reaches the opposite conclusion; and how the incoming Congress responds.

A mathematician on Anthropic's payroll says he and Claude have proved the Riemann Hypothesis

AI & tech industry New subject Independent corroboration

Disclosure, and it is doing more work than usual today: this brief is written using a model made by Anthropic. The claim below is made by an Anthropic employee about an Anthropic product. Read everything that follows with that in mind, including the parts where this brief sounds sceptical.

Levent Alpöge, a member of Anthropic's technical staff, posted a four-page explanation on his own website on Friday saying that, working with Claude, he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Formulated in 1859, it concerns the distribution of prime numbers as they grow toward infinity, and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offered $1 million in 2000. “Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line,” Alpöge wrote. “No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”

The form of the claim is as unusual as its content. The proof was released as 17 million lines of Lean code — a language in which theorems are machine-checked — rather than as a paper. Alpöge says more than 250,000 pages came out of the work and that he is “presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics”. He says the discovery came late on Sunday night, and that he messaged the chief executive about “my ability to announce” because the company was about to go public. He announced the day after the largest initial public offering in history. Anthropic did not respond to a request for comment from the Associated Press.

Two authorities are on the record and they say different things. Alex Kontorovich, mathematics department chair at Rutgers, had Claude follow the code; it said the proof is true. “Does that mean I understand it? Not yet. But the computer is extraordinarily picky, way pickier than I am and so if you can satisfy the computer, there's a very, very good chance you're going to satisfy me too. But I have to do the work.” Martin Bridson, president of the Clay Institute: “This is an announcement of historic significance, which holds the promise of a dramatic transformation in the perception of what is possible… But the journey from today's announcement to a place of real understanding will be long and difficult.” Hours after the announcement, the Clay Institute's website still listed the hypothesis as unsolved.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What a machine-checked proof nobody has read is worth. One reading: Lean does not care who wrote it, and a proof that compiles is a proof — verification is the whole point of the language. The other: mathematics is not only a certificate that a statement is true but an explanation of why, and 17 million lines that no human has followed supplies the first without the second. Kontorovich holds both at once, which is the most honest position available and the reason to quote him at length. A dispute about significance, not facts.

Cause. Take the technical point first, because it is the one that decides whether this is real. A Lean proof's correctness depends on the kernel and on the statement being the one you think it is. The kernel is small and heavily scrutinised; the serious failure mode is not a wrong proof but a right proof of the wrong theorem — a formalised statement that is subtly weaker than the Riemann Hypothesis, or that quietly assumes something. That check is fast and does not require reading 17 million lines, which is why the next few days will tell you more than the next few months. Now the uncomfortable part. This file's rule is to reach for the mundane explanation, and the mundane explanation of the timing is not flattering: a discovery made on a Sunday night, held while an IPO priced, and announced the day after the largest flotation in history, by an employee who asked the chief executive about “my ability to announce”. That says nothing about whether the mathematics is right — Lean does not know when the shares listed — but it is the second such claim from an AI company in three months, after one in September about the Navier–Stokes equations, and a pattern of announcements by interested parties is a reason for slower verification, not faster acceptance.

What it means for you. Nothing yet, and anyone telling you otherwise is ahead of the evidence, including the people who made the claim. Believe that the announcement was made, that the Lean code was released, and the two quoted assessments. Do not believe that the Riemann Hypothesis is proved: the body that would say so has not said so, and the one mathematician quoted says he has not done the work yet. This brief has not seen the four-page explanation, the Lean repository, or any independent formal verification, and it will not be the one to tell you the problem is solved. What would settle it: whether the formalised statement is confirmed by independent mathematicians to be the actual hypothesis; whether the Clay Institute's listing changes; whether the proof is replicated in a second system; and whether a human-readable account follows. The Institute's own rules require publication and a waiting period, so the prize is not a short-term signal in either direction.

What to watch: independent confirmation that the Lean statement is the Riemann Hypothesis; the Clay Institute's listing; peer commentary from number theorists; whether a readable paper follows the code; and whether Anthropic says anything itself.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — the Associated Press via CNN, and Al Jazeera separately; the BBC also carried it. Two named authorities on the record. The claimant is an employee of the company that makes the model this brief is written with. No part of this item has been checked against the Lean code, and this brief has no competence to check it. Texas says the execution went as planned; a toxicologist says the state's own explanation does not work

US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Two days ago this brief led on an execution in Texas that took more than an hour, and recorded the state's position that nothing abnormal happened and that the prisoner may have been exaggerating. Both halves of that have now been tested, and the state's half has lost an argument.

The timeline, from the Texas Department of Criminal Justice itself: Reinaldo Dennes was strapped to the gurney at 6:04pm; attempts to place two lines in his hands and arms failed; a line went into the left side of his neck at 6:20, and another into his left foot at 6:38. Witnesses were brought in; his final statement began at 6:52 and the drugs began flowing less than a minute later. They were stopped at 7:07 and he was declared dead at 7:08. The spokesperson said she did not know how many attempts failed, and that “it's not a race… There is no normal time limit.” Her conclusion: “We feel that all of our processes and procedures were followed and that it was carried out as planned.” The state plans no additional inquiry.

The state's theory of the screaming is that it was performance, and it offered two grounds: that Dennes complained before the drugs were given, and that he complained of pain in a leg with no IV line in it. She added: “It makes you wonder” whether the widely reported Tennessee case had influenced prisoners to act out. “They watch the news. He was aware of the other executions.”

Then the second ground collapsed. Dr Paul Wax, executive director of the American College of Medical Toxicology, said he could not think of any reason why an injection of pentobarbital into the left foot or the left side of the neck would cause pain in the right leg. The state's argument was that right-leg pain was evidence of faking. The toxicologist's answer is that right-leg pain is exactly what you would not expect from those injection sites, whatever the prisoner was feeling — which removes it as evidence of anything, rather than converting it into evidence of suffering.

The witness account is the other new material. The Rev. Jeff Hood, Dennes's spiritual adviser and a death-penalty opponent, had his hand on Dennes's shoulder in the chamber and said he felt he was holding a bucking bronco. “Twice he said, ‘It's not working. It's not working. It's not going how it's supposed to go. This isn't right.' Those types of words kept coming.” He said Dennes was “clearly in tremendous distress” and called it torture. It was Texas's ninth execution this year.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether anything went wrong. The state says its processes were followed and the outcome was as planned. The witness says it was torture. A dispute about facts that is unusually tractable, because both sides agree on the timeline and disagree only about what it means.

Cause. The two claims are compatible and that is the finding. “All our processes and procedures were followed” and “this took sixty-four minutes and involved failed lines, a neck injection and a foot injection” can both be true, because the procedures do not set a time limit — the spokesperson said so herself. Reach for that before reading either side as lying. The state is not claiming it went quickly; it is claiming there was no rule to break. That is the real disclosure in this story, and it is more consequential than the dispute about screaming: a procedure with no defined limit on duration or number of venous access attempts cannot fail by its own terms. Idaho tried eight times on one prisoner in 2024 and stopped; Tennessee called off an execution in May after more than an hour; in Texas on Thursday nobody stopped, and by the department's account nobody was required to consider it.

What it means for you. The question that can actually be answered is not whether he was in pain but whether the protocol has a stopping rule, and the answer appears to be no. Believe the timeline, which is the state's own; the toxicologist's statement; and the adviser's account of what he heard, which an Associated Press reporter also witnessed. Do not believe anyone on whether Dennes was exaggerating — it is unknowable and the state's one piece of evidence has been answered by a specialist. This brief has read no protocol, no autopsy and no court filing. What would settle it: publication of the Texas execution protocol's provisions on failed access attempts and elapsed time; an autopsy or toxicology report; whether any legislator or court asks for the inquiry the department says it will not conduct; and what happens at the next scheduled execution, on 3 December.

What to watch: the 3 December execution; any legislative or judicial demand for review; the Texas protocol's text; and whether other states revise access-attempt limits after Idaho's and Tennessee's experiences.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — the Associated Press via CNN, with the state spokesperson, the spiritual adviser and a named medical specialist all on the record, and an AP reporter present as a witness. This brief has not read the protocol or any post-mortem material. Sources: CNN/AP · 20 November

A tanker has hit a mine east of Hormuz, and two more were struck off Oman on Saturday

Energy & grid Continuation Underreported Single source

Iran's navy said on Saturday that an oil tanker caught fire after hitting a naval mine in a restricted area east of the Strait of Hormuz. That is the first mine in this file's record of the campaign, and it is a different instrument from everything that came before it. Reuters could not verify the Iranian account or establish whether it referred to either of two separate incidents reported the same day.

The United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre reported two strikes on Saturday. A tanker outbound through the strait was hit on its stern by an unidentified projectile about 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman; UKMTO later reported another tanker hit in its engine room by an unidentified projectile 17 nautical miles east of Khasab. It was not immediately clear whether the two reports concerned the same vessel. No casualties in either; environmental impact unknown. A tanker was attacked near Khasab on Friday and caught fire. Khasab sits on Oman's Musandam Peninsula at the strait's entrance; the strait carried close to a fifth of global crude supply before the war.

This brief has been asking for the UKMTO advisories by name since early November. They are the source here, through a wire report rather than directly, and they are now carrying numbered attack warnings — warning 216 of 2026 is the one cited for Saturday.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether Iran is escalating or losing control of its own campaign. The stated Iranian position is control: Tehran has imposed restrictions on vessels transiting the strait and attacks ships it says have violated them, as a way of asserting authority over the waterway and pressuring Washington. The reading the evidence also supports is the opposite: a mine does not choose its victim. A dispute about facts, and the instrument used is the evidence.

Cause. This is the part worth being careful about, because it is the first genuinely new mechanism in weeks. Projectile attacks are discriminating: someone identifies a ship, decides it has broken the rules and fires at it. That is consistent with an enforcement regime, however illegitimate. A moored or drifting mine is indiscriminate by construction. It cannot read a manifest. Iran's own announcement frames the mine as being in “a restricted area”, which is an attempt to keep it inside the enforcement logic — the ship should not have been there. Reach for that before concluding the campaign has changed character: a declared minefield in a declared zone is, in Tehran's framing, a warning sign rather than a weapon. But mines drift, clearance is slow and expensive, and the insurance market does not distinguish between a mine aimed at you and one that was not. The practical effect of mining a waterway is the closure of that waterway to everybody, including Iran's own customers. That is why this is a different event from the fourteen-odd projectile attacks this file has logged, and why it belongs above the line on a Sunday.

What it means for you. Watch war-risk insurance premiums and transit counts, not the attack tally. A mine in the strait raises the cost of every hull that passes, whether or not it is targeted. Believe the two UKMTO reports and the positions, which are a specialist agency's own numbered warnings; treat the Iranian mine claim as an unverified official statement that Reuters explicitly could not confirm. This brief has still not read a UKMTO advisory directly and has these only through a wire report. What would settle it: whether any navy or the IMO declares a mine danger area; whether minehunters deploy; war-risk premium quotes for Hormuz transits; and whether tanker transit counts fall, which is the only measure that shows whether the threat is working.

What to watch: a declared mine danger area; mine countermeasures deployments; war-risk premiums; transit volumes through the strait; and whether UKMTO's warning numbers keep climbing at this rate.

A drone strike burned a sanctioned Russian military-logistics ship in the Gulf of Finland, and Finland says Ukraine did it

Geopolitics Continuation Independent corroboration

The cargo ship Sparta II, bound for Kaliningrad, was hit by drones and caught fire in the Baltic on Saturday in international waters west of Russia's Gogland island, then drifted into Finnish waters. Its sixteen crew evacuated to lifeboats and then to a nearby Russian ship and are on their way home. The fire was still burning late on Saturday with Finland leading the rescue, the vessel at anchor in Finnish waters. Estonia sent a navy vessel for firefighting and pollution response. Swedish combat aircraft flew under Finnish command. A Russian helicopter is suspected of briefly violating Finnish airspace during the operation.

Finland said openly who it thinks did it. “It is likely that Ukraine… is behind (the attack),” an armed forces spokesperson said, adding it was not clear why the vessel was targeted. Prime Minister Petteri Orpo: “The war is spilling over into our neighbouring areas… Ukraine has the right to fight against the aggressor.” Estonia's prime minister: “The consequences of fighting Russia's aggression have reached the Baltic Sea. Another signal for the aggressor to stop. But nothing will stop us from supporting Ukraine.” Helsinki imposed temporary aviation and maritime restrictions in the eastern Gulf of Finland so authorities could operate and intercept drones if needed. Ukraine's foreign ministry and Russia's embassy in Helsinki did not immediately comment.

The ship is not a neutral merchantman. It is under US sanctions and is linked to Oboronlogistika, which by its own website provides logistics services for the Russian defence ministry. The Finnish border guard commander: “At the moment, there is no confirmed information about what cargo is on board, but it cannot be ruled out that there is military materiel on board.” Russia's Leningrad region governor said the vessel was attacked by two drones about three nautical miles from the Russian border, and that 80 drones were shot down over the region overnight. Finland's military said last week it had detected a strike on a Russian warship in the same area.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether NATO members should treat this as a threat or as someone else's legitimate war aim. Helsinki and Tallinn did something unusual: they said both at once. The war is spilling over, and Ukraine has the right to fight. There is no second camp quoted here contradicting them, which is itself the finding — nobody on the record is calling this an attack on NATO or asking Ukraine to stop. A dispute about significance that the parties are deliberately declining to have.

Cause. The target explains the restraint. A sanctioned vessel operated by the Russian defence ministry's logistics arm, sailing to Kaliningrad, is a military supply ship in everything but name — which is why the Finnish border guard will not rule out materiel aboard and why no NATO government has objected to it being hit. Reach for that before reading this as escalation against NATO. What is escalating is geography, not intent. The strike was in international waters three miles from Russia; the burning hull then drifted into Finnish waters, and the alliance's newest members found themselves running a rescue, a pollution response and an air-policing operation for a ship their side's partner had just set on fire. That is the actual cost and it is borne by Finland and Estonia: 80 drones over Leningrad overnight means a lot of airframes near a short border, and a Russian helicopter crossed into Finnish airspace during the response. The restrictions Helsinki imposed are the tell — they exist so Finland can shoot something down over its own gulf without improvising.

What it means for you. The thing to watch is not whether Ukraine strikes shadow-fleet and military shipping again, but what Finland and Estonia have to stand up permanently to cope with it. Believe the vessel's name and ownership, the sanctions designation, the crew's evacuation, the Finnish and Estonian statements and the airspace restrictions — three newsrooms carry them and the attributions are named officials. Treat the attribution to Ukraine as a Finnish assessment that Ukraine has not confirmed, and the 80-drone figure as a Russian regional governor's claim. What would settle the direction: whether the restrictions become permanent; whether NATO's Baltic Sentry mission is reinforced; any Ukrainian acknowledgement; and whether Russia responds against Finnish or Estonian shipping rather than against Ukraine.

What to watch: the duration of Finland's gulf restrictions; NATO force posture in the Baltic; any Ukrainian claim of responsibility; further airspace violations; and whether the wreck is towed to Russia as Finnish authorities expect.

Two US Attorneys filed 882 immigration cases in a fortnight, and masked officers took two people off a jet bridge in Seattle

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Primary source

The Justice Department publishes its own enforcement volume and almost nobody reads it. This brief got into its press feed this morning for the first time, and the numbers are these. The US Attorney's Office for the Western District of Texas filed 568 new immigration and immigration-related criminal cases between 6 and 20 November. The US Attorney's Office for the District of Arizona charged 314 individuals with immigration-related criminal conduct in the single week of 14 to 20 November. That is 882 federal criminal cases from two of the ninety-four districts, in a fortnight, announced by the government as an achievement.

The same enforcement looks different at an airport gate. On Saturday morning, masked officers in unmarked jackets and khaki trousers detained two people on a crowded jet bridge at Seattle-Tacoma International as passengers boarded. Video shows officers dragging at least one person away while a woman screams that her daughter is being taken and that she has a work permit. ICE said it was a targeted operation to arrest a man who had overstayed a visa. It said another passenger “hurled hot coffee on the officers”, that she was determined to be in the country illegally, and that she could be charged with assaulting a federal officer. The Los Angeles-bound Alaska Airlines flight was delayed.

The airline says it had no warning. “We are concerned by this morning's law enforcement action… We recognize how distressing this incident has been for affected employees and guests,” Alaska Airlines said, adding that it is checking with the Port of Seattle whether protocols were followed and has raised concerns with the authorities. The Port said local authorities would “engage federal officials to understand more about what occurred”. Washington's governor said the tactics were “deeply un-American”, called the video “horrific… and chilling”, said ICE is “beyond reform” and should be abolished, and said he believes the officers violated a state law he signed in March limiting face coverings by law enforcement. ICE has stepped up airport arrests since the summer, particularly of people who overstayed visas. A union president noted the action came days after the nationwide ground stop: “Airport workers are already managing extraordinary stress… instead of restoring calm and confidence, federal agents introduced more fear and more chaos.”

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is law enforcement operating at scale or something that has stopped resembling law enforcement. The government's position is in its own press releases: these are criminal cases, filed by prosecutors, in volume, and publishing the count is accountability. The governor's position is that masked, unmarked officers seizing people in front of boarding passengers is not what enforcement of any law should look like, whatever the paperwork says. A dispute about significance, and the two sides are describing the same programme from the two ends.

Cause. Put the two together, because separately each is misleading. 882 cases in a fortnight from two border-district offices is not evidence of a dragnet by itself — those districts have always handled high volumes of illegal-entry and re-entry prosecutions, and a count published without a baseline cannot tell you whether it is up or down. This brief does not have last November's figure and says so. What the releases do establish is intent: the Department is choosing to publicise weekly and fortnightly case counts as a performance metric, which is a decision about what the office is for. And a prosecution metric creates pressure to supply it. That is the connection to the jet bridge: visa overstays are an administrative violation that became an arrest priority, airports are where overstayers are findable, and an officer detaining someone at a gate is working the same pipeline that ends in a line on a press release. Reach for that before treating Seattle as a rogue incident; it is the retail end of the wholesale number.

What it means for you. If you fly, the practical change is that enforcement has moved inside the secure area and to the gate, and the airline will not know in advance. Believe the 568 and the 314 — they are the government's own published figures — and the Seattle account, which is the Associated Press with video, ICE's statement and named officials on all sides. Do not read a trend into the case counts: this brief has no prior-period comparison and will not manufacture one. The port strike did not happen, and the deal says what 250 watchmen are worth

Economy & supply chains Continuation Single source

Yesterday this brief led on a contract expiring at 12:01am Pacific and said it did not know what would happen and would not guess. The answer: the Pacific Maritime Association and ILWU Local 26 reached a tentative agreement on Saturday, averting the strike. It is subject to ratification by the members, and the existing contract is extended until they vote. The two sides had resumed federally mediated talks on Friday, two days after breaking off with the employers reporting “little-to-no movement from the union on PMA's proposals”. Negotiations had run more than a year.

What was on the table, per the employers' own account of their offer: a one-time bonus of $3,200 per member; a wage proposal raising average annual earnings for full-time watchmen from more than $170,000 to $198,000; fully employer-paid healthcare for active workers and retirees valued at more than $62,000 a year; and increased pensions up to $93,200 a year for the longest-tenured. Whether the tentative agreement matches that offer has not been reported, and the union did not respond to a request for comment.

The leverage this file described yesterday held up exactly. 250 security officers — watchmen — control access at terminal gates, conduct security patrols and report safety issues, which is why a group this small could have shut the two busiest container ports in the United States.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What the episode demonstrates. One reading, implicit in the 110 trade associations' joint statement on Friday: the system worked — mediation, pressure, a deal before the deadline, no disruption. The other, implicit in a year of talks ending in a weekend: the system only works at the brink, and a 250-person unit holding eighty-five per cent of a port's cargo is a structural vulnerability that a settlement does not remove. A dispute about significance.

Cause. The sequence is the explanation and it is unglamorous. Talks collapsed on Wednesday, 110 associations issued a joint statement on Friday, federal mediators reconvened the parties the same day, and a tentative deal landed on Saturday. A year of negotiation produced nothing; seventy-two hours of deadline produced an agreement. Reach for that before crediting any one actor: deadline bargaining is what collective bargaining is, and both sides had been holding positions precisely because the deadline had not arrived. The one number that complicates the easy story is $170,000 to $198,000. This is not a low-paid workforce, and the dispute was not about poverty wages — which is worth stating plainly because a port-strike story invites an assumption the figures do not support. It was about what a critical-path job is worth when the employer cannot operate without it, and the answer both sides arrived at is in the offer above.

What it means for you. Holiday cargo moves; nothing is backed up; the thing this brief flagged yesterday as a risk is closed unless the members vote no. Believe the tentative agreement, the extension pending ratification, the federal mediation and the terms of the employers' offer — the last are PMA's own published figures. Do not assume the deal equals the offer: that is not reported, and the union has not spoken. What would settle it: the ratification vote and its margin; whether the agreed terms are published; and the contract's expiry date, which tells you when this recurs. This brief has no comment from Local 26 and has not seen the agreement.

What to watch: the ratification vote; publication of the final terms; the new contract's length; and whether the longshore, clerk and foremen agreements come up on a similar cycle.

Elections · Five states the Democrats will fully control next year would put the National Popular Vote compact over 270 electoral votes.

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact — under which member states award their electoral votes to the winner of the national popular vote — currently has 222 electoral votes, from 18 Democratic-led states and the District of Columbia. Below the line because nothing has been signed and the legal questions — whether the compact needs congressional consent, above all — are unresolved.
