The FAA wants $50 billion. It has not spent two-thirds of the last tranche

US politics & policy US debt & fiscal Continuation Independent corroboration

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy spent Sunday asking Congress for $50 billion for the Federal Aviation Administration, up from the roughly $30 billion he had been seeking before Friday's five-hour nationwide ground stop. The president posted that Congress should give the money fast. Democrats, who take the majority in January, say the agency already has what it needs.

The numbers both sides are arguing from are public and they do not say what either claims. The 2025 Republican spending and policy bill, which no Democrat supported, gave the FAA $12.5 billion over three years, of which $4.75 billion was designated for telecommunications infrastructure modernisation and systems upgrades. Duffy said at Saturday's press conference that the agency has spent about one-third of that modernisation budget. That leaves roughly $3.2 billion appropriated, designated for exactly this purpose, and unspent, while the request on the table is fifteen times the whole telecom line.

The failure has a place name now. Duffy said the ground stop traced to work at an air traffic location in El Dorado, Arkansas, where the contractor L3Harris was switching an old copper line to fibre; whether it was contractor error or faulty equipment is still undetermined. He said the full telecom build can be finished by the first part of 2029 if the money arrives.

Reps. Rick Larsen and André Carson, the senior Democrats on the two House panels with aviation jurisdiction, pushed back on Saturday. The fight lands on a lame-duck Congress that may struggle to keep the government open next month.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the FAA has the money. Duffy's claim is specific and narrow: there is no money in the $12.5 billion that he can use for a new telecom architecture, new airspace-management software, or towers. The Democrats' claim is also specific: Congress appropriated $12.5 billion last year and $4.75 billion of it is telecom modernisation. A dispute about facts — what an appropriation permits — and it is adjudicable from the appropriation's own text.

Cause. Both are reporting from the vantage their job gives them. A secretary who has to deliver a system sees a line item scoped to upgrades of the existing architecture and reads it as unable to fund a replacement; appropriators who wrote the line see $4.75 billion labelled modernisation. The fact that resolves most of it is the one Duffy supplied himself on Saturday and did not repeat on Sunday: one-third spent. An agency that has drawn down a third of a three-year telecom appropriation in roughly a year and a half is spending it roughly on schedule, not running out of it. Note also what the escalation from $30 billion to $50 billion in two days reveals: the larger number is a response to Friday, not to an engineering estimate that changed.

What it means for you. Believe that the FAA both has unspent modernisation money and cannot build a new national telecom architecture for $4.75 billion; those are not in conflict and each side is stating the half that suits it. The thing to watch is not the headline figure but the obligation rate. What would settle it: the FAA's apportionment and obligation data for the $4.75 billion line, which Treasury and OMB publish; the agency's own cost estimate for the replacement architecture, which Duffy has asserted but not released; and the scope language in the 2025 act. This brief has read none of the three and is relying on the secretary's characterisation of his own appropriation.

What to watch: whether any FAA cost estimate is published; the obligation rate on the telecom line; whether aviation funding rides on the December government-funding deadline; Thanksgiving week operations; and the preliminary incident report on El Dorado.

The rule published this morning deletes the Head Start wage floor

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Primary source

A final rule published this morning removes the staff wage and benefit requirements from the Head Start Program Performance Standards. Gone, from 45 CFR 1302.90(e) and (f): the requirement that a programme develop and maintain a pay scale for all staff; that education staff be paid wages comparable to public preschool teachers; that all staff receive a salary sufficient to cover basic costs of living; that wages be comparable across Head Start Preschool and Early Head Start; and the requirement to provide specified benefits. The rule takes effect sixty days after publication.

These requirements were added in 2024 and were not due to bite until 2031. The Administration for Children and Families says it is rescinding them well ahead of that compliance date so programmes stop making operational and financial changes for rules that will not apply. It argues the requirements are not in line with the plain language of the Head Start Act, and that because the 2007 standards contained no federal regulation of staff wages and benefits, removing them does not breach the Act's bar on reducing the 2007 level of quality, scope or types of service.

The 2024 rule existed because Head Start could not keep staff. ACF's own account, restated in this document, is that programmes then faced historically high turnover and vacancies, that low wages and limited benefits were identified as key causes, that qualification requirements had risen without matching pay — and that staffing shortages were stopping some programmes opening classrooms at all.

ACF received nearly 300 comments, including two multi-signatory letters from Members of Congress, most of them objecting on workforce stability, compensation and parity with public schools. The agency built a severability clause into the rule so that striking down the removal of (e) would leave the removal of (f) standing, and vice versa.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether removing a pay floor increases access to Head Start or reduces it. The agency's case is that the requirements are costly and that money not spent on compensation can serve more children; the commenters' case is that the 2024 rule was the remedy for a staffing shortage that was itself closing classrooms. A dispute about facts with a causal direction at stake, not about weighting.

Cause, and the document adjudicates it. The agency's headline number is “over $2 billion in future cost savings”. Commenters objected that this is not money anyone receives — it is compensation that will now not be paid. The agency's response concedes the point in its own words: the roughly $2.1 billion is characterised not as a reduction in appropriations but as an eventual annual transfer, running to staff who would otherwise lose hours or jobs from staff who would otherwise have been paid more. That is an unusually candid statement of the actual trade: this rule moves money between Head Start workers, from wages to headcount. It is not new funding, and ACF does not claim it is.

What it means for you. Treat “over $2 billion in savings” as avoided future wage costs, not resources, and treat the access argument as a real but unproven bet — that slots respond to budget more than staffing responds to pay, which is the opposite of what ACF concluded in 2024 on the same programme. Two concrete things follow for anyone with a child in the programme: nothing requires a programme that already raised wages to cut them, and nothing requires any programme to raise them. What would settle it: Head Start enrolment and classroom counts against staff turnover and vacancy rates over the next two years, which the Office of Head Start collects; and the fate of the rule in court, which the severability clause tells you the agency expects.

What to watch: litigation and whether the severability clause is tested; the effective date sixty days out; enrolment and vacancy data; whether appropriators respond in the next bill; and whether states with their own pre-K pay parity rules diverge further from federal Head Start.

The reading of the transfer language is this brief's own. Sources: ACF, Restoring Flexibility to Support Head Start Program Access (RIN 0970-AD21) · 15 November

A major who survived the strike says he was told the base did not need protecting

US politics & policy Geopolitics New subject Single source

Army Maj. Stephen Ramsbottom, who survived the 1 March Iranian strike on a makeshift operations centre at Kuwait's Shuaiba port, has filed a whistleblower disclosure with the House and Senate Armed Services committees alleging the Pentagon disregarded warnings that the site had no protection against aerial attack. Six US Army Reserve soldiers were killed — the first American service members to die in the war with Iran — and dozens, Ramsbottom among them, were wounded.

The specific allegations are operational, not political. He says he raised the lack of protection from aerial attack with superiors in several meetings and was told protection was not a concern. He says leaders later said they would try to get a counter-drone system sent to Shuaiba; it never arrived, and he says he does not know whether it was ever requested or ordered. The disclosure states the installation had no physician and no fixed aid station, and one ambulance.

He called the attack a tactical failure by the Army, its leadership and himself, in an interview broadcast on Sunday, and said some described the operations centre as a trailer park. He has made similar claims in interviews since April, including that the facility was fortified for legacy threats such as mortars and car bombs rather than for drones.

The timing is the part with consequences. The disclosure went to the committees last week, weeks before Democrats take control of Congress in January and open investigations into the conduct of the Iran war. A complaint to a committee that is about to acquire subpoena power is a different instrument from one filed to a committee that is not.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the Shuaiba site was protected. Senior Pentagon officials said at the time that the building was fortified. A source familiar with the site has described concrete barriers that would stop a car bomb and nothing that would stop a drone or a missile. A dispute about facts that dissolves once you ask “fortified against what” — and then becomes a harder question about who chose the threat model.

Cause. Both statements can be literally true, which is the mundane explanation and almost certainly the right one. Force-protection standards are written against a named threat set, and a site hardened to the standard for indirect fire and vehicle-borne devices is correctly described as fortified by anyone reading that standard. The substantive allegation is not that someone lied about the barriers; it is that the threat set was out of date and that a named remedy — counter-drone cover — was discussed and never delivered. Note the vantage: the Pentagon's statement was made in the first days after the strike, when the institutional answer to any question is the standard the site was built to; the disclosure is made twenty months later by someone who was inside it.

What it means for you. The claim that can actually be checked is the counter-drone system: either a requisition exists or it does not. That single document decides whether this is a story about a threat model everyone shared and got wrong, or about a specific request that died in a staff process. Believe meanwhile that six soldiers died at a site whose protection was designed for a different war, because both sides agree on that. What would settle it: the requisition or tasking record for counter-drone equipment at Shuaiba; the force-protection assessment in force on 1 March; and the Army's own investigation of the strike, which has not been published. This brief has read none of them, and has not read the disclosure itself.

What to watch: whether the committees act before January; whether the Army releases its investigation; whether the counter-drone requisition surfaces; the Pentagon's response, which had not come by the time this was written; and whether other survivors file.

Confidence: Single-sourced on the disclosure — CNN, working from a copy it obtained, crediting CBS with the first report and a Sunday broadcast interview. The Pentagon and the committees had not commented. This brief has read neither the disclosure nor any Army document. Sources: CNN

A missile meant for the Revolutionary Guard hit a girls' school, and the targeting was AI-assisted

AI & tech industry Geopolitics New subject Single source

A US cruise missile aimed at Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck a girls' school in the Iranian city of Minab in late February, killing more than 150 people, most of them schoolchildren. Handelsblatt, reporting from Geneva and Berlin, says investigations attribute the error to time pressure, outdated intelligence, and excessive trust in AI-supported target planning. The strike was not autonomous: a human authorised it.

That last distinction is the whole of why the story is being told now. The 128 states party to the United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons are making a fresh attempt in Geneva to constrain autonomous weapons — systems that select and engage targets without a human deciding. Minab is being used as the argument that the line between assisted and autonomous is already thin in practice: a human approved a target package that a machine assembled from stale data under a clock.

This is a gap in this brief's own record. Minab has not appeared here in any issue, in a brief that has carried the Iran war as a standing thread since February and the AI industry as a core beat. A strike that killed more than 150 people, most of them children, and whose stated cause is AI-assisted targeting, sits exactly where those two threads cross, and this brief missed it for nine months.

The CCW has been discussing autonomous weapons since 2014 without producing a binding instrument; it works by consensus, which means any one state can stop it.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether AI-assisted targeting is a different kind of risk from ordinary targeting error. One reading: machines did not decide, humans did, and the failure modes — stale intelligence, time pressure — are the oldest in warfare, so Minab is an argument for better intelligence, not for new law. The other: a system that assembles and ranks targets changes what the human in the loop is actually doing, because approving a machine's package under a clock is not the same act as choosing a target. A dispute about significance.

Cause. The two readings come from different definitions of the thing being regulated, and each party's definition serves it. States that field these systems define autonomy narrowly — the machine pulls the trigger — which puts almost everything now in service outside any new rule. Campaigners define it by meaningful human control, which puts decision-support inside the rule. Minab is evidence for the second definition and is being deployed as such; note that the attribution of cause comes from investigations that this brief has not seen and that Handelsblatt does not name. The timing also matters: the detail surfaces in a news cycle about the Geneva conference, not from a published inquiry.

What it means for you. The evidence supports treating decision-support as the live question and full autonomy as the decoy, because the first is deployed and the second mostly is not. The practical test for any instrument that emerges from Geneva is whether it reaches systems that recommend rather than fire; a treaty that bans only the machine pulling the trigger would not have changed anything about Minab. What would settle the factual half: the investigation Handelsblatt cites, named and published; the US military's own assessment of the Minab strike; and the casualty count from a source other than this one. Until then this stands on one non-English report and is labelled single-sourced for that reason.

What to watch: whether the Geneva conference produces a mandate and on what definition; whether any US assessment of Minab is released; whether the figure of 150 is corroborated; and whether Congress's January investigations into the Iran war reach targeting practice.

Confidence: Single source — Handelsblatt, in German; everything here is paraphrase, and the investigations it refers to are not named by it or read by this brief. No US, Iranian or UN document has been seen. Sources: Handelsblatt, in German

The company at the centre of the Nvidia case says she left fifteen months ago

AI & tech industry Continuation Independent corroboration

Megaspeed International said on Sunday that Alice Huang left her executive role on 30 August 2025 and has held no position with the firm since. The Singapore data-centre company added that it does not comment on her personal affairs, pushed back on reports about its operations as not supported by verifiable evidence, and said it has retained outside counsel for an internal investigation. Its chairman and chief executive is Tan Yong Pong.

That date matters because of what this brief has been publishing. The case as reported rests on intelligence intercepts from this spring, a memo written by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, and a relationship between Jensen Huang and a woman US officials believe to be a Chinese intelligence asset who helped establish Megaspeed. If she left Megaspeed in August 2025, she was not an executive of that company when the intercepts were collected.

The rest of the weekend was about who is to blame and who is next. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Fox News the Nvidia board should hire from outside the company and that it needs a dramatic shake-up. Handelsblatt reports Huang is in talks about returning. CNN, working from more than a dozen sources, published an account of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick cultivating Huang from the start of the term — Huang recalled Lutnick calling his company a national treasure — while officials accuse Lutnick of blocking the Entity List designation that Treasury, State, the Pentagon and the trade representative all wanted.

The market has already voted. On LSEG data, Nvidia's market value fell from about $5.8 trillion in early October to $3.9 trillion on Friday. It reports third-quarter results on Tuesday. Trump meets Xi Jinping next week in Shenzhen, and US officials say he is not expected to raise the spying allegation.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Alice Huang's relationship to Megaspeed at the time of the conduct alleged. Megaspeed: she left on 30 August 2025 and has held no role since. The reporting this brief has carried: she helped establish the company and is the link between an Nvidia chief executive and a suspected diversion route. A dispute about facts, dated and checkable, and it is the load-bearing joint of the whole story.

Cause. Reach for the mundane reading first: these two claims are compatible. “Helped establish” and “held an executive role until August 2025” are statements about the past; neither is refuted by her leaving. A company under investigation has every reason to date her departure precisely and no reason to volunteer anything else, and the statement is carefully bounded — it addresses her position, not her access, her shareholding, or her contact with anyone. Equally, the reporting has never clearly stated what she was doing at the time of the spring intercepts, and the absence has been easy to miss because the affair supplied the narrative glue. Timing again: this statement lands three days after Huang resigned and the day before the company's quarterly results.

What it means for you. Hold the two halves apart. The diversion question — whether Megaspeed's chips reach Chinese developers — does not depend on her at all, and is the question the Entity List decision turns on. The espionage question does depend on her, and the public record now contains a specific date that the reporting has not engaged with. What would settle it: what, if anything, the spring intercepts say about Megaspeed as opposed to about a relationship; the Rubio memo; Singapore's corporate filings for her directorships and shareholdings; and the Entity List decision, which is still not made. This brief has none of these and has been running the story on wire accounts for four days.

What to watch: Tuesday's Nvidia results and any guidance on China; whether the board hires outside, inside, or re-hires; the Entity List decision; whether Lutnick stays; the Shenzhen meeting; and whether anyone dates the intercepts against 30 August 2025.

The Russian diesel went to Egypt and Brazil

Energy & grid Macro & markets Continuation Independent corroboration

Two of the tankers that left Russia's Primorsk in mid-October declaring the US Atlantic coast have discharged or are heading elsewhere. The Talisman has docked at the Port of Alexandria in Egypt; the Leruo is now bound for Brazil. Both are under European and British sanctions and the Talisman is sanctioned in the United States as well. The Agioi Fanendes, which listed the Port of New York and New Jersey and was expected around 7 November, spent Sunday hovering hundreds of miles off the east coast broadcasting FOR ORDERS.

More than a month after the deal was announced, no Russian diesel cargo has reached the United States. US businesses and ports have avoided the cargoes over legal and reputational risk, according to experts cited by CNN; bipartisan lawmakers and European allies condemned the arrangement as a concession that helps finance the war and warned that importers could fall foul of sanctions law.

A White House official's formulation is worth reading twice: consistent with the deal, Russian diesel is on the water and heading to the market, which will ease prices for Americans. The official did not say it is heading to the United States — and, on the tracking data, it is not.

Meanwhile the American price keeps falling without it. AAA's national diesel average is $5.9989 this morning, through $6 for the first time since the summer and 52.9 cents below the $6.5276 record of 22 September. Regular is $3.8118, down 51.5 cents on the month. The diesel premium over regular is $2.1871, a second consecutive day below the record set on Friday.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the Putin–Trump diesel arrangement is working. The White House says the diesel is on the water and will ease prices for Americans. Ship-tracking data says the cargoes that declared the United States went to Egypt and Brazil, and none has landed here. A dispute about facts, and the statements do not actually collide — which is the finding.

Cause. Yesterday this brief offered three mundane explanations for the U-turn: loading is not delivery, no US buyer will take the cargo, or Russia is short of diesel. Today's evidence picks the second and nearly rules out the third: the Talisman discharged, and a cargo that discharges in Alexandria is a cargo that existed and found a buyer somewhere other than here. The careful official wording fits the same reading — “to the market” is true of a barrel landing in Egypt, and displacing non-Russian barrels in Egypt does loosen the global product balance a little. Note the vantage: the administration is measuring the deal against world supply, and its critics against US imports, and the deal was sold on the second.

What it means for you. Believe that the barrels are moving, that none is coming here, and that the price fall at your pump has nothing to do with either fact. Diesel is through $6 because crude fell $9.62 in the week to 6 November and because refining margins are finally being competed down, not because of a deal that has delivered nothing to a US terminal. The settling observation remains a single discharge at a named US terminal. The second one, which is now more interesting: whether the crack spread narrows from $97.99 on Wednesday's EIA release. Two consecutive days of the diesel premium falling is the first hint of it, and two days is not a trend.

What to watch: the Agioi Fanendes's eventual port; the other loaded tankers; Wednesday's EIA weekly release and the crack spread; whether the diesel premium keeps falling; and whether Congress legislates on Russian-origin product.

Three ships burning off Romania, eight arrests in Estonia, and a NATO border closes

Geopolitics New subject Independent corroboration

Drones struck three commercial ships in the Black Sea near Romania's Sulina canal late on Saturday, Prime Minister Luca Niculescu said, and Romania scrambled fighter jets twice. Defence Minister Stefan Radu Oprea named the vessels: the Greek-flagged oil and chemical tanker Evia Petrol V, and the bulk carrier Marsol and products tanker Ali Najafov, both flagged in St Kitts and Nevis. No casualties; the crews controlled the fires. Romanian officials did not identify the drones' origin but called them a direct consequence of Russia's war. Russia denies targeting Ukraine's neighbours.

The Sulina canal is the link between the Danube and the Black Sea and is the route Ukraine uses for exports when the deep-water ports are unusable. It runs through NATO territory.

On the other sea, Estonia is closing its border with Russia from 1 December. Eight people were detained after an attempted arson attack on an Estonian defence technology company overnight on Saturday; Estonian security services say it was carried out on the orders of Russian military intelligence. Prime Minister Kristen Michal said Russian services exploit border crossings to exert influence and that closing the crossings reduces the risk; he did not say when they would reopen. Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna expected further sanctions on Russian individuals and entities at a foreign ministers' meeting in Brussels today. Russia's foreign ministry called it Russophobia.

The common feature is that neither is an attack on a NATO military target, and both are expensive.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether these are spillover from the war in Ukraine or a campaign directed at NATO states. Romania's own officials call the strikes a consequence of the war and decline to name an origin; Estonia names the GRU and treats the arson as deliberate policy. A dispute about significance that governs the response, since spillover calls for protection and a campaign calls for deterrence.

Cause. Vantage and evidence, not motive. Estonia has eight people in custody and an intelligence service's attribution; Romania has wreckage and no recovered guidance package it has described. A government that can attribute does; one that cannot, does not, and the difference in language tracks the difference in evidence rather than in judgement. There is also a cost asymmetry worth naming: attribution obliges you to respond, and Romania's response options in a shared sea lane are far worse than Estonia's at a land border. Reach for that before reaching for reluctance.

What it means for you. The US channel is concrete and it is not Article 5. It is the Sulina route, which carries Ukrainian grain and now carries war-risk premiums; it is the Brussels sanctions list, which reaches US correspondent banking; and it is the NATO air-policing and Black Sea maritime presence that American forces underwrite while the US Navy's weight is in the Gulf. Believe that both states are describing the same Russian pressure and that only one of them can prove it. What would settle it: Romania naming a drone type or origin; whether NATO invokes Article 4 consultations; the Brussels sanctions package; and whether the Sulina incidents repeat, which is what distinguishes stray munitions from a campaign.

What to watch: any Romanian attribution; Article 4 consultations; today's Brussels sanctions decisions; whether Black Sea war-risk premiums move; Estonian border traffic before 1 December; and whether more EU states restrict crossings.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — Reuters via gCaptain with the Romanian prime minister and defence minister named and the three vessels identified, and CNN with the Estonian prime minister, foreign minister and the Russian foreign ministry response. No NATO or Estonian document has been read. Sources: Reuters via gCaptain · CNN

Nineteen years without a 5% ten-year, and the last one was July 2007

US debt & fiscal Macro & markets Continuation Primary source

Yesterday this brief withdrew its own 18 September correction, having established that the four figures it rested on were the seven-year column rather than the ten-year. One claim was left withdrawn, because the file then to hand could not check it: that the ten-year going through 5% on 15 September was the first time since July 2007. Twenty annual files from Treasury settle it.

The last session on which Treasury's ten-year par yield closed at or above 5.00% before this year was 19 July 2007, at 5.04%. From 2008 through 2025 — eighteen calendar years, 4,502 sessions — there was not one. The annual maxima run 4.27, 3.98, 4.01, 3.75, 2.39, 3.04, 3.01, 2.50, 2.60, 2.62, 3.24, 2.79, 1.88, 1.74, 4.25, 4.98, 4.70 and 4.79; the closest approach was 4.98% on 19 October 2023. The original 15 September lead was right to the month and is restored in full.

The file also contains something this brief has not previously said. The 2007 peak was 5.26%, on 12 June; the 2026 peak is 5.31%, on 5 October. In these twenty years, this October was the highest the ten-year has been.

Friday's close was 5.15% and Monday has not printed yet. The lesson is the one in the chart: three weeks of commentary about a spread, and a correction built on a misread column, both came from looking at a window instead of at the series.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Not between outlets — between this brief's September self and its November self, about whether the ten-year crossed 5% and whether that was a two-decade event. Having resolved the first yesterday, the second is now resolved too, and in the original story's favour on both counts.

Cause, restated because it generalises. The September error was a column read by position; yesterday's finding was that the correction reproduced the seven-year on all four dates. Today's addition is that the claim withdrawn alongside it — “first since July 2007” — was withdrawn not because it was doubted but because it was unverified, and those are different things. A correction that withdraws everything travelling with a bad number is tidy and it is also lossy: it threw away a true statement that twenty small files could have confirmed in a minute. The mundane explanation for all of it is hurry, on a morning when the brief believed it had just published a wrong market level.

What it means for you, and the rule. Nothing about the market reading changes: 5.15% is not a high, 5.31% in October was, and November's move is almost entirely real yield rather than inflation expectations. What changes is how far back you should think this goes. A ten-year at these levels is not a return to 2023; it is a level the United States last borrowed at before the financial crisis, with roughly three times the debt stock outstanding. The rule, now in the file: withdraw only what the error touches, and flag the rest unverified with a named way to check it.

What to watch: Monday's close; whether the ten-year holds above 5% into the mid-December FOMC; the ten-year real yield; the twenty- and thirty-year, which have not confirmed the move; and the next ten-year auction.

Space · A Vulcan rocket failed ten minutes after lifting off from Cape Canaveral on Sunday, destroying forty Amazon satellites — the first failed mission in the twenty-year history of the Boeing–Lockheed launch venture.

United Launch Alliance's Vulcan failed about ten minutes after leaving Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, and the forty satellites aboard for Amazon's Leo internet service did not reach orbit. ULA said an anomaly occurred, citing launch-director data, and that it does not yet have precise data on what happened. The cause was not immediately clear.

Vulcan is the company's new principal vehicle and the one it intends to use to put Amazon's constellation up on a regular cadence; ULA is the joint venture of Boeing and Lockheed Martin and this is its first failed mission. The practical consequence is schedule, not hardware: a constellation operator with a licence deadline needs launches at a rate, and the vehicle that was going to supply that rate is now grounded pending an anomaly investigation while its main competitor demonstrated a tower catch of a far larger rocket two days ago.

Aviation · The system that moved 45,000 flights a day until Friday runs on paper strips passed between controllers, floppy discs, and copper wire for nearly a third of its communications.

Courts · Florida's attorney general says his office will stop defending convictions under the state's 2018 bump stock ban, and has issued a formal opinion that the law is unconstitutional.
