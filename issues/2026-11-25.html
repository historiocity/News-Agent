They reproduced the failure that grounded every aircraft in America, and it was a router setting nobody checked

Change control Continuation Underreported Single source

This file has carried the nationwide ground stop as its longest-running owed question. Investigators have now recreated the failure in a laboratory and believe they have the cause. A new router installed at a remote radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas had an overlooked configuration setting that governed how it interfaced with the decades-old routers the FAA is decommissioning. That interaction flooded the air traffic control network with data, producing the radar and radio gaps that stopped every aircraft in the country. Investigators believe software patches will prevent a repeat, and are preparing a report. A source familiar with the investigation says they are “90% sure” it was not caused by nefarious actors; the FBI and other national security representatives took part.

The second half of the story is the one this brief reported on 18 November from the technicians' union and two sources, and it now has numbers. The maintenance job — switching from an old router to a new one — used to involve at least six FAA employees. It is now often coordinated by one: an FAA liaison. There are four people in the country with that title. Their job is to coordinate and schedule the work, not to approve it, because an administration directive aimed at speeding up network maintenance eliminated the requirement for FAA approval.

“It's not reasonable to have four people coordinating telecommunications maintenance and outages for the entire national air space system,” said David Spero, president of the Professional Aviation Safety Specialists. A source familiar with the process: “Their workload is unimaginable; they are working massive amounts of overtime.” The same source says the directive also removed a layer of FAA review that was critical to assessing risk — local technical operations managers and site technicians deciding when work would least affect traffic and gaming out worst cases. The union says that did not happen before the contractor arrived in El Dorado.

The contractor is L3Harris, which owns and operates the telecommunications system. Upgrades have been paused during the investigation. The FAA administrator and the transportation secretary appear before Congress next week, and the secretary is asking for another $50 billion. CNN's aviation analyst, a former NTSB managing director: “This does expose the system's vulnerability as the FAA continues the work of upgrading its system.”

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this was a technical fault or a process failure. The investigation's answer is technical and narrow: a setting, an interface, a patch. The union's answer is that the setting was missed because the review that would have caught it was abolished. A dispute about causation, and both are supported by the same facts.

Cause. They are not alternatives, and conflating them is the mistake to avoid. A misconfigured router is the proximate cause and it is genuinely mundane: a new device talking to obsolete devices during a transition is exactly where interface defects live, which is why such work is normally scheduled, reviewed and risk-assessed rather than merely coordinated. The removal of the approval step did not cause the setting to be wrong. It removed the opportunity to notice. Reach for that before reading either “just a bug” or “deliberate sabotage of safety”. And note what the 90% figure means in practice: investigators are not certain it was benign, the FBI was involved, and a report is still being written. The honest summary is that a documented technical cause has been reproduced, and that the organisational conditions the union described a week ago are now corroborated by sources inside the process rather than only by the union. This brief printed that corroboration gap on 18 November as the thing it could not resolve. It is resolved.

What it means for you. The fix is a software patch, which means the risk recurs until it is deployed, and the upgrade programme that caused the exposure is paused in the meantime. Believe the lab reproduction, the router setting, the six-to-one staffing change and the four liaisons — all sourced, with the union president on the record. This brief has not seen the report, which is not finished, nor the administration directive, nor any statement from the FAA or L3Harris, both of which were asked. What would settle it: the report itself; whether it addresses the removed approval step or only the setting; what the FAA administrator and transportation secretary say under questioning next week; whether the $50 billion request is conditioned on restoring review; and whether the paused upgrades resume before or after the patches.

What to watch: publication of the investigators' report; next week's congressional hearings; whether the approval requirement is restored; the patch timetable; and whether the upgrade pause lifts.

Nvidia's board took back the chief executive it removed, and the attorney general will not say whether he was cleared

AI & tech industry Continuation Correction Independent corroboration

Correction. On 21 November this brief wrote, of Jensen Huang's resignation, that “the circumstances of the resignation are personal conduct and this brief is not reporting them”, and carried the story only as a governance item. That framing was wrong and the material to see it was already in this file. Reporting now states that US officials feared the woman involved could be a Chinese intelligence asset — a counterintelligence matter, not a private one — and this brief had itself reported on 18 November that Commerce had filed a rule making remote access to Nvidia chips an export-control question, and that California was seeking answers about the company's China shipments. Three strands pointing at export control were on this file's own pages, and it chose the narrowest reading of the fourth. That is recorded here rather than quietly repaired.

Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive on Monday, restoring his board seat, a week after its chair said the board had unanimously accepted his resignation and had no intention of bringing him back. He returned to headquarters to applause. The shares rose more than 30% on Monday. The three longest-serving directors — Tench Coxe, Mark Stevens and Harvey Jones — saw their combined holdings rise by nearly $2.5 billion between Friday and Monday. Nearly half of the eleven-person board has served since the company's early days; four members for about three decades.

The president has been open about pushing for it, and went further on Tuesday. Asked about a pardon for Huang: “It's always an option, I guess, but I don't think it'll be necessary.” He urged Huang to “tell the truth” before Congress: “I don't think he needs to take the Fifth.” Huang testifies behind closed doors next Tuesday and in public next Thursday. The president also checked the share price from the Resolute Desk during the interview.

His own party is not with him. The Republican chairs of the House intelligence and foreign affairs committees and of the select committee on the Chinese Communist Party said in a joint statement that the company's “brief internal investigation” and the reinstatement “do not erase the national security threats and questions that must be addressed”. Rep. Pat Harrigan: “Today, we sent a signal that one man's financial impact on our economy outweighs the importance of good governance, good citizenship, and adherence to the law. That is a dangerous precedent.” Sen. Kevin Cramer: the legal and reputational risk of hiring a chief executive “who may very well be convicted in the United States or other nations of violating espionage and export control laws” sits with the board, but “the national security risk is passed along to the United States military and its citizens”. Sen. John Curtis, on the board's statement that it relied on input from “senior levels of the United States government”: “Who in our government weighed in? What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?” Sen. Tom Cotton: the Nvidia and Megaspeed controversy “is just the tip of the iceberg, and it's not resolved because Jensen Huang has his job back”.

And the question nobody will answer: Attorney General Todd Blanche, asked on Monday evening whether Huang had been cleared of any national security concerns or potential legal violations before being reinstated, refused to say. Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld called the board's decision “a complete failure of corporate governance” and urged the directors to resign. Huang has apologised to employees and said he is in therapy.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the reinstatement resolved anything. The president's position is that it did: the man is aboveboard, the stock recovered, a pardon probably will not be needed. The position of his own party's national-security chairs is that an internal investigation by a board of thirty-year friends cannot resolve an export-control question, and that nothing has been established either way. A dispute about facts — whether Huang was cleared — and it is unresolved because the only official who could answer declined to.

Cause. Separate the three things being argued about, because they are being deliberately merged. First, the governance question: a board where nearly half the members have served three decades, which reversed a unanimous decision in a week under presidential pressure, and whose three longest-serving members gained $2.5 billion in two days. Reach for the ordinary explanation here — long-tenured boards of founder-led companies are common and were long praised at this one — but a board that cannot hold a founder to a decision it took unanimously seven days earlier is not exercising oversight, and a professor who studies this called it a complete failure. Second, the national-security question, which is entirely separate and entirely unresolved: a relationship that US officials feared involved a potential Chinese intelligence asset, an ongoing controversy over chips reaching China, and an attorney general who will not say whether the man was cleared. Third, the presidential one, which is the newest: floating a pardon for someone the government will not say is under investigation establishes, in public, that the outcome of any such investigation is at the president's discretion. That is why Republicans who agree with him about China are breaking with him here.

What it means for you. The checkable moment is next Thursday, in public, under oath. Believe the reinstatement, the share move, the $2.5 billion, the quoted legislators — all on the record — and the attorney general's refusal, which is itself a fact. Believe nothing about what Huang did or did not do: no charge has been brought, no clearance has been confirmed, and this brief is not in a position to say there was wrongdoing or that there was none. What would settle it: whether the Justice Department confirms or denies an investigation; what Huang says in the closed and public hearings and whether he invokes the Fifth Amendment; whether the board publishes who in government advised it, which is Senator Curtis's question; and whether Cotton's Chip Security Act, requiring location verification on advanced chips, moves.

What to watch: next Tuesday's closed hearing and next Thursday's public one; any DOJ statement; whether the board answers Curtis; the Chip Security Act; and whether any director resigns.

This brief has seen no charging document, no board minutes and no government statement on whether any investigation exists. Up to fifty commercial ships have been hacked, and the same campaign spoiled the food on US military bases

Cyber Continuation Underreported Single source

The United States government has intelligence that as many as fifty commercial shipping vessels were recently compromised by cyberattacks, and is investigating whether the activity is linked to Iran. The sourcing is a US official not authorised to speak on the record, a former military cyber operator and an industry executive briefed on the attacks.

The mechanism is specific and it is not exotic. Attackers used known, unpatched vulnerabilities in Fortinet FortiGate devices, which manage firewalls and VPNs and are common in the maritime industry. The Coast Guard and FBI issued a bulletin in September warning the industry: “Threat actors routinely scan for specific, unpatched vulnerabilities to gain a foothold.” Having got in through the IT systems, the official says, they were able to pivot to operational technology — the devices controlling physical processes — and “could have impacted the systems on board that control propulsion, steering, navigation, and cargo management”. No examples of that actually happening were offered. At least one affected ship was an LNG tanker, where cargo temperature control is a safety function. Crews noticed email failures, login failures and in one case an extended communications blackout.

What the agencies will say on the record is narrow. In August the Coast Guard and FBI sent a “highly specialized team” to board two foreign commercial vessels in the Gulf of America bound for US ports; the captain, crew and shore staff were “critical partners in helping to ensure the threats were mitigated”. Both press offices say there are no reports of “operational disruptions, vessel instability, physical danger to crews, or environmental impacts”. Dozens more ships in international waters are believed compromised.

And here is the answer to a question this brief has asked in four consecutive issues. On 20 November it reported Britain's operation to pull refrigeration controllers off the internet, noted that the first wave of such attacks this year hit US military bases in August, and wrote that the American angle was unexamined and no advisory had been seen. NPR's report attributes that pattern: cybersecurity experts and US officials believe Iranian hackers were behind attacks on US water treatment systems in August, and behind “a pattern of attacks targeting refrigeration cooling systems that led to spoiled food at grocery stores on U.S. military bases and all across Europe as recently as early November”.

Divergence

The point in dispute. How bad this is. The agencies' on-the-record position is that nothing has gone wrong: no disruption, no instability, no danger to crews, no environmental impact. The unnamed official's position is that attackers reached the systems controlling propulsion and steering. Both can be true, and the gap between them is the whole story: access without use. A dispute about significance, in which the optimistic reading describes the present and the pessimistic one describes the option held.

Cause. The entry point explains the scale and it is dull. FortiGate appliances are standard maritime kit, the vulnerabilities were known and published, and ships are hard to patch: a vessel at sea has intermittent connectivity, a small crew with no IT staff, and an owner who may be in a different jurisdiction from the manager and the charterer. That is why one unpatched product can yield fifty ships rather than one. Reach for that before reaching for Iranian capability: the sophistication here is in the targeting and the pivot to operational technology, not in the break-in. What makes the attribution credible rather than speculative is the pattern NPR sets out: water treatment in August, refrigeration controllers in Europe and on US bases into early November, and now shipping — three sectors, one method, all civilian critical infrastructure, during a war. And it connects to this file's own reporting in a way that is uncomfortable: on Thursday GAO said USDA and FDA had not met five NDAA requirements on information sharing and emergency preparedness for exactly the sector that was being attacked.

What it means for you. Nothing to do, and that is the point: this is infrastructure you depend on and cannot patch. Believe the Coast Guard and FBI statements and the September bulletin, which are on the record; treat the fifty-vessel figure, the Iranian attribution and the propulsion claim as an unnamed official's account carried by two newsrooms. This brief has not read the bulletin, has no vessel names, and notes that “as many as” fifty is a ceiling, not a count. What would settle it: whether the Coast Guard issues a marine safety alert naming the vulnerability; whether any owner discloses; whether CISA publishes anything — this brief has asked for five issues running; whether an incident causes an actual casualty; and whether the US government attributes publicly rather than through officials who will not be named.

What to watch: a Coast Guard marine safety alert; any public attribution; CISA advisories; insurance-market reaction; and whether the refrigeration attribution is ever stated officially.

Confidence: Single source — NPR, with an unnamed US official, a former military cyber operator, an industry executive, and on-the-record statements from the FBI and Coast Guard; the series was first reported by the Wall Street Journal, which this brief has not read. No bulletin, no vessel names, no official attribution. Sources: NPR · 20 November · 23 November

Ten tankers struck in a week, twenty-six tugboats blacklisted, and no evidence for the mines Iran says it laid

Energy & grid Continuation Underreported Independent corroboration

Four more commercial vessels were attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz on 22 November — three tankers hit by unidentified projectiles while transiting eastbound, and a bulk carrier at anchor off Khor Fakkan in the UAE, which caught fire. All crew accounted for; the tankers continued their voyages; the four vessels have not been identified. UKMTO initially logged the tanker strikes as one warning and then reclassified them as three separate attacks. The Joint Maritime Information Center counted seven tankers struck since 16 November; these bring it to ten in a single week. JMIC assesses the threat level as severe and says further deliberate attacks are highly likely.

The new element is that Iran is now going after the ships that help other ships. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority added 26 tugboats to its list of non-compliant vessels, accusing them of servicing ships that breach its restrictions in the southern strait. It says it identified them through intelligence monitoring and reports from citizens in neighbouring countries, and repeated that companies assisting non-compliant ships will themselves be blacklisted. A maritime security analyst at Orbital Risk Intelligence put the list at 240 vessels as of 19 November, so the total is now around 266. Towing and salvage operators are exactly the services a growing number of damaged and disabled vessels need.

Two counts worth keeping. The International Maritime Organization's tally of confirmed incidents in the strait and the wider Middle East reached 141 as of 23 November, with 25 confirmed seafarer deaths. Recently damaged vessels it names include the Captain Leon, Bahra and On Phoenix. And the Swedish Club, a marine insurer, has urged ships to avoid the strait altogether as the security situation deteriorates.

On the mines, this brief owes a qualification of its own Sunday lead. On 22 November it led on Iran's claim that a tanker had struck a naval mine east of the strait, labelled as an unverified Iranian statement Reuters could not confirm. Orbital Risk Intelligence now says it has seen no evidence to substantiate Iran's repeated mine claims, and gCaptain reports flatly that they have not been independently confirmed. The claim has not been disproved, but the weight this brief gave it — a lead built around a mine being a categorically different instrument — was heavier than the evidence has since supported.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What Iran is actually doing. Tehran's account, through its own strait authority, is enforcement: a published non-compliance list, blacklisting by category, intelligence-led identification. The analysts' account is that the mine claims are unsubstantiated and the attacks are projectile strikes on whatever is transiting. A dispute about facts, and the analysts are winning it on evidence.

Cause. The blacklist tells you more than the attacks do. Adding 26 tugboats is not an escalation in violence; it is an attempt to make the waterway unusable without firing more. A tanker can accept the risk of transiting. A tanker that has been hit and needs a tow cannot operate at all if the towing companies have been blacklisted. Reach for that before reading the count of ten strikes as the main story: the service blockade is cheaper, it compounds, and it does not require hitting anything. Against which, the mine question matters because it is the one claim that would make the strait dangerous to everyone regardless of compliance, and two specialist assessments now say there is no evidence for it. The most economical reading of the whole picture is that Iran is claiming more capability than it is demonstrating — mines it cannot be shown to have laid, a list of 266 vessels it cannot inspect — which is what this file's Sunday lead and yesterday's attack-count lead both suggested from different directions.

What it means for you. An insurer telling ships to avoid the strait is the commercially meaningful event in this issue, more than the attack count. Believe the UKMTO warnings, the JMIC count, the IMO's 141 incidents and 25 deaths, the tugboat additions and the Swedish Club advisory. Treat Iranian mine claims as unsubstantiated, which is a change in this brief's own framing from Sunday. This brief still reads UKMTO only through wire reports, which is now its longest-standing owed item. What would settle it: whether any navy declares a mine danger area; whether the non-compliance list is published in full; whether war-risk premiums or transit counts move after the Swedish Club advisory; and whether the IMO fatality count keeps rising.

What to watch: transit volumes after the insurer advisory; war-risk premiums; the IMO's running incident and fatality tally; further blacklist categories; and any evidence for or against the mine claims.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — gCaptain citing UKMTO, JMIC, the IMO and a named analyst at Orbital Risk Intelligence, across two reports. This brief has not read the UKMTO or JMIC advisories, the IMO tally or the Swedish Club circular directly, and has no vessel identities for the four latest attacks. Alaska has taken its voter roll back from the Justice Department, which never flagged a single name

US politics & policy Continuation Single source

Alaska's outgoing lieutenant governor, who oversees its elections, notified the Justice Department on Monday that the state was withdrawing from a 2025 memorandum of understanding giving federal officials access to its voter rolls, effective immediately. “The circumstances have now changed,” Nancy Dahlstrom wrote. “The election has concluded and the agreement no longer serves a continuing purpose.” The announcement came as the state certified its 3 November results.

The two sentences that matter are the state's, about what the arrangement produced. “Since that time, the DOJ has not identified any potentially ineligible voters or raised concerns regarding Alaska's voter registration records.” “No voters were removed from Alaska's voter rolls as a result of the agreement.”

The national frame is larger than Alaska. The administration sued some 30 states and the District of Columbia after they declined to hand over unredacted voter rolls; 28 of those cases were dismissed, according to the Brennan Center. Last month a federal judge struck down the Justice Department's nationwide voter-list maintenance programme, ruling that it lacked legal authority to collect and assess state registration lists; the administration has appealed. At least 16 states complied with the demands. Alaska was one, and was sued by voting-rights advocates for doing so in alleged violation of its own constitution.

This closes a loop this file opened on 18 November and led on Saturday. Alaska's Senate race was the one in which the Justice Department sought to delay certification, sent dozens of FBI agents, issued hundreds of subpoenas, and then — once the count showed the margin exceeded the spoiler candidate's entire vote — moved to drop its own suit as moot. Democrats also won the governorship, ending nearly three decades of Republican control.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the programme was ever about ineligible voters. The administration's position, maintained through roughly thirty lawsuits, is that federal access to state rolls was necessary to find fraud. Alaska's own statement is that over the life of the agreement the Department identified nobody and raised no concerns. A dispute about facts, and one state has now published its answer.

Cause. One state's null result does not prove a national one, and this brief will not stretch it: Alaska has a small, unusually well-maintained roll, and an absence of findings there says little about Texas or Georgia. Reach for that before treating “zero” as the verdict on the whole programme. What the Alaska case does establish is the cost side of the ledger, which is checkable: a state entered the agreement, was sued by its own voters for doing so, received no findings in return, and withdrew the week it certified. And the timing is the tell. Dahlstrom's stated reason is that the election is over — which concedes that the agreement's purpose was tied to this election rather than to continuing list maintenance, the thing the programme was formally for. Set that beside the judge who struck the national programme down for want of legal authority, and the 28 dismissed suits, and the pattern is of an initiative that has been losing in courts and is now being unwound by the states that cooperated.

What it means for you. If you live in one of the sixteen states that complied, your state's roll was shared and may still be; Alaska's withdrawal is the first this brief has recorded. Believe the withdrawal, the quoted statements and the Brennan Center's count of dismissals. This brief has not read the memorandum, the withdrawal notice or the judge's ruling, and does not know which sixteen states complied. What would settle the direction: whether other complying states follow; what the appeal of the nationwide programme produces; whether the Alaska lawsuit by voting-rights advocates continues now that the agreement is gone; and whether the Department says what, if anything, it did with the data it already holds — which nothing in this report addresses.

What to watch: withdrawals by other states; the appeal of the struck-down national programme; the fate of the Alaska suit; and whether DOJ returns or deletes data already collected.

The man the government admitted it deported by mistake has left the country on his own terms

US politics & policy New subject Single source

Kilmar Abrego Garcia, whom the government illegally deported to El Salvador last year and later admitted in court filings it had removed because of a clerical error, voluntarily left the United States for Spain on Tuesday. Homeland Security announced the departure. He flew commercial with his wife, a US citizen, and his lawyer; ICE agents removed his ankle monitor at the airport. An advocacy group that supported the family said he “walked out of the United States a free man”; he plans international speaking engagements.

The sequence is worth setting out because each step was contested. He entered the country illegally around 2011. In 2019 an immigration judge barred his removal to El Salvador, finding a gang there had been “targeting him and threatening him with death because of his family's pupusa business”. He was nonetheless arrested in March last year and flown to El Salvador, where he was held in a mega-prison. A federal judge ordered the administration to work to bring him back; officials resisted for months. He was returned in June and arrested on human smuggling charges on arrival — charges a federal judge dismissed in May this year, citing a “tainted investigation” by the attorney general. The government appealed and said it would deport him to a third country in Africa, naming Uganda, Eswatini, Ghana and most recently Liberia. He is now in Spain, by his own choice.

Senator Chris Van Hollen, who flew to El Salvador and met him in prison: “A year and a half ago, the Trump administration wanted to leave Kilmar Abrego Garcia to rot in a Salvadoran gulag. Today, Kilmar is traveling to a country of his choice, a free man — unharmed and with dignity.”

The programme he became the face of has not stopped; it has gone quieter and got more expensive. The administration promised a million deportations in its first year and reported 605,000 in 2025. After confrontations between federal and state officials in several states — including video from Minneapolis of masked agents killing protesters — the White House retreated from the visible approach. Recent contract awards: nearly $1.3 billion for ICE deportation flights, at least $1.5 billion for border infrastructure, and a multimillion-dollar contract to transport detainees. That is the same ledger GAO audited on Thursday, and which this brief led on Monday.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What his departure means. Van Hollen's reading is vindication: he leaves free, unharmed, to a country of his choosing. The government's position, pursued through an appeal and four proposed African destinations, was that he should be removed regardless. A third reading neither side offers: a man who won in court twice and left anyway is not obviously a demonstration that the law worked. A dispute about significance.

Cause. The mundane explanation is the strongest and it is about cost rather than principle. He had a 2019 order barring removal to El Salvador, a dismissal of the criminal charges for a tainted investigation, and a court that had already ordered his return once. Against that the government had an appeal and a list of third countries with no apparent connection to him. Leaving voluntarily for Spain ends the government's leverage — there is nothing left to remove him from — and ends his exposure to being sent somewhere he has never been. Reach for that before reading either triumph or capitulation: both sides had reasons to want this over, and voluntary departure is the one outcome neither had to lose to get. What it does not do is resolve anything legally. No appellate court ruled on the third-country removals, no finding was made about the tainted investigation beyond the dismissal, and the policy of proposing removals to countries a person has no tie to is untouched.

What it means for you. The precedent he was going to set has not been set. Believe the departure, the clerical-error admission, the 2019 order, the May dismissal and the 605,000 figure — all on the record or in filings. This brief has read no filing in this case and is relying on one newsroom's account of a two-year history. What would settle the wider question: whether the appeal is dismissed as moot; whether any court rules on third-country removals to states with no connection to the person; whether DHS publishes a 2026 deportation total to set against 605,000; and whether the contracts above produce capacity the GAO audit says is not yet costed.

What to watch: mootness of the government's appeal; any ruling on third-country removals; the 2026 deportation count; and further ICE contract awards.

Five tons of lead azide will be detonated in Iowa this morning, for the first time since 1977

US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

The detonation this brief led on five days ago, after a judge paused it, takes place at 9am Central this morning at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown. A federal appeals court reinstated the EPA permit on Monday. FEMA has sent an emergency alert to phones in the area: it will contact residents within five miles to offer evacuation or relocation assistance, and tells people between five and twenty miles to stay indoors with windows closed. Col. Aaron Teller, commander of the 71st Explosive Ordnance Disposal Group, says the last Army lead azide detonation of this scale was in 1977.

The Army's containment measures, set out at a news conference in Middletown on Tuesday: the concrete storage magazines are already covered in dirt, and officials have added sand barriers, water and gel blocks to reduce how much lead escapes into the air. Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle: “A controlled mitigated detonation is far preferable for public health and safety than an uncontrolled detonation, which is a worst-case scenario.” And, unusually: “We don't want to give the public the feeling that there's absolutely no risk. The public needs to know what risk we understand and take precaution to protect themselves against any risk they perceive.”

The Army's account of why it cannot wait has also firmed up. It discovered in March that the lead azide — more than 60 barrels, in two bunkers the Army calls magazines — had been degrading, and notified the EPA that month. The remains of the US lead azide stockpile were moved to the Iowa facility around 2009; the ATF director says moving it again could cause an accidental explosion. The detonation plan was made public only five days before its original date. Environmental groups sued last week; the president said he also wanted agreement from Iowa's Democratic governor-elect.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is safe. The Army's position has shifted usefully since Friday, and the shift is the news: it no longer claims there is no risk. The environmental groups' position is that it risks fouling air, contaminating water and harming health, and that the process was rushed. A dispute about magnitude, now that both sides agree there is a non-zero risk.

Cause. The argument has narrowed to one that can actually be resolved, which is progress. Nobody disputes that degrading lead azide is dangerous to store or move, or that detonation disperses lead. The question is only whether a mitigated detonation disperses less than the alternative, and the alternative is an uncontrolled detonation at a time nobody chooses. Reach for that before reading the court's reinstatement of the permit as a political outcome: an appeals court restoring a permit on the merits is the ordinary machinery working, and the Army spent the intervening days adding mitigations rather than arguing. What has not improved is the process complaint this brief made on Friday, and the Army's own timeline now proves it: the material was known to be degrading in March, the EPA was notified in March, and the public was told five days before the blast. Eight months of planning, five days of notice.

What it means for you. If you are within twenty miles of Middletown, the operative instruction is the FEMA one: inside, windows closed. Believe the 9am time, the five- and twenty-mile zones, the 1977 comparison, the containment measures and the March discovery date. Nobody has published a predicted deposition figure, and this brief has still not seen the dispersion modelling it asked for on Friday. What would settle whether the mitigations worked: whether ambient lead monitoring was in place before the blast and whether its results are published; soil sampling afterwards; and whether anyone measures at four and eighteen miles, the distances at which residents quoted last week live and farm. Absent monitoring there will be no way to know, which is the single most important thing to watch today.

What to watch: whether the detonation proceeds at 9am; whether ambient monitoring results are published; soil sampling; the environmental groups' remaining claims; and the dispersion modelling, still unreleased.

A Brooklyn jury convicted Huawei on ten counts, eight years after the case was filed

Courts New subject Single source

A jury in Brooklyn federal court convicted Huawei on Tuesday on 10 of 12 criminal counts, including bank fraud, wire fraud, sanctions violations and conspiring to steal trade secrets. It acquitted the company of conspiring to obstruct justice and of racketeering conspiracy — the charge alleging it had operated as a criminal enterprise. The trial ran more than two months, with 60-plus prosecution witnesses and 15 for the defence. Sentencing is set for 14 September 2027. Penalties are not yet known.

The case is older than most of the policy built on top of it. It began in 2018; the United States put Huawei on a trade blacklist the following year. In December 2018 the founder's daughter and then chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou, was detained in Canada on a US warrant over allegedly misleading an HSBC banker about the company's Iran business, triggering a three-way diplomatic crisis; she fought extradition for nearly three years, entered a deferred prosecution agreement, and the charges were dropped. She did not testify; the HSBC banker she met in 2013 did. The 2018 indictment alleged Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas; charges added in 2020 alleged two decades of trade-secret theft. Prosecutors argued Huawei took router source code from Cisco, antenna technology from Quintel, and used a hired professor to reach a circuit board developed by CNEX.

Both sides claimed something. US Attorney Joseph Nocella: “Few crimes are as serious as Huawei's decades-long pattern of endangering US national security, violating the sanctity of US commerce, and impeding the enforcement of US laws.” Huawei's lawyer said the racketeering acquittal vindicated the company's “good-faith compliance culture” and that it will appeal; asked about penalties, he said the acquittal “could make a significant difference”. China's embassy did not respond.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What a conviction at this distance is worth. The prosecution's position is a decades-long pattern proved to a jury. The defence's position is that the one charge alleging the company was a criminal enterprise failed, which is the difference between bad conduct and a bad company. Experts quoted say the case did not undermine Huawei's business, which diversified and became central to China's AI push. A dispute about significance, and the commercial verdict was in long before the legal one.

Cause. Hold on to the dates. Filed 2018, blacklisted 2019, charges expanded 2020, tried in 2026, sentencing in September 2027 — nine years from indictment to sentence. The export-control architecture the United States built against Huawei was erected on allegations that only now have a verdict, and the company adapted in the meantime. Reach for that before reading the conviction as vindication of the policy or as proof it was unnecessary: the policy was always a separate instrument from the prosecution, which is precisely why it did not wait. What is genuinely new, and not much remarked, is the acquittal on racketeering — a jury that believed the bank fraud and the trade-secret theft declined to find the enterprise itself criminal, and the defence is already using that to argue down the penalty. Note the shape, because it recurs in this issue: an American prosecution of a Chinese technology company over Iran-related conduct and chip-adjacent trade secrets, concluding in the same week that an American chip company's chief executive was reinstated amid unanswered questions about export controls and China. This brief draws no equivalence between them and notes that the symmetry is doing no analytical work.

What it means for you. Nothing immediate: the blacklist predates the verdict and is unaffected by it, and sentencing is ten months away. Believe the counts, the acquittals, the trial length and the sentencing date. This brief has not read the indictment or the verdict form and does not know the maximum exposure on the ten counts. What would settle the consequence: the penalty in September 2027; whether the appeal reaches the trade-secret counts; whether China responds with measures against a US company; and whether the conviction features in the chip export-control bills now before Congress.

What to watch: the appeal; the September 2027 sentencing; any Chinese retaliation; and whether the verdict is cited in pending chip legislation.

Ukraine · Hacked Rosstat data puts confirmed Russian military deaths at 490,000, and the outlets that obtained it estimate the real figure above 720,000.

Russia's statistical bureau had confirmed 490,000 Russian troop deaths by the end of September, according to Rosstat data obtained by the opposition outlets Mediazona and Meduza after two pro-Ukrainian hacker groups breached its servers this month. Adding reporting lags and troops recorded as missing, the outlets put the total above 720,000 — about one death every four minutes since February 2022, and more than Russia lost in Afghanistan and both Chechen wars combined. Rosstat stopped publishing demographic data in 2025.

The method is the reason to take it seriously and also the reason to be careful. The reporters compared reported death dates against registration dates and against relatives' search notices — actuarial technique — to extrapolate roughly 230,000 unreported deaths. Mediazona's separate database of troops reported missing by relatives holds 160,000 names, of whom it estimates only about 10% survive. So 490,000 is a count and 720,000 is a model. The data also shows the war was the fourth most common cause of death in Russia since 2022, after heart disease, Covid and stroke, and that losses fall disproportionately on poorer regions and ethnic minorities. Below the line because the source is stolen data and neither CNN nor this brief can verify it.
