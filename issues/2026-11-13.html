Jensen Huang resigned over an allegation nobody in the US government will stand behind

AI & tech industry Continuation Independent corroboration

Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia on Thursday. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive while the board searches for a permanent replacement. Nvidia makes an estimated 90 per cent of the world's advanced AI chips. Huang had admitted on Sunday to being unfaithful to his wife and apologised to his family and to employees.

On the same day, the United States government declined to say the thing he resigned over was true. CNN reports that the intelligence collected to date does not prove that Alice Huang — no relation — was acting as a spy for Beijing, and that officials disagree about the strength of the evidence that she was recruited by the Ministry of State Security. Attorney General Todd Blanche, on Thursday: there are "no conclusions reached by the Department of Justice, or by me, or by [FBI] Director Patel around who she is or is not." Asked whether Huang was right to step down, the President said: "the big thing is, was the woman a spy? And I don't know if that's been determined yet."

One new fact changes the shape of this. The FBI has been investigating the affair for at least a year. That is not a reaction to last week's reporting; it means the relationship was known to the government long before it was known to the public, and that a year of investigation has produced no conclusion in either direction.

Divergence. A dispute about what he resigned for, and the two readings have different implications for every company in the same position. Read one: the security question. A chief executive with access to the most sensitive export-controlled technology in the economy had an undisclosed relationship with someone intelligence agencies were investigating; whether or not she was an agent, the exposure is disqualifying, and the board acted on risk rather than on proof. Read two: the disclosure failure. What is established is an affair, an apology, and a year-long FBI investigation he did not tell his board about; that is a governance breach regardless of the counterparty, and it is the thing a board can actually act on.

Cause of the divergence. The two readings are not rivals so much as a sequence, and the government's position is what makes the first one unavailable. An intelligence community that cannot agree she was recruited, an attorney general who says no conclusion has been reached, and a president who says he does not know, are not a basis for removing anyone. The board had the second reading available and did not need the first. Which also means the resignation establishes nothing about whether chips or secrets reached China — a point this brief has made for five days while the coverage ran ahead of the evidence, and which has now been confirmed by the attorney general rather than by this brief's scepticism.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the resignation as a fact about Nvidia's governance and not as a finding about espionage. The settling evidence is unchanged and still absent: a charge, an Entity List addition for Megaspeed, an indictment, or a licence revocation. Add one that is now available and dated: whether the FBI's year-old investigation produces anything now that its subject has left the company. The documented leakage this brief reported on Wednesday — a blacklisted Chinese defence university's own procurement record for two H200s — still has nothing to do with any of this.

What to watch: whether Kress is confirmed or an outside successor is named; any charge or Entity List action; whether the FBI investigation becomes public in any form; and what Nvidia discloses about what the board knew and when.

The diesel report arrived and said two opposite things

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

The EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report published at noon yesterday, a day late because the federal government was closed for Veterans Day. This brief named it on Wednesday as the discriminator for a six-week story and said what each outcome would mean. It has now been read, and it does not resolve cleanly.

For the week to 6 November: distillate stocks rose to 103,330 thousand barrels, a build of 4,071 — the second-largest weekly build of 2026, off the year's low of 99,259. Refinery utilisation rose to 95.2 per cent. Distillate product supplied fell to 3,411 thousand barrels a day, down 1,028 in a week. Exports fell to 1,198, down 143.

Wednesday's test was: if stocks build and utilisation holds, the weekly premium reading is confirmed. Both happened, and the retail premium did narrow, to $2.132 from $2.139.

The wholesale margin went the other way. New York Harbor ULSD fell to $4.618 a gallon from $4.757, but WTI fell much further, to $95.97 from $105.59. The crack spread therefore widened to $97.99 a barrel from $94.20. Crude at two-month lows made the refining margin bigger, not smaller.

Two cautions this brief owes on its own figures. Utilisation at 95.2 per cent is not a high: it ranks seventeenth of the forty-five weeks of 2026, whose maximum is 98.0. And the demand collapse is large but not unprecedented — the mean absolute week-on-week change in this series in 2026 is 398 and the largest is 1,151.

Divergence. A dispute about which number is the signal, and the week supplies one of each. Read one: the squeeze is ending. Stocks built 4.1 million barrels off the year's low, refineries ran harder, exports fell, and the retail premium turned. That is the whole mechanism of a tight market loosening, and all four moved together. Read two: nothing structural changed and demand did the work. A 1,028 fall in product supplied in one week is most of the build by itself; if demand bounces back, the stock build reverses and the premium resumes.

Cause of the divergence. The two readings are separable by a known property of the series, and the chart shows it: weekly product supplied is noisy, swinging hundreds of thousands of barrels a day without anything happening in the world. One week below the mean is not a demand trend. What is not noise is the crack spread, and it moved against the loosening story: the margin widened by $3.79 while crude fell $9.62. Refiners captured the whole of the crude fall and more. That is the mechanism this brief described on 8 November — US Gulf Coast refiners taking the discount — still running at full strength in the same week the retail premium eased.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Take the stock build as real, the demand drop as probably noise, and the margin as not yet unwinding. The retail premium narrowing is mostly gasoline falling faster, not diesel relief. The settling observation is next Wednesday's pair: whether product supplied reverts toward 3,837 while stocks keep building. If both happen the squeeze is genuinely ending; if stocks fall back as demand returns, this week was a demand artefact and the six-week story is unchanged. A cold snap is forecast for the eastern United States, which is the obvious reason demand would revert hard.

What to watch: next Wednesday's product supplied and stocks together; the crack spread; whether the retail premium keeps narrowing; and heating degree days in the Northeast, which this brief still does not track.

The two-year has risen 40 basis points in five sessions

Macro & markets Continuation Primary source

Treasury's 12 November curve, read here: two-year 4.86 per cent, five-year 4.95, ten-year 5.10, twenty-year 5.48, thirty-year 5.43. Against 10 November — there was no curve on the 11th, the bond market being closed for Veterans Day — that is +13 basis points at two years, +14 at five, +12 at ten and +8 at thirty.

The run matters more than the day. On 5 November, in the flight to quality during the \$2.5 trillion equity rout, the two-year closed at 4.46. It is now 4.86. That is 40 basis points in five trading sessions, and it leaves the two-year within six basis points of its 2026 high of 4.92, set on 28 September.

Two other things in the same numbers. The ten-year is back above 5 per cent, at 5.10 — though well below its own 2026 high of 5.31 on 5 October. And the long-end hump this brief has tracked since late October has compressed: the twenty-year is 38 basis points above the ten-year, from 43 on 6 November and 41 on Tuesday.

Divergence. A dispute about what the curve is now saying, and the front and the back disagree. Read one: a Fed repricing, full stop. Core CPI printed 0.3 against 0.2 expected, a governor floated a 50 basis point rise for December, and the two-year — the maturity that tracks policy expectations — has moved almost twice as far from its low as the thirty-year. That is the market pricing tighter policy and nothing else. Read two: the back end is telling a different story. If this were only about near-term policy the long end would move less and the hump would be unaffected; instead the hump has compressed five basis points in a week, which is a change in how the twenty-year is being valued relative to the ten.

Cause of the divergence. The two readings can be separated by what the hump was ever about, and this brief has never established that. It has carried the twenty-year's premium over the ten-year for three weeks, corrected itself once for calling it a record, and said repeatedly that it did not know whether the cause was supply, duration demand or the twenty-year's own liquidity. On Wednesday it wrote that a hump surviving a 17 basis point move in the two-year could not be a function of policy expectations. It has now not survived the next move, which is evidence against that reasoning and in favour of the simpler reading: the whole curve is repricing and the hump is going with it.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat this as a policy repricing that is now reaching the long end, and treat the brief's own three weeks of hump commentary as having been over-read. The settling events are dated: the next core CPI print, and the December meeting, which now carries a live 50 basis point possibility. The near-term marker is whether the two-year takes out 4.92; if it does, the market is pricing more than one further rise.

What to watch: whether the two-year exceeds 4.92; the next core CPI; whether other governors adopt Waller's 0.3 threshold; and whether the hump keeps compressing, which would retire a thread this brief has carried without ever explaining it.

A projectile hit a tanker's engine room off the UAE, and twenty-two crew got off

Geopolitics Continuation Single source

The Liberia-flagged crude tanker Dominica Prosperity was struck by an unidentified projectile at about 1800 UTC on 11 November, roughly 14 nautical miles north-west of Ras Al Khaimah in the United Arab Emirates, near Mina Saqr. The projectile hit the engine room. All 22 crew were evacuated while emergency crews fought a fire aboard. The account is UK Maritime Trade Operations', reported by the trade press.

It is one of four tanker incidents in the latest maritime security advisory covering the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz, including a separate strike that injured multiple seafarers.

Yesterday this brief led on the gap between a de-escalating oil market and a war that has not stopped at sea, and noted that 37 ships were attacked in October, the highest monthly total of the war. Four incidents in one advisory, with a named vessel, a named position and a crew count, is a sharper version of the same thing.

There is a point about this brief's own sourcing here. It has reported on most mornings since 11 October that UKMTO is unreachable — the site returns 403 — and treated that as the reason it has no instrument for the Strait. UKMTO's advisories have been reaching the trade press the whole time, with vessel names and positions, on a front page this brief reads daily. An inaccessible website is not an inaccessible source.

Divergence. A dispute about who is doing this, and the reporting does not resolve it. Read one: the war's attribution holds. Attacks in the Gulf and the Strait through this conflict have been attributed to Iran or to forces aligned with it, and an unidentified projectile into a tanker's engine room near the Emirati coast fits that pattern. Read two: unidentified means unidentified. The advisory does not attribute, the vessel is Liberia-flagged with no stated owner or charterer, and this stretch of water has seen limpet mines, drones and missiles from more than one quarter over several years.

Cause of the divergence. Attribution of attacks at sea is slow because the physical evidence is on a damaged ship, and the pattern that would substitute for evidence is weaker than it looks. What distinguishes this incident is where it happened: Ras Al Khaimah is well inside the Gulf and close to the Emirati coast, not in the Strait itself. An attack on a tanker near a UAE port is a different act from one on a transiting vessel, because of whose waters it is in and whose protection failed.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Take the vessel, the time, the position, the engine-room strike and the 22 evacuated crew as established, and the attribution as open. What would settle it is the UAE's own statement, the owner's, or UKMTO's follow-up advisory. This brief's practical conclusion is about itself: the Washington Institute's monthly count and UKMTO's advisories via the trade press are both usable, both were available throughout the month it reported having no instrument, and both should be in the morning pull from today.

What to watch: any attribution; the condition of the vessel and crew; the other three incidents in the advisory; whether UAE ports change their guidance; and war-risk premiums for Gulf calls.

This brief did not reach UKMTO directly, which returns 403 here, and has no owner, operator or flag-state statement; the four-incident total and the injury report in the separate strike are as described in the advisory account. Outlook and Teams went down worldwide and stayed down

AI & tech industry New Independent corroboration

A Microsoft outage has left people in many countries without Outlook, Teams and other core office software. Workers at some American companies went home early on Thursday. In Australia, businesses could not print invoices and some schools could not hold online classes on Friday. As of 3:30 GMT on Friday it was still widespread.

The company's own updates are the most informative part. At 01:59 GMT: "We're seeing positive results with our efforts to restart affected infrastructure and recover server availability in an isolated manner." At 03:13 GMT: "We've monitored the status of the changes applied to improve serviceability and confirmed they're not taking as expected." And then: "We're identifying alternate workstreams that can be applied to increase capacity in the face of sustained traffic management imbalances."

Divergence. A dispute about what those updates mean, which is itself the story. Read one: a capacity problem, as described. "Sustained traffic management imbalances" and "increase capacity" describe a system that cannot serve its load — a restart that is not holding because demand overwhelms the recovered servers. On this reading it resolves when capacity is added, and the sequence of updates is an engineering team narrating a slow recovery. Read two: the language is doing concealment work. Three updates in ninety minutes that say progress, then not-progress, then a change of approach, in vocabulary that avoids naming a cause, is what organisations write when the cause is either unknown or unsayable.

Cause of the divergence. Nothing in the public record distinguishes them, and that is the point worth making rather than guessing. Microsoft has not said what failed. The difference between a configuration change, a capacity shortfall, a dependency failure and something adversarial is the difference between a bad night and a serious event, and the words "traffic management imbalances" are compatible with all four.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the outage and its scale as established and the cause as unstated, not unknown-to-Microsoft. What would settle it is the post-incident review, which Microsoft publishes for outages of this size and which is the only document that matters here. The broader fact is the one this brief should hold onto: schools, businesses and governments in several countries stopped working because one company's email and meeting software stopped, which is a concentration observation and not a technology story.

What to watch: the post-incident review and whether it names a cause; the total duration; whether any regulator asks; and whether the concentration question reaches any government that was affected.

Confidence: Independent corroboration for the outage — CNN and Al Jazeera — with the quoted updates from Microsoft's own posts as reported by CNN. This brief did not read Microsoft's service health page directly and has no figure for how many users were affected; "untold millions" is CNN's phrase and this brief has not substituted a number for it. Spain passed housing reforms the day after four million people struck

World economy Continuation Single source

Yesterday this brief led on Spain's first general strike since 2012 — four million workers by the unions' count, over housing affordability, three weeks before a snap election — and asked whether it was a strike against the government or a strike the government wanted.

The answer arrived in a day. On Thursday the Spanish parliament passed measures limiting evictions and real estate speculation. The interim lower house ratified them. Their duration depends on the outcome of the election on 29 November.

A general strike on Wednesday and legislation on Thursday is not the usual tempo of either.

Divergence. A dispute about what one day's turnaround proves. Read one: the strike worked. Four million people stopped work and the legislature acted within twenty-four hours; that is industrial action producing law, and it is rare enough to be worth saying plainly. Read two: the legislation was already there. Nothing passes a parliament in a day. A bill ratified on Thursday was drafted, negotiated and scheduled before Wednesday, which means the strike and the vote were two parts of one sequence rather than cause and effect.

Cause of the divergence. The second reading is almost certainly right on the mechanics and the first may still be right on the politics. What makes the question answerable is the detail this brief flagged yesterday and now has: the measures' duration depends on the election. A reform passed by an interim chamber with an expiry tied to a vote three weeks away is not primarily a housing policy; it is a commitment device, designed to make the election a referendum on whether the measures survive. That is consistent with yesterday's second reading — a government trying to make housing the ballot question rather than corruption — and the speed is evidence for it, not against.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the measures as passed and temporary, and the strike-to-law sequence as coordinated rather than causal. What would settle the political question is the polling: whether housing displaces corruption as the top issue between now and 29 November. This brief has no Spanish-language source, no text of the measures, and no polling of its own — the same three gaps it recorded yesterday, unclosed.

What to watch: the measures' text and expiry mechanism; whether polling moves to housing; the election on 29 November; and whether the unions call the result a win.

Confidence: Single source — Al Jazeera with AFP and AP. This brief read no Spanish-language source, has no text of the legislation, no vote count, and the reading of the expiry as a commitment device is its own inference. Sources: Al Jazeera · 12 November
