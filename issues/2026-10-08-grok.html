The Fed’s minutes say another hike is “likely” by year end; the 10-year auction cleared at 5.3%, the highest since 2000, and Fed staff partly blamed Treasury’s own buyback programme for the yield surge

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal New fact

The minutes of the 15–16 September meeting (released 2pm Wednesday): “most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end.” They also said decisions would depend on incoming data. The vote for the quarter-point hike was unanimous. Of the 18 officials who submitted projections, 16 expect another increase; the median shows one more this year and none in 2027. Chair Warsh has not submitted a projection since May. Next decisions: 28 October and 9 December.

The detail that matters for the debt beat: officials attributed the rise in yields to expected Fed hikes, the AI build-out and solid growth, and staff said some of it may have come from “uncertainty related to the U.S. Treasury’s announcement and implementation of the buyback program”, the long-bond buybacks Secretary Bessent stepped up in August to bring yields down. Treasury runs a buyback of at least $4bn in 20–30 year paper today, double the normal size, alongside the $22bn 30-year reopening.

The auction answered yesterday’s question. Treasury sold $39bn of 10-year notes at 5.3%, the highest auction yield since 2000, but demand was strong: indirect bidders, which include foreign central banks, took 80.3% (ten-auction average 72.4%) and dealers were left with 2.5% (average 9.4%). Intraday the 10-year touched 5.35%, its highest since 2002. Treasury’s par curve closed Wednesday at 5.28% (5.27% Tuesday), the 20-year 5.71% and the 30-year 5.67%. Thursday morning, with Brent up 5%, the Guardian has the 10-year at 5.33%.

Households: the New York Fed’s September consumer survey shows one-year inflation expectations at their highest since May 2023, and more households saying they are worse off than a year ago.

Congress’s permitting deal would make big data centers pay the full grid cost of serving them, reversing a decades-old federal pricing rule; a Harvard grid lawyer says “I don’t think we’ve seen it before”

AI & tech Energy & grid US politics & policy New fact

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, introduced by Senators Heinrich (D-N.M.), Lee (R-Utah), Capito (R-W.Va.) and Whitehouse (D-R.I.), the chairs and ranking members of the Energy and Environment committees, is a 417-page permitting bill. It puts short clocks on lawsuits, makes issued permits harder to pull, and strengthens FERC’s backstop authority to site interstate transmission lines. Heinrich wants it on the Senate floor in November, which means the lame duck.

The data-centre title is what Axios calls a new power regime. Data centres would pay both their share of the existing transmission network and the new transmission needed to serve them, which reverses a long-standing federal rule that generally bars charging both. New loads of 20 MW or more would bear the incremental generation, storage, transmission and distribution costs they cause. Regulators could charge them more than that and use the excess to cut other customers’ bills. They would keep paying if they leave early, and must post financial guarantees before a utility builds for them. States could go further, including allocating power competitively or capping use.

Reactions split predictably. Amazon says the bill “could unlock the investment needed”. An unnamed industry official calls it an “unprecedented level of discriminatory treatment” and warns that developers may go off-grid instead. A former Trump energy official now at Cato offers “mild support”. The Data Center Coalition is still reviewing it.

Reading: this is the first federal attempt to put a price on the AI build-out’s grid burden, not just ask for pledges. It lands as the minutes cite AI capex as a source of higher yields (lead 2).

GAO rules HHS broke the Impoundment Control Act by sitting on $78m for health-care research after firing the staff who ran the grants

US debt & fiscal US politics & policy New fact

The Government Accountability Office’s decision, released Wednesday: HHS “withheld funds when it declined to obligate or expend more than $78 million of its FY 2025 appropriation” for the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality before the fiscal year closed, and that was “an impoundment under the ICA rather than a programmatic delay.” Because HHS never sent Congress a special message proposing to rescind the money, it violated the Impoundment Control Act.

The evidence GAO cites: a large unobligated balance near year end, cancelled grant peer-review sessions, a July 2025 reduction in force that removed the grant staff, and managers’ own admissions of funding uncertainty. On the lump-sum defence, GAO wrote that an agency cannot withhold money “simply because they were appropriated as part of a lump-sum appropriation.” A GAO spokesperson noted the Comptroller General may sue to compel obligation if the funds are not released. House Appropriations Democrats say GAO issued multiple decisions on Wednesday.

Why it connects: the FY-end pocket-rescission message this brief covered on 30 September included about $28m of AHRQ’s FY2026 money (of $345m appropriated), per Bloomberg Law. Grant recipients are suing separately, and the government’s response in that case was due Wednesday. A finding is not a court order; the question is whether GAO actually sues.

Cantwell. The top Democrat on Senate Commerce released a six-principle frontier-AI framework: enforceable safety standards written by NIST; continuous independent testing; transparency, incident reporting and liability; public-private partnerships; protections for children, workers and consequential decisions; and international standards work, including a secure US–China incident channel. OpenAI released 722 machine-written maths manuscripts from an unreleased model, and one mathematician says a Riemann-linked result would earn a human a Fields Medal

AI & tech Lab safety New fact

On Tuesday OpenAI published 722 manuscripts in 372 “families” of results on long-standing problems, generated by a powerful model it has not released, and invited researchers to check and build on them. A Rutgers mathematician said on X that a result connected to the Riemann hypothesis would warrant an automatic Fields Medal if a human had done it.

The scepticism is real. Some mathematicians question whether these results, and one OpenAI released last month, are original or lean heavily on prior human work. Stephen Wolfram: “You can make a trillion theorems easily. The problem is most of those theorems are not ones that anybody will care about.” Axios’s point is that maths, like code, lets you check whether the machine is right, because proofs can increasingly be verified line by line by computer.

Why it is Core: a capability jump from a model held back from release is the kind of event the lab-safety thread exists for. It comes the same week as the reported training pause and Robinson’s exit (Issue 33-grok). What is unknown is whether the model gets an external evaluation before release.

The AI build-out moves into debt: SpaceX is arranging $40bn of GPU-backed borrowing through Apollo as AI bonds sell off; TSMC and Samsung show where the money goes

Reading for financials: collateralising loans with a depreciating, fast-obsoleting asset is the credit question of this cycle. If the seven-year assumption proves wrong, the losses land with private-credit and IG investors, not just equity.

Prediction markets reach the Supreme Court: the NFL says they are gambling for states to police, as NPR finds their federal regulator has 21% fewer staff and made 80% fewer enforcement cases

US financials US politics & policy New fact

In an amicus brief supporting New Jersey regulators against Kalshi, the NFL argues sports event contracts are gambling, not swaps under exclusive federal (CFTC) jurisdiction. It says football was $1.8bn of prediction-market volume on the season’s first Sunday, more than half the total. It wants bans on manipulable contracts (missed field goals, injuries, officiating) and a minimum age of 21; Kalshi allows 18. Federal appeals courts have split on whether state gambling law applies.

NPR: by the end of 2025 the CFTC had 21% fewer staff than its ten-year average, and it brought nearly 80% fewer enforcement actions in 2025 than its decade average (agency annual reports, OPM data). A former enforcement lawyer says staff behind the Biden-era crypto cases were pushed out. The CFTC says it “remains committed” to market integrity. The NFL’s own line: “given the current resource constraints of the CFTC, this is a job better left to the states.”
