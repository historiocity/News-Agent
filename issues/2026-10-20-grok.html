Why the barrel is tight: Iran’s exports are at zero. Ship-tracking and EU Sentinel imagery show no tanker has docked at Kharg Island’s crude terminals since late August — the longest idle stretch since the war began in February. Iranian crude exports fell from almost 1 million b/d in July to nothing after the US reimposed its blockade in mid-July (Bloomberg via gCaptain, read; Kpler/Vortexa data). Jask is empty too. Chinese refiners that took most of Iran’s ~650m barrels in the year to April are now bidding for other cargoes and ships — the channel into the US price.

The ship shortage, and Washington’s answer. More than 40% of the world’s ~850 VLCCs are in the Gulf or within a couple of days of it (Signal Ocean), and Gulf–East Asia freight costs more than six times its pre-war level. DHS orders every civilian agency to strip out DeepSeek — including third-party products built on it

AI & tech US politics & policy New fact

The order. DHS on Monday made public an order requiring federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek-related technology from their IT systems and barring them from buying DeepSeek products or services. It reaches “third-party products and services developed using the company’s technology,” not just the chatbot app. It names Treasury, State and Justice among covered agencies and excludes the Department of Defense. The order is dated 24 July and was published only now. Agencies have 60 days from issuance to ask for exceptions or delays in writing (CNBC, read; Reuters reporting relayed).

The stated grounds. DHS says DeepSeek models “present multiple threat vectors”: possible access to sensitive data by Chinese authorities, transfer of government data to China, and malware or other vulnerabilities. It cites open-source research on critical flaws in DeepSeek’s iOS app. Beijing’s foreign ministry said it “consistently opposes the over-generalization of national security.”

Why it matters beyond the headline. Earlier curbs were patchwork: DISA blocked access (January 2025), Commerce bureaus banned it on devices (March 2025), and Congress restricted it in intelligence systems (December 2025). This is the first government-wide civilian rule, and the third-party clause is the part with teeth. Any contractor product that fine-tuned or wrapped an open-weight DeepSeek model now has a federal compliance problem. Ask your vendors.

What to watch: whether the text is posted as a CISA directive with a definition of “developed using”; which agencies seek exceptions; any matching move by DoD, which was carved out.

Prosecutors say the OpenAI bugging suspect was in WhatsApp contact with a Saudi royal; judge denies bail despite a Saudi offer to pay it

AI & tech Lab security New fact

The ruling. Magistrate Judge Alex Tse (N.D. Cal.) ordered Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, held without bail Monday, citing a “significant” flight risk. Alfozan is a former OpenAI engineer, since fired, accused of planting a recording device in a television in an executive conference room at OpenAI’s San Francisco headquarters on 28 Sep. The Saudi consulate in Los Angeles had offered to pay bail in full. Tse: “It seems like a blank check… what confidence do I have that they’re going to protect the interests of the [U.S.] government?” Saudi Arabia has no extradition treaty with the US (CNBC, read).

What the government filed. The Sunday detention memo says Alfozan was in “frequent communications via WhatsApp with a member of the Saudi Royal Family” through 2025–26, most recently 5 Oct, four days before his arrest. The royal is unnamed. In May the person told him “Saudi needs you more then [sic] the US!” and he replied “100.” Prosecutors say OpenAI executives met government officials in the same room on days close to the alleged planting, and that the case “may yet result in more serious charges.” The current count carries a five-year maximum (CNBC, read; BBC, read).

Why it clears the bar. This is the first criminal case to put a foreign government’s alleged support next to a physical intrusion at a frontier lab. It lands in the same weeks the White House is building lab-oversight machinery (lead 4). It also lands as Riyadh buys US weapons in bulk and pitches its own AI programme. Altman told OpenAI staff there are three endgames for AI safety — and he favours an IAEA for AI

AI & tech Lab safety Single-sourced Conflict disclosed

Sourcing and conflict

Every account traces to one report, The Information on 19 Oct, citing people familiar with the remarks. OpenAI has not commented. Relays read: Dealroom, Yonhap, Times of India. The story names Anthropic, which makes Claude, an industry rival of this edition’s writer. Run under the standing rule: disclose, don’t soften.

The three paths (as reported). (1) An international agency modelled on the IAEA, enforcing safety standards on frontier labs. Two people said Altman appeared to favour this one. (2) The “Demis plan”: the big labs merge into, or work through, one CERN-like body that supplies models to participating countries. (3) The “Eliezer plan”: a lab that reaches recursive self-improvement uses its economic power to starve rivals of compute — in Altman’s reported words, “melt everyone else’s GPUs.” He reportedly said “obviously I think that one’s terrible,” and included it for completeness.

Why it matters to the US policy fight. None of the ideas is new; the first two appear in OpenAI’s May 2023 “Governance of superintelligence” post. The setting is new. Relays report that the White House’s Super Intelligence Force adopted its charter last week, with labs required to report and fix rogue-model incidents. Anthropic is circulating a draft federal bill (Issue 44-grok). And the labs are said to be negotiating an industry standards body while disagreeing over who tests models. Altman reportedly told staff OpenAI must choose within a year which path to push. Option 1 needs China in the room. Option 2 is an antitrust question as much as a safety one.

What to watch: any on-record OpenAI statement; whether the Super Intelligence Force charter is published as a primary document (not yet located by this brief).

JPMorgan put $5 billion into defence and supply-chain start-ups in a year — and may go past its $10 billion cap

US financials Geopolitics New fact

The number. JPMorgan said Monday it made $5 billion of start-up investments in the first year of its Security and Resiliency Initiative. It had pledged $10 billion over a decade. Co-President Doug Petno told CNBC the bank may expand past that figure. Most stakes are undisclosed. Two known ones: Hadrian (automated defence-parts factories; JPM co-led a $1.4bn round in August) and Castelion (low-cost “Blackbeard” hypersonic missile; $1bn round). The investment arm is run by former Berkshire executive Todd Combs, who reports to Dimon. Separately, the bank says it has financed or facilitated $230bn+ toward a $1.5 trillion ten-year target, and has extended the initiative to Europe and Canada (CNBC, read).

Why it matters. The largest US bank is acting as a direct equity investor in weapons and industrial start-ups. That is a departure from the lender-and-adviser model; JPMorgan spun off One Equity Partners more than a decade ago. Petno, widely seen as a Dimon successor candidate, runs the programme. Credit exposure, equity-risk capital treatment and conflicts with advisory clients are the questions a regulator would ask. None was answered Monday.

Also on JPMorgan (X summary, not verified to a primary): a reported ₦50bn deposit with Nigeria’s central bank toward a merchant-banking licence. Held at this label.

DOJ charges 28 noncitizens with election crimes in California and asks a court to stop the state processing flagged ballots

US politics & policy Courts New fact

The actions. The Justice Department on Monday announced charges against 28 noncitizens (green-card holders, per the Guardian) for election crimes in California. It also filed an emergency request in federal court to stop California from processing ballots returned by registered voters the federal government calls “likely noncitizens.” The state says it has asked how those conclusions were reached and hasn’t been told. Background: last week DOJ delivered a flash drive of 20,000 names to Los Angeles County with a 48-hour deadline. Local and state officials declined and DOJ sued. On Friday Acting Deputy AG Trent McCotter warned officials they could be prosecuted for knowingly processing such ballots (NPR, read).

Why it clears the bar. This is substance, not tactics. A federal court is being asked to intervene in a state’s ballot processing two weeks before the 3 Nov election, and election officials have been threatened with prosecution. NPR notes noncitizen voting has never been found to be widespread. Election-law scholar Rick Hasen names the risks as disenfranchising eligible voters and building a record to contest results. A Republican county recorder in Arizona says officials nationwide are watching. This connects to the DHS election-administration thread (§203 jurisdictions list, opened 20 Sep).

What to watch: the court’s ruling on the emergency request; whether DOJ discloses the matching method; copycat demands to other states.
