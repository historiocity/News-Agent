The Supreme Court left the mail-ballot rule blocked for the midterms

US politics & policy Trigger fired Primary source

On Monday the Supreme Court denied the government’s application for a stay in Postal Service v. California, No. 26A305. The unsigned order leaves intact District Judge Indira Talwani’s preliminary injunction against the Postal Service’s Ballot Mail for Federal Elections rule. The government’s challenge is “unlikely to succeed on the merits,” the Court said, and the equitable factors for emergency relief do not favour a stay.

Justice Kavanaugh concurred: he sees a fair prospect the final rule falls within USPS’s statutory authority under 39 U.S.C. §401(2), but applying it in the 2026 elections would be arbitrary and capricious under the APA because state and local officials lack enough time to implement it. Justice Alito, joined by Justice Thomas, dissented.

The rule would have required election-mail logos, high-speed-scannable envelopes, a specialised barcode unique to each voter, and a state upload of voter information to a Postal Service portal — with noncompliant outbound ballots returned. Mail ballots have already gone out in some states. For this brief the operative fact is the wake trigger: the order, not another day of a quiet docket.

What to watch: the First Circuit merits track; whether the administration tries a narrower rule for a later cycle; any parallel DHS citizenship-list work under the March executive order that is still live outside this stay.

Dig deeper — why the absence of an administrative stay was already decisive, and the order locks it

Yesterday’s Grok edition carried 26A305 below the line because the docket had been silent since the 10 September reply — four days with no administrative stay, which meant Talwani’s PI continued to operate for November. Monday’s denial converts that interim posture into the Court’s own equitable judgment for this cycle. Kavanaugh’s concurrence matters for the next round: he separates statutory authority from timing, so a refiled rule with a longer runway is not foreclosed by this order even if the 2026 application is dead.

Sources: Supreme Court order, 26A305 (14 Sep, primary) · Reuters · CBS News · text.npr.org Tuesday sweep

Today, every F, J and I visa stops being open-ended

US politics & policy Threshold event Primary source

Weekday discipline

Earlier issues briefly called 15 September a Monday. It is a Tuesday. The Federal Register effective date (2026-14439) was always 09/15/2026; only the weekday label was wrong. Corrected here and in the 13 Sep second edition.

DHS final rule FR 2026-14439 takes effect today. Admission for “duration of status” ends for the F (academic student), J (exchange visitor) and I (foreign media) classifications. New admissions get a fixed date: programme end on the I-20 or DS-2019, capped at four years, plus a 30-day grace period (F grace falls from 60 to 30 days). I holders get up to 240 days (90 for most PRC passport holders), or the assignment length if shorter.

People already here on D/S transition without an immediate filing: they may remain to programme end or four years from today, whichever is first, without a corrected I-94. After that, staying means an extension of stay. No stay application was located as of this morning’s sweep.

The omission that still matters: neither this rule nor the 11 Sep grace-period NPRM (comments close 10 Nov) commits USCIS to any adjudication time on extensions — the concession that would make either workable.

What to watch: first wave of I-539 filings; any CRA joint resolution; employer-coalition comments on the grace-period NPRM; a suit seeking to stay either rule.

Dig deeper — why a default change is steeper than a eligibility change

Under D/S, falling out of compliance generally required a finding. Under a fixed date, overstaying is a calendar fact and unlawful presence accrues automatically — which is what drives the three- and ten-year bars. Doctoral programmes and medical residencies routinely run past four years; the filing volume arrives for a population that has never had to file. After today this thread goes quiet until the November comment close unless something is filed.

Sources: FR 2026-14439 (primary) · DHS Study in the States quick facts · Mayer Brown

EPA finalised the repeal of the 2024 power-plant carbon standards

US politics & policy Energy & grid New subject Negative check

On Monday EPA finalised a partial repeal of the Biden-era 2024 Carbon Pollution Standards for fossil fuel-fired electric generating units — existing-unit emission guidelines and the 90% carbon-capture requirements for certain coal and new baseload gas plants among the pieces removed. EPA’s own release puts estimated compliance-cost savings above $300 billion. Simultaneously it proposed rescinding remaining power-sector GHG standards under Clean Air Act §111 (including subparts covering new and existing fossil units); that broader repeal is not final and faces a comment period and hearing.

Mercury, lead and other non-GHG toxics rules are described by major outlets as remaining in place. The grid/AI channel for this brief: large-load interconnection and who pays for generation are already live at FERC and in the House Ratepayer bill; removing federal GHG performance standards changes the build economics for the same thermal fleet that still sets the margin when data-centre load arrives.

What to watch: the comment window on the residual repeal proposal; state attorney-general suits; whether any ISO/RTO response to FERC’s large-load show-cause cites the changed federal GHG posture.

The banking agencies put third-party risk guidance in today’s Federal Register

US financials New subject Primary source

OCC Bulletin 2026-46 and the joint Fed/FDIC/NCUA package propose revised interagency third-party risk management guidance, published into today’s Federal Register issue (document 2026-18859). Comments run 60 days from FR publication. The proposal would replace the 2023 interagency guidance; it is explicitly nonbinding — non-compliance with the guidance alone is not a basis for supervisory action — and emphasises tailoring to each relationship’s assessed risk and to the bank’s size and complexity rather than treating every vendor as high-risk.

Season matters: the same fortnight that raised the on-site exam threshold from $3bn to $6bn (188 additional institutions) is now rewriting how banks oversee the vendors that concentrate operational risk — including core processors and, increasingly, AI/cloud providers. A separate joint statement on community banks and core service providers accompanies the proposal.

What to watch: comment-file tone from bank trades vs fintech vendors; whether final guidance stays nonbinding; any exam manual incorporation that would harden it in practice.

Sources: FR 2026-18859 (primary) · OCC Bulletin 2026-46 · FDIC joint press release
