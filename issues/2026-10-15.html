Inflation is 3.7% and core inflation is 2.6%, and the entire gap is energy

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal New thread Primary source

From the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ September Consumer Price Index, released Wednesday 14 October at 8:30am: all items +0.6% on the month and +3.7% over twelve months. All items less food and energy — core — +0.3% and +2.6%. Food was unchanged on the month and +2.5% on the year. Shelter +0.4% and +3.2%.

Now the energy line, which is the whole story. Energy +5.2% on the month and +20.4% over twelve months. Gasoline +9.1% and +34.2%. Fuel oil +15.3% on the month and +74.2% over twelve months.

That last figure is the one this brief has reported a day at a time since September without being able to say what it meant for the price level. It can now. The 1.1-point gap between headline and core is energy and almost nothing else — food is flat, shelter close to core. Strip energy out and this is a 2.6% economy; leave it in and it is a 3.7% one.

The independent check works. AAA’s national diesel average is $6.3173 against $3.6554 a year ago — up 72.8%, against BLS fuel oil at +74.2%. Two series, different methods and samples, within a point and a half — about as close as a card-reader average and a household survey get.

And the print has already done its largest piece of work. The Social Security Administration announced the same day that the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment is 3.6%, computed from CPI-W between the third quarter of 2025 and that of 2026. It reaches nearly 72 million beneficiaries from January 2027 and 7.3 million SSI recipients from 31 December, worth about $75 a month on the average cheque; the taxable maximum rises from $184,500 to $192,300. The 2026 adjustment was 2.8%; this is the largest since 2023’s 8.7%.

What to watch: whether core keeps running near 2.6% while headline is dragged by energy, which is the gap the Federal Reserve must decide whether to look through; the October print, which captures this month’s renewed diesel rise; and whether any part of the energy index turns.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a dispute between outlets — a dispute inside the number, and one with a real answer. Headline 3.7% and core 2.6% are both correct descriptions of September, and they imply opposite policies. The case for looking through it: energy is volatile, the Federal Reserve’s mandate is best served by the measure that predicts next year’s inflation, and core does that better. The case against: a 34% annual rise in petrol and a 74% rise in fuel oil are not noise to a household, they feed into every delivered good with a lag, and an energy shock sustained for a year stops being transitory by definition. The honest adjudication is that core is the better forecast and headline is the better description of what happened to people, and anyone who cites only one of them is making an argument rather than reporting a number.

Cause. The energy move is not mysterious and this brief has reported its components daily: a chokepoint conflict, a hurricane that shut two-thirds of Gulf production for a week, and refinery outages. What is worth naming is the timing of the measurement. The COLA is computed from the third quarter — July, August, September — which is precisely the quarter the energy shock ran through. Had the shock arrived in October, the 2027 adjustment would have been materially lower and beneficiaries would have waited a year for it. That is not design; it is the calendar, and it cuts the other way just as often.

What it means for you. Two concrete things. First, if you receive Social Security the 3.6% is now fixed — it does not change if energy prices fall in November, and it is worth about $75 a month from January. Second, and less comfortable: the same arithmetic means your 2027 increase was set by a quarter in which your energy costs rose far faster than 3.6%. The COLA tracks a basket in which energy is roughly 7%; if energy is a larger share of your spending than that — rural, long commute, oil heat — the adjustment undercompensates you, and the fuel oil line at +74.2% is the measure of by how much. For everyone else the thing to watch is the gap, not the level: core at 2.6% is the number the Fed will act on, and headline at 3.7% is the number that will be quoted at it. When those two diverge by more than a point, the argument about monetary policy stops being about data and starts being about which index you choose.

A US agency has proposed charging every Spanish ship $200,000 a voyage and restricting Spanish imports

World economy Geopolitics New thread Underreported Primary source

The Federal Maritime Commission has proposed a rule to “adjust or meet unfavorable shipping conditions created by the Government of Spain”. The remedies are a per-voyage fee of $200,000 on all Spanish vessels and restrictions on the import of certain Spanish goods into the United States. The Commission says it is also invoking additional statutory authority because Spain “has unduly impaired the access of U.S. flag vessels to trade between foreign ports”. At 91 FR 65515; comments close 14 December 2026, with the offer of a public hearing.

The proceeding’s title names the trigger: an investigation into conditions affecting shipping in the foreign trade and denial of entry of vessels into Spanish ports. Spain denied entry to vessels, the Commission investigated, and it now proposes to charge every Spanish ship a fifth of a million dollars a voyage.

Two things make this larger than a shipping dispute. The first is the instrument: the Federal Maritime Commission is an independent agency with no trade-policy mandate, and section 19 of the Merchant Marine Act of 1920 lets it act against foreign conditions unfavourable to US shipping. It is being used to impose what is functionally a tariff and an import restriction on a NATO ally and EU member state, without a trade statute, a proclamation or a vote.

The second is that the EU’s common commercial policy is exclusive. Spain cannot negotiate its own way out; Brussels answers for it. A fee on the vessels of one member state is therefore a measure against the single market whether or not it is framed that way.

This brief read the notice, not the rule, so the scope of “Spanish vessels” — flag, ownership, operator, charter — and which goods would be restricted are not established. Those definitions are the whole measure.

What to watch: whether the European Commission responds, and in what forum; whether any carrier reflags out of Spain, which is the cheapest avoidance and would make the fee self-defeating; and the 14 December comment file, which is where the shipping industry will say what it actually costs.

An Ebola outbreak has killed 4,178 people, and the US has suspended entry from three countries including for green-card holders

Science & public health US politics & policy Primary source

A CDC order signed 9 October and effective 11 October at 5pm Eastern, published this morning at 91 FR 65556, continues the suspension of the right to introduce “covered aliens” into the United States — people who departed from or were present in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda or South Sudan in the previous 21 days, regardless of nationality. The order is time-limited to 30 days and runs to 10 November. Comments close 30 October. It was signed by Admiral Brian Christine, Assistant Secretary for Health.

The figures in it are the reason it exists, and they are worse than this brief has previously reported. As of 5 October, the DRC had 8,665 confirmed cases and 4,178 deaths of Bundibugyo virus Ebola across 64 health zones, and the outbreak is still expanding. Contact tracing is at 80%, against the 95% the order cites as the operational threshold. Uganda completed a 42-day enhanced monitoring period on 27 August; South Sudan has no confirmed cases and is judged high risk.

The legal authority is sections 362 and 365 of the Public Health Service Act, 42 U.S.C. 265 and 268, implemented at 42 CFR 71.40 — the provision used during the pandemic and litigated for three years afterwards.

One line deserves to be read twice. The order “expressly includes lawful permanent residents”, subject to its exceptions. A green-card holder who has been in Kinshasa in the past three weeks is covered. The exceptions are US citizens and nationals, armed forces and government personnel serving overseas with their spouses and children, people a customs officer excepts with supervisory approval on the totality of circumstances, and people provisionally excepted by CDC.

What to watch: whether the order is renewed on 10 November or allowed to lapse, which is the only scheduled decision point; whether contact tracing reaches 95%, the threshold the order itself names; and whether any lawful permanent resident is turned back, since that is the provision most likely to be litigated and the one with no case law of its own.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. There is no reporting to diverge from — the order published this morning and nothing reachable has covered it. The disagreement worth adjudicating is the one the document carries inside it: whether 42 U.S.C. 265 is a public health power or an immigration power. The text is a public health text: it names a pathogen, a case count, a tracing rate, an incubation period and a 30-day limit with a review commitment. The effect is an immigration effect, and the class it reaches — “covered aliens”, expressly including lawful permanent residents — is defined by status rather than by exposure. Those two facts sit in the same document and the litigation history of this section is precisely about which one governs.

Cause. Take the mundane reading first, because it is well supported here: an outbreak with 8,665 cases and 4,178 deaths, still expanding, with contact tracing fifteen points below the threshold the agency itself uses, is a genuine emergency, and a 21-day travel suspension matched to the incubation period is the textbook response. The order is time-limited, carries a comment period, commits to narrowing if conditions change, and was signed by a public health officer rather than a political appointee at DHS. Against that: the exception for lawful permanent residents is discretionary rather than categorical, which is a choice, and a disease-control measure that distinguishes a US citizen from a green-card holder who sat in the same aircraft row is not doing epidemiology at that margin.

What it means for you. If you hold a green card and have travelled to or through these three countries, your right of return is currently subject to an official’s discretion, and the operative document is this order rather than your residency. That is the direct consequence and it is narrow. The wider one is about the instrument: 42 U.S.C. 265 was used for three years on a disease with a very different transmission profile, and the courts never finally settled what it permits. Each new invocation builds the record for the next. The observable that will tell you which way this one runs is the 10 November decision — a renewal on unchanged facts, without the tracing rate improving, would indicate the authority is being held open rather than used and released. The honest position today is that the epidemiology in this document is strong enough to justify the measure on its own terms, and the class definition is broader than the epidemiology requires.

Confidence: Primary source, full text read. The case and death figures are the order’s own, attributed to the DRC as of 5 October, and were not independently verified against WHO or DRC reporting. Sources: CDC, Order Under Sections 362 and 365 of the Public Health Service Act Continuing the Suspension of the Right To Introduce Certain Persons From Countries Where a Quarantinable Communicable Disease Exists, 91 FR 65556, Docket CDC-2026-0892

The long end fell on the day the Fed-governor memorandum published, and the short end rose

US debt & fiscal Macro & markets Primary source

Treasury’s Daily Par Yield Curve for Wednesday 14 October, read by enumerated column position: 3-month 4.28%, 6-month 4.37%, 1-year 4.53%, 2-year 4.82%, 5-year 5.00%, 10-year 5.19%, 20-year 5.57%, 30-year 5.52%.

Against Tuesday the curve flattened from both ends at once. Short rates rose — the 1-year +5 basis points, the 6-month +3, the 2-year +3. Long rates fell — the 10-year −2, the 20-year −3, the 30-year −4. The 2s10s spread narrowed from 42 to 37 basis points, and the 10-year at 5.19% is the month’s lowest.

Yesterday this brief named Wednesday’s print as the observable, because it is the first close after the presidential memorandum establishing a committee of inquiry into Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, which published Wednesday morning. The answer is that the long end did not sell off. It rallied.

Be careful with that. A threat to central bank independence taken seriously by the market would normally show up as higher long yields and a steeper curve — a larger credibility premium on money lent for twenty years. The shape was the opposite. Three readings are available and this brief cannot distinguish them: the market does not expect the removal to succeed; it reads a more accommodating Fed as lower rates and is pricing a rally rather than a risk; or the move is about something else, most obviously the CPI print released the same morning with core at 2.6%.

The third is the most likely and the least interesting, and it is the one this brief would bet on. A soft core print pulls short rates up on reduced easing expectations only if the market reads it as hawkish — which cuts against it. What can be said without qualification is the negative: there was no long-end sell-off on the day, and the 20-year hump narrowed rather than widened.

What to watch: today’s and tomorrow’s prints, because a one-day move is not a repricing; the 4 November quarterly refunding; and 5 November, the Cook hearing, which is the event rather than the announcement.

Criminals have taken as much as $233m from food-stamp cards, and most states do not replace it

US politics & policy New thread Single-sourced

The USDA’s Office of Inspector General estimates that as much as $233m may have been lost to food assistance theft over the past two years, most of it to skimming — devices fitted to card readers that copy the magnetic stripe and PIN. Most states do not replace stolen benefits. One Indiana recipient described her balance being drained within minutes of the monthly load, with the card still in her wallet.

The mechanism should not survive scrutiny. American banks moved to chip cards more than a decade ago; SNAP did not. An EBT card is in most states a magnetic-stripe card with a PIN — the technology the payments industry abandoned precisely because it can be copied at the reader. Organised rings target SNAP because it is the last large magnetic-stripe system in American retail.

The consequences are not abstract. A survey by Propel, which makes a widely used EBT balance app, found that most victims skipped or ate fewer meals and nearly half borrowed money or went into debt. A stolen SNAP balance is not an inconvenience pending a chargeback; in most states it is simply gone until the next month.

And the fix got harder this month: federal funding changes beginning in October will make it harder for states to pay for chip conversion and anti-skimming work, according to a GAO director. The USDA said there had been “continued inaction” against skimming well before the funding changed.

Note what kind of fraud this is, because the word does a lot of work in this debate. Skimming is theft from recipients, not theft by them. A programme-integrity argument that treats the two as one thing will produce policies that tighten eligibility and leave the card technology alone.

What to watch: whether any state announces chip conversion with a date; whether Congress restores replacement authority, the only thing that makes a victim whole; and whether USDA publishes skimming losses as a series rather than an estimate.

Confidence: Single-sourced. One news investigation; the USDA OIG estimate, the Propel survey and the GAO comment were not obtained in the original, and no state-level replacement data was checked. The account of card technology is this brief’s own. Sources: NPR · OPB

AAA diesel is $6.3173 this morning, up 1.76 cents and a third consecutive rise: +1.22, +2.10, +1.76 — 5.08 cents in three days. It is above the week-ago level for the first time since the run began and the highest since 6 October. Petrol is $4.3344, up 0.11 cents, essentially flat after three falls.

The gap is the cleanest expression of it. Diesel minus petrol is $1.9829, the widest of the period, up 7.2 cents from Monday. A crude-oil move pushes both products the same way; a gap opening at this rate is distillate-specific.

The stocks series supports that, with the caveat that it is not current. EIA’s weekly inventory table runs only to the week ending 2 October, and over the four weeks to then distillate stocks fell from 107,859 to 105,138 thousand barrels — down 2.7 million barrels, with the sharpest drop in the week to 25 September. Gasoline stocks fell about 3.0 million over the same four weeks and commercial crude rose slightly. Distillate was drawing down before the hurricane, before the Russian licence, and before this week’s rise.

Put that beside the first story: BLS has fuel oil up 74.2% over twelve months and energy up 20.4%, and that is the quarter that set the Social Security adjustment. The diesel price is no longer only a commodity story; it is now a line in the inflation statistic and a transfer payment to 72 million people.

What to watch: today at noon Eastern, the delayed EIA report, which will give the first stock figures covering the hurricane weeks; 18 October, three days away, the Graham Act implementation deadline; and whether petrol follows diesel up, which would turn a distillate story into a crude one.

US debt & fiscal Treasury changed its auction rules three weeks before the quarterly refunding, and one change is not technical.

Treasury has issued final amendments to the Uniform Offering Circular for the Sale and Issue of Marketable Book-Entry Treasury Bills, Notes, and Bonds — the auction rules. At 91 FR 65456, effective today. Most of it is housekeeping: removing outdated references and aligning terminology.

One change is operational. The amendments modify the requirements for submitting noncompetitive customer bids, to require submitters to provide all customer bid information before the noncompetitive auction close. Noncompetitive bidding is how smaller buyers — including individuals through TreasuryDirect — take securities at whatever yield the auction sets, rather than bidding a price. Requiring complete customer information before the close rather than after tightens the window for dealers and brokers aggregating those bids.

The timing is the reason this is here at all: the quarterly refunding is on 4 November, and this brief has been following the long end of the curve. An auction-rule change effective three weeks before a refunding is worth noticing even when it is small.

Below the line because it is a procedural amendment with no issuance, size or schedule change in it, and the rule text was not read — only the abstract. It leads if the refunding announcement changes issuance at the long end.

US politics & policy The rule making airlines say why your flight was late has been pushed to February.

The Department of Transportation is delaying the effective date of its 3 September 2026 final rule, Cause of Airline Delay and Cancellation Categories under Section 511(b) of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, until 1 February 2027. At 91 FR 65456.

Section 511(b) exists because the categories airlines currently use to report delays are their own. A delay attributed to “air carrier” versus “extreme weather” versus “national aviation system” determines what a passenger is owed and how an airline’s on-time performance reads, and Congress legislated standard categories precisely because the self-reporting was unfalsifiable.

A five-month delay to an effective date is not a repeal, and rules get delayed for implementation reasons that are often genuine. What it does is move the first standardised reporting past the winter — the season the data would be most revealing about.

Below the line because it is a date change to a rule that has not yet operated, with no substantive amendment, and the notice was not read beyond its abstract. It leads on 1 February, or if the delay is extended again.

Energy & grid Holtec has applied to build two small modular reactors at Palisades, with a request to start work before the permit issues.

The NRC is giving public notice of a phased construction permit application including a limited work authorization request from SMR, LLC on behalf of Palisades SMR, LLC, a Holtec International subsidiary, for a dual-unit SMR-300 plant at the Palisades Energy Center in Covert, Michigan. The units are Pioneer Unit 1 and Pioneer Unit 2. At 91 FR 65608; this is the third of four weekly notices beginning 1 October.

The second is the limited work authorization: a request to begin specified construction activity before the permit is granted, at the applicant’s risk. It is the mechanism that decides whether “approved in 2029” means first concrete in 2029 or first concrete now.

US financials The credit union regulator proposed three rules in one morning, one of them finalising emergency measures from 2020.

The National Credit Union Administration published three proposed rules today, all with comments closing 14 December. The Central Liquidity Facility proposal (91 FR 65481) would finally adopt as permanent the revisions made by two interim final rules published on 29 April 2020 and 24 March 2021, remove provisions that have since lapsed, drop certain filing requirements and membership-approval criteria, and change the timing of capital stock subscription adjustments. Powers of Federal Credit Unions (91 FR 65479) would confirm that a credit union need not obtain an advisory opinion from NCUA’s general counsel before petitioning the Board about an activity outside its preapproved incidental powers. The third covers corporate credit unions and fidelity bond coverage.

The first is the one worth marking. The Central Liquidity Facility is the credit union system’s lender of last resort, and its pandemic-era expansion has been operating on interim rules for five and a half years, with some provisions already lapsed. Finalising them now is housekeeping that also decides what the facility looks like in the next liquidity event.

Below the line because three proposals opening a two-month comment period change nothing yet, and none was read beyond its abstract. It leads if the CLF rule narrows access rather than codifying it.

Science & public health The FDA has issued four emergency authorisations for animal drugs against New World screwworm.

The FDA has issued four Emergency Use Authorizations for animal products against New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax), at 91 FR 65562. One, requested by Elanco US, is for application on or around wounds to prevent infestation in sheep, cattle, goats, swine and camelids, and in captive and captured cervids, American bison, bighorn sheep and Sonoran pronghorn. A second, requested by Zoetis, is for treatment.

Screwworm is a fly whose larvae feed on the living tissue of warm-blooded animals. The United States eradicated it in 1966 through one of the great successes of applied entomology — releasing sterile males by the billion — and has held the barrier in Central America ever since. The barrier has been moving north.

Emergency Use Authorizations for animal drugs are rare, and four at once is a statement about expected need rather than about any single product. The species list is the tell: it covers the commercial herd and the wildlife reservoir that would make reintroduction permanent.

Below the line because an authorisation is a preparation, not an outbreak: no US detection is reported in what was read, and the notice itself was not obtained beyond its abstract. It leads on a confirmed US case.

US politics & policy Civil rights groups plan to send thousands of observers to polling places for the midterms.

Civil rights advocates plan to deploy thousands of observers to polling places for the midterm elections on 3 November. No organisation, state list, training protocol or legal basis was obtainable here beyond the headline.

The context that makes it worth a line is structural. Private election observation grew in the years after the Supreme Court’s 2013 decision in Shelby County ended preclearance, which had required certain jurisdictions to get federal approval before changing voting procedures. What replaced a federal review requirement was, in part, volunteers watching. The Justice Department also sends its own monitors, and whether and where it does so this year is a separate question nobody has answered publicly.

Observers have no enforcement power. What they produce is a contemporaneous record, which matters only if something is later contested.

Below the line because nothing has happened: it is a stated plan with no numbers, places or named groups established here. It leads if the Justice Department announces its own monitor deployment, or declines to.

US politics & policy Almost all federal civilian service now counts toward family leave eligibility, and some VA staff are excluded.

The Office of Personnel Management has issued a direct final rule making technical revisions to its Family and Medical Leave Act regulations to incorporate statutory amendments. At 91 FR 65437, effective 14 December, with comments to 16 November. Three changes: virtually all federal civilian service and certain honourable active military service now count toward FMLA eligibility; annual and sick leave provided under authorities outside title 5 may be substituted for unpaid FMLA leave; and certain Veterans Health Administration employees are excluded from title 5 FMLA coverage.

The first change is the largest in practice. FMLA eligibility turns on twelve months of service, and federal employees who had moved between appointment types or served in the military frequently found that earlier service did not count. Counting it means a new federal hire with prior service can take protected leave immediately rather than after a year.

The third cuts the other way for a defined group: VHA employees under a separate personnel authority are now outside title 5 FMLA, which does not mean no leave — it means a different statutory scheme governs it.

Below the line because it is a direct final rule implementing statutory changes already enacted, with no discretion exercised, and the rule text was not read. It leads if the VHA exclusion turns out to reduce leave rights rather than relocate them.

On the correction, because the lesson is narrower than the mistake. Yesterday’s error was not a wrong figure. It was asserting a publication time without checking the publication schedule — and the schedule was on the same page the brief would have had to open to get the data. The holiday that delayed it was the same holiday this brief wrote three paragraphs about on Monday. The rule: when naming a future document as the thing that will settle a question, open its release calendar in the same breath. It costs one fetch and it is the difference between a promise and a guess.

And on the observable that came back the wrong way. Yesterday this brief said the first Treasury print after the Cook memorandum was the thing to watch, with the clear implication that a threat to Federal Reserve independence would push the long end up. The long end fell. The fourth story sets out three readings and says this brief cannot distinguish them, and names the dullest as most likely. That is the honest position, and it is worth recording that naming an observable in advance is only useful if the brief accepts the answer when it is inconvenient. One day is not a repricing; the next two prints and the 5 November hearing are.
