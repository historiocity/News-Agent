After months of threatening to take Greenland, the United States has a deal that leaves it Danish

Geopolitics New subject Independent corroboration

The President announced on Friday that the United States and Denmark have reached an agreement to expand the American military presence in Greenland. It follows months of open threats to take the island from a NATO ally by force, and it leaves the territory in Danish hands.

On the reported terms, the agreement bans any non-NATO base in Greenland, restricts adversary investment there, and guarantees that neither China nor Russia can establish a base. The President's own description was that it “gives the United States permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland”, and that development of a larger military presence would begin immediately.

Officials from the United States, Denmark and Greenland negotiated quietly for months. The office of Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen says all three governments will sign next week during the UN General Assembly — and that parliamentary action is still required in both Denmark and Greenland before it can be enforced.

That last clause is the whole story's load-bearing element. Greenland has about 56,000 residents and its own parliament, and nothing here is binding until that parliament acts.

This wakes a thread this brief had let go dormant on 7 September, when the EU signed its own €530m partnership with Nuuk. Greenland now has two suitors with signed paper and one of them wants basing rights.

What to watch: the signing at UNGA next week; the Danish and Greenlandic parliamentary votes; and whether “permanent control over security” survives into the treaty text in those words.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not about whether there is a deal, but about what it does to sovereignty — and the two descriptions are hard to reconcile. The President says the agreement gives the United States “permanent control over security, and all other needs”. The Danish and American press framing is that the deal keeps the island in Denmark's hands and is the alternative to annexation. “Permanent control over all other needs” and “remains Danish territory” are not obviously compatible claims about the same document.

Cause. Audience, and it is the ordinary explanation rather than the motivated one. Each government has to sell this domestically as a win, and the win is differently shaped in each capital: Washington needs it to sound like the acquisition it promised, Copenhagen needs it to sound like sovereignty preserved, Nuuk needs it to sound like neither of them decided anything without asking. A defence-access agreement can honestly be described all three ways, because basing rights and sovereignty genuinely are separable — that is what a status-of-forces arrangement is. Note the timing, too: the President is characterising an unsigned text, and the text is what the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments will vote on.

What it means for you. Wait for the signed text and read one thing: what the agreement says about duration and termination. “Permanent” in a press statement and a treaty with a notice period are very different objects, and the notice period is where sovereignty actually lives. Until then, treat the substantive and verifiable part as the exclusion terms — no non-NATO bases, no Chinese or Russian presence, restricted adversary investment — because those are specific, consistent across accounts, and are the part the US genuinely wanted. The observation that would settle it is the Greenlandic parliament's debate, which will be conducted by people with no incentive to help either capital's framing.

The President said three news organisations are banned from the White House. By Friday afternoon their reporters were still there

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

In a social-media post on Friday the President said he is banning CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House, effective immediately, on the grounds that outlets should not be able to report “FICTION and LIES” about the President or the administration.

Then nothing happened. Reporters from all three organisations were at the White House on Friday afternoon, doing live shots, coming and going. Their passes had not been revoked. It was not clear whether the White House intended to strip credentials entirely, or to exclude the three from briefings, events and the press pool, or to do neither.

The gap between the announcement and the administrative act is the story, and it is a gap with precedent. In the first term the White House tried to pull CNN correspondent Jim Acosta's credentials; CNN sued, and the White House abandoned the effort. Leading First Amendment organisations said on Friday that an actual ban would be unconstitutional and would not survive court review.

What makes this more than a repeat is the scope. Acosta was one reporter. This is three newsrooms, one of which — Politico — is a trade publication whose subscribers are substantially the government itself.

What to watch: whether any credential is actually revoked, whether the White House Correspondents' Association is consulted about pool composition, and the first filing if it is.

Dig deeper — why the pool matters more than the briefing room

Credentials and access are two different things, and only one of them has been litigated. A hard pass is the building credential, issued through the Secret Service, and the Acosta case established that revoking one requires at minimum some due process — notice and an opportunity to respond — because the pass is a protected interest once granted. That is a thin holding but it is a real one, and it is why a straightforward revocation is the version most likely to fail in court.

The press pool is the softer target and the more consequential one. Most presidential events are too small for the whole press corps, so a rotating pool of a few reporters attends and shares its reporting with everyone else. Who sits in the pool has historically been decided by the White House Correspondents' Association rather than by the White House — an arrangement of custom, not law. Earlier in this administration that custom was already changed, with the White House taking control of pool composition. Excluding an outlet from the pool achieves most of what a ban would achieve, requires revoking nothing, and is far harder to challenge, because no individual's credential has been taken away.

So the thing to watch is not the briefing room, where cameras are, but the pool list, where they are not. If the three organisations keep their passes and quietly stop appearing in pool rotations, the ban will have been implemented without ever being executed as announced.

Why the Politico detail matters. Politico's readership is concentrated among federal employees, lobbyists, congressional staff and executive-branch officials; its subscription products are sold into agencies. Excluding it is unlike excluding a broadcaster, because a substantial part of its audience is inside the government being covered. That cuts both ways as an argument, which is why it is worth naming rather than asserting: it makes the exclusion more consequential for the flow of information within Washington, and it also makes the outlet less dependent on White House physical access than a television network with a stand-up position on the North Lawn.

What this brief cannot yet tell you is whether any formal step has been taken at all. As of Friday afternoon the only artefact is a social-media post. A post is not an administrative action, and treating one as though it were is a way of reporting things that have not happened.

Diesel set a tenth consecutive record this morning and is now 75% dearer than a year ago

Macro & markets Energy & grid Primary source

AAA's national average for diesel this morning is $6.4866, against $6.4476 yesterday. That is a tenth consecutive record. A week ago it was $6.1602; a month ago $5.5042; a year ago $3.7060, which puts the annual increase at 75.0%.

Regular gasoline is $4.4759, from $3.1996 a year ago — up 40% and still below its June 2022 record of $5.0165. The spread between the two is the shape of this shock: it is a distillate shock, not a crude shock, and it lands on freight, agriculture, rail and heating rather than on the commuter first.

The daily increment is slowing. Yesterday added 5.2 cents; today added 3.9. The weekly change is +32.6 cents, against +39 cents a week ago. That is the first honest sign of deceleration in this run, and it is a sign about the rate, not the level.

It is Saturday, so there are no settlements to report and no EIA print. The last verified crude reading in this brief was Brent around $102.57 on Friday, its third consecutive session of losses, on reports that Saudi Arabia is moving some crude to Asian buyers via Oman — a route that genuinely avoids Hormuz rather than substituting one chokepoint for another.

What to watch: Wednesday's EIA distillate stocks; whether the weekly increment falls under a nickel, which would be the first real turn; and confirmation of the Oman routing in loading data rather than in reports of it.

Trump and Xi meet on Thursday with AI on the agenda, and the people who talk when governments cannot are already talking

AI & tech industry Geopolitics Single-sourced

The two presidents meet in Washington on Thursday 24 September, and artificial intelligence is expected to be a principal subject. Expectations among people who follow the relationship are low: the assessment is that the chances of changing the dynamic are dim.

The dynamic in question is specific and mutual. The administration has accused Chinese AI companies of stealing intellectual property by distilling leading American models, and has threatened sanctions over it. Beijing accuses Washington of running a “Cold War playbook” to hold Chinese AI down. Neither accusation is obviously false, which is why neither is negotiable at a summit.

Underneath the official channel, an informal network of dialogues at think tanks and universities has been putting American and Chinese researchers in the same rooms to discuss shared risks. These are “Track Two” talks — unofficial, deniable, and historically the place where arms-control language gets drafted years before governments will sign it.

That is the reason to pay attention to a summit expected to achieve nothing. Track Two exists precisely for periods when Track One cannot move, and its output is vocabulary: agreed definitions of what a dangerous capability is, what an incident is, what would count as a verification step. None of that is announced; it shows up later, in treaty text, if it shows up at all.

What to watch: the readout's language on AI — specifically whether any shared definition appears; whether distillation is named; and whether either side commits to a working group with a date.

Confidence: Single-sourced — one NPR report, syndicated across many stations, which is one newsroom rather than many. Florida & Tampa Bay: a Tampa company has gene-edited a rootstock against citrus greening, and growers are planting it

Florida & Tampa Bay Science & public health New subject Single-sourced

Florida growers are planting citrus trees on a rootstock designed with CRISPR to resist citrus greening. The rootstock is called CarriCea T1 and it was developed by Soilcea, a Tampa company. The EPA approved the genetic modifications earlier this year. In Soilcea's test field the modified trees are taller and greener than unmodified ones, and the design intent is to keep the tree's immune response working so growers can use fewer insecticides.

The scale of what it is up against is worth stating in full, because it is the most brutal economic number in this brief. In the late 1990s, before greening arrived, Florida shipped around 300 million boxes of fruit a season. The season that ended in June shipped a little over 15 million. That is a 95% collapse of a signature state industry inside a working lifetime.

Soilcea's chief executive is candid about what the evidence currently supports: the data suggests it will work, but until there is a ten-year-old tree, nobody knows how the tree performs. Citrus is a perennial crop and the disease kills slowly; a rootstock that looks good at three years is a hypothesis.

For a Pasco or Hillsborough reader the near-term effect is not the orange juice price. It is land. Groves that cannot produce get sold, and in this part of the state they get sold to developers.

What to watch: acreage planted on CarriCea T1 next season; independent field data from the University of Florida's IFAS extension; and whether replanting shows up in county agricultural land-use figures.

Confidence: Single-sourced, and the company is an interested party on the performance claim — the 300m-to-15m industry figures are the load-bearing ones and are independent of it. Sources: WUSF Health News Florida / NPR

Florida & Tampa Bay The Atlantic finally has a system at 80% — and it is southwest of the Azores, which is about as far from Florida as the basin gets.

The National Hurricane Center's 2am outlook gives AL99, a low southwest of the Azores in the eastern subtropical Atlantic, an 80% chance of formation at both 48 hours and seven days. Conditions are expected to turn less favourable from Sunday night or Monday. There are still no named storms in the basin.

This is the first system all season this brief has reported above 20%, and it is worth one line precisely to say that it does not matter to this reader. The eastern subtropical Atlantic near the Azores is the far side of the ocean; systems forming there overwhelmingly recurve into the North Atlantic. Florida's threat comes from the Cabo Verde track further south, the western Caribbean, or the Gulf, and all three remain empty nine days past the climatological peak.

The practical consequence is unchanged and is fiscal rather than meteorological: the state's emergency management funding request has still not been tested by a landfall.

Below the line because a probable depression in the wrong part of the ocean is weather, not news. It runs as a lead on a named storm with a Gulf or Florida track.

US debt & fiscal Friday’s correction stands, and it changes how this brief will quote yields from now on.

Yesterday's issue corrected an earlier claim that the ten-year Treasury yield had gone through 5%. Treasury's own constant-maturity series says it did not. The correction was right to run as a lead: the reader's standing preference is that a wrong framing earns space rather than a footnote.

The operational lesson is about sourcing, not arithmetic. Intraday quotes from data vendors, headline figures in market wraps, and Treasury's official H.15 constant-maturity series are three different numbers, and they disagree by several basis points at exactly the moments when a round number is about to be crossed — which is precisely when a brief is tempted to report it. A 5% headline is worth nothing if the official series never printed it.

From here this brief quotes the Treasury par yield curve or H.15 for any level claim, names the source in the sentence, and treats vendor intraday prints as directional only. It is Saturday; the last figure verified here was about 4.93% on 17 September, easing after the hike.

Below the line because it is a process note on a correction already made prominently, not new information. Wednesday's auction schedule and the next refunding are the events.

Florida & Tampa Bay Five days past the objection deadline on the school vaccine rule, and the state register still shows no hearing noticed.

Comments and hearing requests on the rewrite of rule 64D-3.046 closed on 14 September. The Florida Chapter of the American College of Physicians filed a timely request for a public hearing; a timely request inside the statutory window obliges the agency to hold one.

As of the latest check the Florida Administrative Register still shows no hearing noticed. Five days is not yet a violation — the agency has a reasonable period in which to schedule — but the count is the thing worth keeping, because the rule cannot be adopted while a properly requested hearing is outstanding, and the adoption date determines whether this reaches the current school year.

The rule drops hepatitis B, varicella, Hib and pneumococcal from school-entry requirements. Measles, polio, diphtheria, pertussis, mumps and tetanus are statutory and survive it, though the widened exemption language reaches those too.

Below the line because the absence of a docket entry is not a development, and this is the fourth issue running to say so. It becomes a lead on a noticed hearing date, on adoption, or on a challenge at the Division of Administrative Hearings.

US financials Buffett’s handover is done rather than pending: chairman emeritus at 96, his son in the chair, and he stays on the board.

Warren Buffett has stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, taking the title of chairman emeritus. Howard Buffett takes the chair. He remains on the board. He has run the company since 1965, over which time it compounded at about 19.7% a year, roughly double the S&P 500.

The governance point that matters is the separation this completes. The chair is now a Buffett; the capital allocation is not. That split — family continuity in the chair, professional management of the investment decisions — is the structure Buffett has described for years as the way to keep the culture without making the returns hereditary, and it is now in place rather than promised.

The succession risk was never that the company would be badly run. It was that Berkshire's ability to be the buyer of last resort in a crisis rested on one person's reputation for deciding in a weekend. That is the thing that cannot be transferred by title, and it will not be tested until the next crisis.

Below the line because it ran as a lead yesterday and today adds nothing but confirmation. It returns if the board announces a change in capital-allocation authority, or at the first large acquisition under the new chair.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. Sources: NPR index · continues 18 September
