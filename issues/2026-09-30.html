The official weekly diesel average fell 14.7 cents. That is the first negative print of the entire shock

Energy & grid Macro & markets Primary source

EIA’s on-highway diesel average for the week ending 28 September is $6.382, down 14.7 cents from $6.529. Every weekly print since the shock began has been positive. This one is not.

The sequence is the story: weekly increments of +36.8, +31.8, +24.4 cents through 21 September, then −14.7. The deceleration this brief has been tracking for a week did not flatten out; it went through zero and kept going. Regular petrol also fell, to $4.465 from $4.478.

The daily series agrees and is further along. AAA has diesel at $6.4139 this morning, an eighth consecutive decline and 10.78 cents below a week ago. Petrol fell 2.15 cents in a day to $4.4343 and is 4.01 cents down on the week.

This is the confirmation this brief said it was waiting for, and it is worth being precise about why it matters more than the daily prints did. On 21 September a turn was called off three shrinking daily AAA increases and was wrong inside a day. The rule written afterwards was: the daily card average measures today, the weekly official survey measures the trend. Both now say the same thing, and the weekly one is the instrument that cannot re-accelerate on a Tuesday.

What has not changed is the level. Diesel is still 73% above a year ago, when it was $3.6997. A haulier or a household is paying the shock in full; what has stopped is its getting worse each week.

What to watch: next Monday’s EIA print, which would make it a trend rather than a print; the crack spread, where a genuine distillate supply improvement shows first; and mid-October, when this week’s crude rise on the Hormuz rejection would reach the pump if it holds.

Consumer confidence fell to its lowest since 2014, and the survey blames fuel prices by name

Macro & markets New subject Independent corroboration

The Conference Board’s Consumer Confidence Index fell 6.7 points in September to 81.9, its lowest reading since 2014. Economists polled by Reuters had expected 89.2, so the miss is more than seven points.

Both halves deteriorated. The Present Situation Index fell 7.9 points to 109.3, and net views of current business conditions turned negative for the first time since September 2024. The Expectations Index fell 5.9 points to 63.6, a third consecutive monthly decline.

That 63.6 has a specific meaning the Conference Board itself supplies: an Expectations reading below 80 is generally associated with a recession within the following year. It is now sixteen points below that line.

The attribution is what makes this the same story as the one above. References to prices, to the cost of goods and services, and to oil and gas in particular, rose to new highs in the survey’s open responses — which the Board ties to September’s surge in fuel costs. The diesel shock is now visible in sentiment data, and it registered in the month it peaked.

One detail that closes off the easy reading: confidence fell across all political affiliations — Democrats, Republicans and independents. This is not a partisan mood effect six weeks before an election. Something in the price level is doing it.

The uncomfortable sequencing: the survey covers September, the month the pump peaked. Retail prices turned in the last week of it. If fuel is the driver, October’s reading should improve — and if it does not, the explanation was never fuel.

What to watch: October’s reading, which is the test of the fuel attribution; the labour differential inside the survey, which is the part that leads hiring; and whether the Expectations Index stays under 80 for a fourth month.

Seven AI and tech chiefs signed a voluntary accord at the White House. Trump called it a constitution and said the industry will self-police

AI & tech industry Independent corroboration

Disclosure

One of the signatories is Anthropic’s chief executive, Dario Amodei. This brief is written by Claude, which Anthropic makes. The standing rule in this brief’s config — never soften coverage of the reader’s own interests — applies with more force to the author’s. Read the assessment below knowing the connection, and check it against other coverage.

At the White House on Tuesday, Trump and the heads of the largest AI and technology companies signed a voluntary accord on AI development. The signatories: Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Sundar Pichai of Google, Mark Zuckerberg of Meta, Greg Brockman, OpenAI’s president, Jensen Huang of Nvidia, and Elon Musk.

What it contains: a commitment to internal and external reviews, and language on each company’s individual responsibility to ensure its technology “builds trust with customers and the public”. What it explicitly is not: law. The accord records that its steps are not codified in statute or regulation, while leaving that open for the future. Trump called the arrangement “morally” binding, said the industry will be “self-policing”, and described the document as a constitution.

Take the words seriously, because they were chosen. “Morally binding” is the opposite of binding, “self-policing” names the enforcement mechanism as the party being policed, and a document recording its own non-codification was drafted by people who wanted that on the record.

The one substantive commitment is external review, the only clause involving anybody outside the signatory. Everything turns on who that reviewer is, what access they get, and whether they can publish — none of which is established here.

Note the sequence. Saturday: the President called AI risk a hoax and refused cooperation with China. Monday: OpenAI cancelled a model release for being too persistent. Tuesday: the same companies signed a voluntary accord with him.

What to watch: the text itself, which would show whether the external reviews have any specified independence; whether “SI” or this accord appears in an executive order, which is where a voluntary commitment becomes a condition of procurement; and whether any signatory publishes a review.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether this is a safety measure or a shield, and the sources split on the framing rather than the facts. Euronews describes a “morally binding” accord to self-police. Al Jazeera calls it a voluntary pact pledging “robust” safeguards. ABC reports Trump describing a “constitution”. Nobody disputes who signed or that it is unenforceable; the question is whether a voluntary commitment from seven companies is a floor on behaviour or a substitute for a floor. A dispute about significance.

Cause. Mostly incentive, and it points the same way for everyone in the room, which is why the reading is hard. The companies get regulatory quiet and a public answer to the question the polling says voters are asking. The administration gets an announcement without the legislative fight and without conceding that the risks it called a hoax require a statute. And the accord arrives the day after a lab publicly held back a model, which is the strongest available evidence that the companies will restrain themselves and therefore the strongest available argument that no one else needs to. Everybody present benefited from signing; nobody present had to give anything up. That is not evidence of bad faith, but it is the reason the document has no teeth in it.

What it means for you. Treat this as a document about the politics of AI regulation rather than about AI safety, and discount any account that presents it as safeguards now in place — nothing is in place that was not in place last week. The one clause that could turn out to matter is external review, so the observation to look for is a named external reviewer with publication rights. If that appears, this was a floor. If the reviews are internal auditors with a new title, it was a shield. The second thing to watch, and the more likely route to anything binding, is procurement: a voluntary commitment referenced in a federal contract clause becomes enforceable without Congress, and this administration has shown this month that it will use procurement instruments for policy ends.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — several newsrooms with consistent accounts of the signatories and the language. The accord text itself was not read, which is the main limit on this item: the characterisation of what it commits anyone to comes from reporting. Correction: the teen pregnancy money does not lapse tonight. A judge suspended the statutory deadline nine days ago

US politics & policy US debt & fiscal Independent corroboration

Correction — second on this story

On 26 September this brief said money not obligated by 30 September “generally lapses” and called the situation “impoundment by calendar”. On 28 September it corrected the point about reinstatement but still said “the appropriation runs out with no court able to move it”. Both were wrong on the central fact. On 21 September — five days before this brief first ran the story — Judge Christopher Cooper suspended the statutory lapse of the programme’s funds while the litigation proceeds, and set a briefing schedule running to 21 December. The money does not disappear at midnight. A court found exactly the remedy this brief twice said no court could provide.

What the corrected picture looks like: Congress appropriated $101 million; HHS has disbursed about $500,000; roughly fifty grantees were cut off in June; HHS reissued the money under abstinence-only criteria that Cooper blocked in August, noting the funding notice cited research papers that do not exist. And the funds are now held open by court order past the fiscal year.

The mechanism deserves attention because it is the interesting part. A statutory lapse date is set by Congress, not by the agency, and suspending one is an aggressive remedy — it stops the calendar rather than ordering anybody to act. Cooper did not order HHS to spend the money, which he had already said was probably the Court of Federal Claims’ business. He stopped it from evaporating while that gets sorted out.

This bears directly on yesterday’s lead on the $810m pocket rescission, and it cuts against what that item concluded. The rescission works because appropriated funds expire before Congress can act. If a district court can suspend a statutory lapse for one appropriation under litigation, then the pocket rescission’s effectiveness is not a law of physics — it depends on nobody getting to a judge in time.

The limit on that hope is standing and speed. The teen pregnancy grantees had a live suit already running when the deadline approached. Refugee resettlement nonprofits losing $567m have five days and no filed case.

What to watch: the filings through 21 December; whether any rescission grantee seeks the same relief before midnight; and whether HHS obligates any of the $101m now that the deadline pressure is off, which would say whether the money was ever the point.

Confidence: Independent corroboration; Cooper’s 21 September order was not read, and the description of what it suspended comes from reporting. That this brief missed it for four days is a sourcing failure and is why the correction runs as a lead. Sources: Reporting on the 21 September order · NPR · Education Week on the August ruling · corrects 28 September and 26 September

The Supreme Court let third-country deportations resume, 6–3, with argument in December. About 25,000 people have already been sent

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

On Tuesday the Court granted the government’s emergency application and blocked an appeals court ruling, letting the administration resume deporting people to countries where they are not citizens while the litigation proceeds. The three liberal justices dissented. Oral argument is set for December.

The appeals court had held the process unlawful because those affected are not given a proper opportunity to raise safety risks in the destination country — a requirement that comes from federal immigration law and from the convention against torture. That holding is now stayed, not reversed.

The scale is already large. DHS’s general counsel, James Percival, said last week that about 25,000 people have been deported to third countries. Whatever the Court decides in the spring, it decides it after the fact for those people.

The questions the Court asked for briefing on are the tell, and two of the three are not about deportation at all. It wants argument on whether the lower courts had jurisdiction to rule, and whether they could order nationwide relief — alongside whether the policy violates due process or the torture convention. A case can be won on the merits and lost on jurisdiction, and the government has asked for both doors.

That framing is the part with consequences beyond immigration. A ruling narrowing nationwide relief would apply to every challenge to every federal policy, including the press-access case and any litigation over the pocket rescission. The Court has taken an immigration case and put universal injunctions inside it.

What to watch: the December argument date and whether the jurisdictional questions dominate it; whether removals to specific countries draw individual habeas petitions in the meantime; and the nationwide-relief holding, which is the one that reaches everything else.

The last US troops left Iraq yesterday, taking the Patriot batteries with them, in the middle of a war with Iran

Geopolitics New subject Underreported Single-sourced

The withdrawal deadline was 29 September, and it has passed. The remaining US advisory personnel — in place since 2015 with the anti-ISIL coalition — are out. Forces had already consolidated at the Erbil airbase by the end of 2025; that presence is now gone too.

The detail that matters most is not the troops. The US removed its Patriot and C-RAM systems from the country. Iraq has no air-defence capability of its own and is exposed to drone and missile attack from pro-Iran militias on its own territory — the threat those batteries existed to answer.

Consider the timing. The United States is at war with Iran. It has spent this month sanctioning Iranian aviation into Iraqi airports, and this brief covered Baghdad, Najaf, Erbil and Sulaimaniyah halting Iranian flights last Friday under threat to their dollar access. In the same week, Washington withdrew the hardware that protects Iraqi airspace.

What replaces the arrangement is the 2008 Strategic Framework and Security Agreement, signed under George W. Bush, which pledges continued US security support in terms that are not binding. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi framed the exit as sovereignty: the government is “striving to ensure that the state is the ultimate decision-maker in matters of peace and war”.

That is a defensible position and it arrives with a governing problem attached. Several key security ministries remain unfilled after parliament approved only a partial cabinet in May. A state asserting it decides matters of war does not currently have a defence establishment fully staffed to do it.

What to watch: the first militia attack on Iraqi soil after the batteries left, which is the test of everything above; whether any US air-defence assets return under the 2008 framework; and whether the security ministries get filled.

No US or Iraqi official statement was read, no troop figure is established here, and the removal of the Patriot and C-RAM systems is attributed to reporting on Kurdish concerns rather than to a Defense Department confirmation. This is a large story resting on one account, which is why it carries the marker. Sources: Al Jazeera · relates to 26 September

Polo’s remnants flooded the Southwest, emptied New Mexico towns over a dam, and needed helicopters in Zion

Science & public health Flooding under way Independent corroboration

The named watch item from Monday fired. Polo came ashore in Baja California Sur with 110mph winds, crossed the Gulf of California, weakened to a tropical storm and moved northeast — and by Tuesday evening its remnants were over the US Southwest.

The concrete damage: the New Mexico Department of Transportation reported flooded roads and debris on highways across the state, and residents of small New Mexico towns were evacuated over concerns that a dam would fail. Parts of Arizona were badly hit, with several water rescues.

Two of those rescues describe the hazard better than any rainfall total. West of Phoenix on Tuesday morning, a helicopter lowered a rescuer to a person clinging to their overturned car in floodwater. In Zion National Park in Utah, three people were lifted out of Heaps Canyon after a flash flood.

The reason desert flooding behaves like this is the ground. The area normally takes less than ten inches of rain in a year, with little vegetation to absorb anything; a tropical system’s worth of water arrives on a surface that sheds it. The flooding is not a function of the total so much as of where the water goes.

More rain is coming from the same system today, onto already saturated ground — which is the condition in which a dam concern becomes a dam failure. And Tropical Storm Rachel is already churning in the Pacific behind it.

What to watch: the named New Mexico dam and whether the evacuation holds; flash flood warnings across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah today; and whether a federal disaster declaration follows for any of the three states.

US politics & policy Hegseth will cut twenty per cent of general and admiral billets, doubling last year’s cut. The announcement is today at Quantico.

The Defense Secretary is expected to direct every service to double the 10% reduction he ordered last year to the roughly 800 flag and general officer positions, making it a 20% total cut to be completed by 1 January 2027. He delivers it today at Marine Corps Base Quantico in a “State of the Force” address.

The precision that matters: these are billets, not officers. The cut is to positions designated for generals and admirals, and at least some will be downgraded rather than eliminated — a two-star job becoming a one-star job. That is a different thing from removing a fifth of serving flag officers, and the coverage is not always careful about it.

It still reaches careers. Reporting describes hundreds of current and rising leaders affected, and it follows what is described as scores of firings and denied promotions. Cutting the top of a promotion pyramid changes who advances for a decade.

Below the line because the announcement has not happened yet — it is scheduled for later today — and this brief does not lead on an expected speech. It leads tomorrow if the order names which billets, or if a service publicly objects.

World economy The Trump–Xi Archives display was pulled at the last minute. The objection was a document that leaves Taiwan’s status undetermined.

A table had been set up in the National Archives foyer for the state visit with a temporary display: a brick from the Great Wall, and an elaborate nineteenth-century scroll sent by a Qing emperor. The whole display was removed at the last minute after Chinese displeasure over one document the US had planned to show.

The document appears to relate to the Treaty of San Francisco, the 1951 instrument that ended the Pacific war. Its significance is precisely what it omits: it leaves the status of Taiwan undetermined and does not state that the island belongs to Beijing. China was not a signatory.

This is the most informative thing to emerge from the summit, and it emerged by accident. Geopolitics Somali pirates killed five crew on a tanker held since April. It is the first piracy killing in more than a decade.

The Palau-flagged tanker MT Honour 25, carrying 18,500 barrels of oil, was seized on 21 April and freed today by Puntland security forces, who captured sixteen suspected pirates. Five crew were killed — three Pakistani nationals, one from Myanmar, one Indian — and four injured.

These are the first deaths caused by Somali piracy in more than a decade, and they came after a five-month hijacking. That duration is itself the story: a vessel held for the length of a growing season implies no effective naval response, and there have been at least fifteen reported attacks since April.

The context this brief has been tracking makes the resurgence legible. Naval attention in the region is concentrated on Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb, where warships from several countries are escorting traffic. The Somali basin is where the ships are not.

Below the line because the US channel is indirect — no American vessel or crew, and no US naval action reported — and this brief has not reached a maritime source on it. It leads if a US-flagged or US-crewed vessel is taken, or if a coalition redeploys.

US politics & policy Tennessee executes Christa Pike today, the state’s first execution of a woman in 200 years, unless the Supreme Court intervenes.

The execution is scheduled for today. Governor Bill Lee refused clemency on Monday. Pike’s lawyers have asked the Supreme Court for a stay, and as of this morning no ruling has been found.

She was 18 at the 1995 killing of Colleen Slemmer, a 19-year-old classmate at the Knoxville Job Corps, and became one of the youngest women sent to death row in the United States. She is 50 now.

The stay application is about the penalty phase. An assistant federal defender says Pike was sexually abused from toddlerhood and raped at eleven and seventeen, and that trial counsel put none of it to the jury and did not argue her age in mitigation. Ineffective assistance at sentencing is the narrow ground on which late stays are occasionally granted, and the age point has more force now than it did in 1996 — the Court’s treatment of youth in capital sentencing has moved substantially since.

Below the line because the disposition is unresolved as this publishes: the stay application is pending and the outcome is the news. It leads tomorrow either way.
