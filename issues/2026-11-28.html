No lead in the water and none in the air at Middletown. The soil results are still out

Environment & health Continuation Primary source

The first set of post-detonation results from the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant arrived early this morning, and they are clean as far as they go. The Environmental Protection Agency found no lead in 38 water samples taken at public water supplies after Wednesday's blast — both finished treated drinking water and untreated water at the intake. Airborne monitoring found zero lead about thirty minutes after the explosion, the same result hours later, and the agency concluded air testing after thirty hours of zero detection.

That is the answer this brief has been recording as owed since the detonation was announced, and it is a partial one by the EPA's own account. Hundreds of agency personnel collected more than 1,600 air, water, soil and dust samples at over 180 structures after the blast, to be compared against 1,600 taken before it; more than 3,200 samples in total are still coming back from the labs. As of Friday evening 88% of post-detonation sampling at previously sampled homes was complete. What has been reported is water and air. Soil and dust have not been reported.

The Army detonated nearly five tons of deteriorating lead azide at Middletown on Wednesday, saying it was the only safe way to dispose of material that had degraded past the point of being movable. The Army has known the material was degraded since at least March; the ATF has indicated it may have been known as far back as 2009. Environmental groups won a temporary stay and then lost before a three-judge appeals panel.

What to watch: the soil and dust results, and whether the agency publishes the pre-detonation baseline alongside them.

Divergence — what “no lead” does and does not establish

Substance. A dispute about significance, between a true headline and an incomplete finding. “No elevated lead” is accurate for the media reported. It is not yet an answer about the event, because the two media reported are the two in which lead from a detonation is least likely to persist, and the medium where it would persist has not reported.

Cause. This is sequencing, not spin. Air monitors give a reading in minutes and water samples turn round in a day; soil and dust require laboratory digestion and validation, which is why the agency says results are returning over days. The agency publishing what it has as it has it is the right practice and it produces a predictable distortion: the fast media are clean, so the first headline is clean, and the slow medium is the one that measures deposition. Lead azide disperses particulate that settles. Thirty hours of zero airborne lead is consistent with dispersal having happened and ended; it is also consistent with very little having dispersed. Only the deposition sampling distinguishes those.

What it means for you. Believe the water result — 38 samples including treated supply is a real finding and the one that matters most for immediate exposure. Do not yet treat the event as closed. The number to wait for is the comparison the agency has already designed: 1,600 samples before against more than 1,600 after, at the same 180-plus structures. That paired design is unusually good, and it means the soil and dust answer, when it comes, will be a difference rather than an absolute level — which is the only form in which it would be worth much. If the agency publishes post-detonation soil figures without the matching pre-detonation baseline, that is the thing to object to.

A $5.9bn Indiana bank failed on Friday, and the insurance fund is out $1.83bn

Banking & finance New subject Primary source

United Fidelity Bank, fsb, of Evansville, Indiana was closed on Friday by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which named the FDIC receiver. First-Citizens Bank & Trust Company of Raleigh assumed substantially all deposits and bought substantially all assets. United Fidelity's 22 branches in Indiana, Illinois, Florida, Colorado and the US Virgin Islands reopen as First Citizens branches; depositors keep their funds and their insurance coverage and need do nothing.

The figures are the story. As of 30 September the bank had $5.9bn in total assets and $5.4bn in total deposits. The FDIC estimates the cost to the Deposit Insurance Fund at $1.83bn.

That is a loss of about 31 cents on every dollar of assets. Bank failures do not normally cost anything like that. A loss rate that high is not what a deposit run produces — a run is a liquidity event and the assets survive it. It is what an impaired loan book produces, and it means the September balance sheet was carrying assets worth materially less than their stated value nine weeks before the bank was closed.

This is the seventh American bank failure of 2026 and the largest since 2024, and the second this year in Indiana after Kentland Federal Savings in July. The system around it is not obviously stressed: the FDIC reported on 24 November that insured institutions earned $84.8bn in the third quarter at a 1.28% return on assets.

What to watch: the FDIC's material loss review, which is required at this size and will say what the examiners saw and when.

Divergence — a big failure or a bad one

Substance. A dispute about significance, and the framings available today point opposite ways. By assets this is a mid-sized thrift and the seventh failure in a year of small ones; wire coverage leads on “biggest in over two years,” which is true and makes it sound systemic. By loss rate it is an outlier: $1.83bn against $5.9bn is far outside the range a healthy supervisory process produces, and that is an idiosyncratic fact about one institution and its examiners.

Cause. Size is the number a wire can get in an hour; loss rate requires dividing two numbers in the same release and knowing what normal looks like. There is also a reporting asymmetry in the regulator's favour: the FDIC's release leads with continuity for depositors, which is the operationally urgent message and is entirely correct, and puts the assets, deposits and fund cost in a single sentence near the end. Nobody is concealing anything; the emphasis simply follows who each party is talking to.

What it means for you. Read this as a supervision story, not a banking-system story. Quarterly industry earnings of $84.8bn and a 1.28% return on assets are not the profile of a sector in trouble, and a depositor at any insured bank is unaffected. The question worth following is how a book this impaired reached a Friday closure rather than a supervised sale, and the document that answers it exists by law: the Deposit Insurance Fund loss triggers a material loss review by the FDIC's inspector general. That review, not the next failure, is the thing to wait for.

The Supreme Court hears the AR-15 cases on Wednesday

Law & courts New subject Primary source

The Court's own December calendar puts Viramontes v. Cook County (25-238) and Grant v. Higgins (25-566) together on Wednesday 2 December, consolidated, with one hour for argument. They are challenges to the Illinois and Connecticut bans on “assault-style” semi-automatic rifles, and they put the AR-15 itself in front of a 6–3 court for the first time.

The legal question is unusually narrow for a case this large. Under the 2022 Bruen framework a gun regulation survives only if it has some historical analogue from around 1791; under Heller in 2008, Justice Scalia wrote that the Second Amendment protects weapons in “common use.” Everything turns on whether the AR-15 is in common use and whether the bans have an ancestor. The plaintiffs' main brief uses the phrase “common use” 56 times; the administration's September brief supporting them uses it 49 times.

The counting favours the challengers before a word is argued. As many as 26 million Americans own one by one estimate, and the Court itself said unanimously last year that the rifles are “widely legal,” bought by ordinary consumers, and that the AR-15 is the most popular rifle in the country. Cook County's State's Attorney, defending the ban, argues from the other end: every child hit by the weapon at Sandy Hook died.

What to watch on Wednesday: whether any justice presses on how “common use” is measured, since a test that counts current ownership makes any widely sold weapon unbannable by the fact of having been sold.

Divergence — a circular test, and who notices

Substance. A dispute about facts that is really a dispute about a definition, and it is adjudicable. Both sides agree roughly how many AR-15s exist. They disagree about what that number means. The challengers say prevalence establishes common use and therefore protection. The governments say prevalence is the thing a ban is meant to prevent, so a test keyed to it decides every case in favour of whichever weapon was sold fastest before legislators acted.

Cause. The circularity is not invented by either side; it is in the doctrine. Heller's “common use” language was dictum in a handgun case and was never specified as a test with a denominator, a date or a threshold. Each side reads the gap in its own favour, and the brief-counting shows it: 56 and 49 repetitions are not emphasis, they are an attempt to fix a standard by assertion before the argument starts.

What it means for you. The evidence on prevalence supports the challengers and says nothing about the legal question, which is whether prevalence is the right measure. Expect the Court's choice of denominator to decide the case and to matter far beyond it: “common use” measured at the time of litigation protects whatever the market has already distributed, while measured at ratification it protects almost nothing modern. If the opinion sets a date or a threshold, that sentence will govern the next decade of gun litigation regardless of which side wins this week. Arguments are Wednesday; a decision is unlikely before June.

Asylum denials have reached 94%, and the Court's December immigration docket is finally dated

Immigration Continuation Underreported Independent corroboration

This brief has been recording three Supreme Court immigration petitions as owed, without case names or argument dates. One of the three now has both: Department of Homeland Security v. D. V. D. (26-426) is set for argument on Wednesday 9 December, according to the Court's own calendar. It is the challenge to removing people to third countries that have agreed to adjudicate their asylum claims.

The other two remain uncalendared. The first is the challenge to the proclamation, issued within hours of the inauguration, that suspended asylum access at the southern border; two lower federal courts have rejected the administration's argument and the ban has stayed largely in force while they did. The third concerns indefinite detention of people in deportation proceedings.

The context is a number. National asylum denial rates reached 94% in June, against about 50% in 2024, according to TRAC at Syracuse University, which tracks immigration judges. Over the same period Border Patrol recorded fewer than 99,000 Southwest apprehensions in fiscal 2026, a 95% fall from more than 1.8 million a year, on the agency's own figures. Thousands of pending applications have been cancelled.

The gap between those two figures is the thing to hold. A 95% fall in people arriving and a near-doubling of the share refused are not the same kind of fact, and the administration cites them together as one success while its opponents cite them together as one injury. Only the second is before the Court.

What to watch: 9 December, and whether the asylum-ban case is calendared for January. The Court's January calendar is normally published in the first half of December, so the answer to the second question arrives within about two weeks.

Divergence — does the 94% measure the applicants or the court

Substance. A dispute about facts, and it is the whole argument. One reading: denials rose because the people now reaching a hearing have weaker claims, having passed through a safe third country, and because the border is quiet. The other: denials rose because the adjudication changed — mass firings of immigration judges, guidance narrowing what a judge may consider, and departmental motions to terminate claims as incomplete.

Cause. The two readings come from different objects in the same statistic. A denial rate is a ratio, and both its numerator and its denominator moved at once: a 95% fall in apprehensions changes who is in the pool, while judicial firings and new guidance change how the pool is processed. Anyone can attribute the whole move to the half they prefer, and both the administration's critics and its defenders did.

What it means for you. The composition argument cannot carry 50% to 94% on its own: a change that large in eighteen months, across a pool that shrank by 95%, would require the remaining applicants to be far weaker than the ones who stopped coming, and nothing in the apprehension data suggests selection in that direction. Weight the adjudication explanation more heavily. What would settle it is a figure neither side has published: grant rates for applications filed before the policy changes and decided after. Those cases have a fixed composition, so any movement in their outcomes is procedure. If someone produces that series, it answers the question in one line.

The divergence this file tracked for five sessions reversed in a single half-day

Rates & markets Continuation Reversal Primary source

On 19 November this brief noticed that Treasury's one-month bill was rising while everything longer fell, and said it was a hypothesis about the December funding deadline rather than a conclusion. By Wednesday it had held for five sessions and this file said so. On Friday it went the other way, hard.

Treasury's curve for 27 November, against 25 November: the ten-year rose from 4.85% to 4.94%, the thirty-year from 5.24% to 5.33%, the two-year from 4.61% to 4.69% — eight and nine basis points across the long end in one session. The one-month did not move at all, holding at 4.27%. The pattern this brief was tracking was the front end rising against a falling long end. What happened on Friday is the long end rising against a flat front end. That is not a continuation; it is the opposite trade.

Two cautions on reading too much into it. Friday after Thanksgiving is a shortened session with thin participation, in which a modest flow moves the screen further than it would on a full day. And it coincided with oil's first weekly gain in five weeks, which is the most ordinary available explanation for a simultaneous move at the long end of a nominal curve.

What to watch: Monday, which is the first full session, and whether the one-month resumes rising or follows.

Divergence — this brief's own reading against Friday's tape

Substance. A dispute about facts, between what this file told you for five sessions and what the curve did on the sixth. The reading offered here was that a front end rising against a falling long end reflected a December funding problem that did not extend to the Treasury's longer-dated debt. Friday is not consistent with that reading continuing.

Cause. Reach for the mundane explanation first. A five-session run in a market that trades every day is a short series, and this file said so when it reported it; the honest description was always that it was a pattern, not a finding. The session that broke it was a half-day with thin volume on a day when crude rose 3.5% for the week, and a long end that moves on an oil print is the most-documented relationship in nominal rates. The alternative — that the funding concern evaporated between Wednesday and Friday — requires something to have happened, and nothing did.

What it means for you. Treat Friday as low-information and the five-session pattern as weaker than it looked, not as refuted. The one-month holding exactly flat at 4.27% while everything else jumped is the detail that keeps the original observation alive: if the front end were re-pricing with the rest of the curve, it would have moved too. Monday is the test, and it is a clean one, because it is a full session with the oil move already in the price. If the long end holds its gain and the one-month resumes rising, the original divergence is intact and this reversal was a holiday artefact. If the one-month follows the long end up, the story was never about December at all.

$81.5m at Guantanamo for 849 people, against a promise of 30,000 beds

Defence & immigration New subject Underreported Primary source

The Defense Department has obligated $81,525,406 on Operation Southern Guard, its mission transferring and holding migrants at Naval Station Guantanamo Bay for the Department of Homeland Security, since the operation began in 2025. It has held 849 people in that time. The figures are from a lead inspector general report published on Wednesday.

That is roughly $96,000 per person detained, and the gap against what was announced is larger still. In January 2025 the president said there were 30,000 beds at Guantanamo and that using them would double detention capacity immediately. The inspector general reports that the maximum capacity at the base since the operation began has been 399. As of 3 September, eight people were held there. About 350 US military personnel were supporting the mission as of September 2026.

Two details from the report complicate the simple reading. The average stay between July and September was seven days, but average detention time has risen since the operation began, which the report attributes to home countries refusing to take their nationals back and to flight scheduling. One person was held for 102 days. The Army has carried most of the cost.

One limit worth stating plainly: this brief could not read the report. The Defense Department inspector general's own index refused an automated request this morning, so every figure above is as CNN reported it from the document, and none has been checked against the original.

What to watch: whether the operation is wound down or re-scoped, and the next quarterly inspector general report.

Dig deeper — why the capacity number collapsed, and what the cost actually bought

The 30,000 figure was never a description of existing capacity. Officials said at the time that such a capacity may have existed at Guantanamo in the 1990s, during the Haitian and Cuban migrant operations, but that there was no way it still did. What followed was a scramble to erect tent facilities and a surge of several hundred military personnel in anticipation of thousands of arrivals that did not come. The inspector general's 399 is the maximum the base actually reached.

The per-person figure is a blunt instrument and should be used carefully. A standing capability has fixed costs that do not scale with occupancy: you cannot run a holding facility, a security detail and an air bridge for eight people at one-fiftieth of the cost of running it for four hundred. Much of the $81.5m bought readiness for a population that was planned for and never materialised, which is a different criticism from waste. The honest version is that the cost reflects a capacity decision made in January 2025 on a premise that was wrong by an order of magnitude, and that the money follows the premise rather than the people.

The detention-time trend is the detail with forward consequence. If average stays are lengthening because origin countries will not accept repatriation, then Guantanamo's throughput depends on diplomacy the Defense Department does not control, and the 102-day case is what that looks like at the tail. It also connects directly to the fourth lead: the third-country removal policy the Supreme Court hears on 9 December exists in part to solve exactly this problem — where to send someone whose own country will not take them. A ruling against the government on 9 December makes the Guantanamo bottleneck worse, not better.

One limit on this item: the report does not specify what the money was spent on beyond the service breakdown. “The Army has carried most of it” is consistent with airlift, construction, base support and personnel in almost any proportion, and no line-item detail is available here.

Divergence — $96,000 a head, or the price of an option

Substance. A dispute about significance. Divide the money by the people and you get a number that sounds like scandal. Treat the spending as the cost of standing up a capability that policy asked for, and you get a procurement decision that was sized to a forecast and not revised when the forecast failed.

Cause. Both framings come from the same report and the difference is which denominator you choose. A watchdog counts obligations against outcomes because that is its function. A department defends against the plan it was given, because that is its function. Neither is being dishonest, and the report itself supplies both numbers without adjudicating between them.

What it means for you. The defensible criticism is not the unit cost; it is the eighteen months. A capacity built for thousands and running at single-digit occupancy by September is a decision that had many opportunities to be revisited and was not. What would settle whether that is still true is in the next report: if obligations keep accruing at this rate while the population stays near zero, the premise is no longer the explanation. If the spending flattens, the January 2025 surge was a one-off mistake that has since been managed.

The UN names fuel costs as a driver of famine in sixteen countries

Global & food security New subject Underreported Single-sourced

The Food and Agriculture Organization and the World Food Programme published their joint hunger-hotspots assessment on Friday, covering December 2026 to May 2027. It names sixteen countries where hunger is expected to deepen, up from thirteen in the June edition. Chad, Uganda and parts of southern Africa and central America are added; Lebanon comes off.

Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, northern Nigeria and Yemen face the gravest threats, as all five have in every edition since 2022. The Palestinian territories move down one level after the October 2025 ceasefire, though the agencies record that 67% of Gaza's population still faces crisis-level hunger or worse and warn the gains are fragile.

Three drivers are named. Armed conflict is driving hunger in twelve of the sixteen. Funding has collapsed: aid to the five worst-affected countries fell by two-thirds between 2022 and 2025, and an FAO emergencies official says the cuts are now also degrading the data collection needed to measure the crisis. And the third is the one this brief has been reporting as an American pump price for weeks — soaring food, energy and transport costs, which the agencies tie explicitly to the war in the Middle East.

A developing El Niño is expected to bring drought to southern Africa and central America and flooding to parts of eastern Africa. What to watch: whether the December–May window produces a formal famine declaration anywhere.

Divergence — is fuel a cause here, or a label

Substance. A dispute about significance, and it is the reason this story is a lead rather than a line. Hunger assessments routinely list economic conditions among their drivers, and a reader can discount that as boilerplate. The specific claim here is narrower: that energy and transport costs linked to one war are materially worsening food access across sixteen countries at once.

Cause. The vantage differs between the two sets of reporting that this file has been running in parallel. Covered as an American story, the fuel price is a number at a pump that is now falling: diesel is down 10.8% in a month. Covered as a humanitarian story, the relevant figure is not the price but the delivered cost of moving food into places with no rail, no refinery and a thin currency, where freight is a larger share of the final price and where the fall has not arrived. Both are accurate about different quantities, and the gap between them is the lag this file described two days ago.

What it means for you. Take the fuel driver seriously but rank it third. Conflict is named in twelve of sixteen and a two-thirds collapse in funding is a larger and more immediately reversible shock than freight. The practical point is that the American pump relief now underway does not reach these countries on the same schedule: it arrives through shipping rates, which this brief reported on Friday are at or near record highs and will stay there while voyages run 23 days instead of nine. The observation that would settle it is in the agencies' own next edition — whether the energy driver is downgraded in the June report, by which time the crude fall should have passed through if it is going to.

Sources: CBC / Reuters, 27 November 2026, reporting the FAO–WFP Hunger Hotspots report; WFP newsroom, checked this morning. Related: 27 November on voyage lengths, 27 November on pump prices.

Oil's first weekly gain in five, and a sovereign wealth fund's tower hit twice in a week

Energy & grid Continuation Independent corroboration

Brent closed the week around $96 a barrel, up roughly 3.5% — its first weekly gain this month and in five weeks — after dipping below $90 earlier in the week. West Texas Intermediate rose about 1% on Friday to around $84.50. The run of falls that took crude from near $120 at the end of October is, for now, interrupted.

What interrupted it was a week of attacks. The Houthis struck Saudi Arabia's largest refinery at Ras Tanura on Wednesday and the Jazan refinery on the Red Sea coast on Thursday, killing a Nepali worker. A suspected Iranian drone killed two Italian technicians employed by Rheinmetall Italia at a Kuwaiti military site, wounding a Kuwaiti and a Pakistani national; Iran denied it. And the Riyadh skyscraper housing the headquarters of Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund was hit for the second time in a matter of days, wounding eleven.

The pump has not noticed yet and will not for a fortnight. AAA this morning: regular $3.5305, diesel $5.6664, both down again on the day — regular 16.9% below where it was a month ago, diesel 10.8% below.

Nothing in the week's news removed a barrel from the market that anyone has quantified. Saudi Arabia's energy ministry has acknowledged a fire and a partial suspension at Jazan and has not said how much of the plant's 400,000 barrels a day is affected, and has said nothing at all about Ras Tanura's throughput.

What to watch: the OPEC+ meeting on Sunday, where reporting this week suggested the group leans towards holding quotas unchanged, and where an independent audit of every member's production capacity is on the table for the first time.

Divergence — refineries and a head office

Substance. A dispute about significance among the week's targets, which market reporting has collapsed into one category. Ras Tanura and Jazan are production assets and a strike on them is a supply event with a quantity attached, however undisclosed. The sovereign wealth fund's tower in Riyadh is an office building; hitting it destroys no capacity at all.

Cause. Price reacts to both because a market prices the probability of future disruption, not only the disruption that has occurred, and an attacker who can reach central Riyadh twice in a week has demonstrated something about reach that no refinery strike demonstrates. The reporting conflates them because they arrive in the same news cycle from the same party. Note also which facts are firm: casualty counts and locations are confirmed by multiple parties, while throughput effects at both refineries remain undisclosed by the Saudi energy ministry.

What it means for you. Separate the two when reading the price. The 3.5% weekly move is mostly a risk premium, not a supply loss, which is why it can unwind as fast as it appeared if next week is quiet — and why it would not unwind at all if an export terminal were actually taken offline. The observable that distinguishes them is loadings, not statements: Saudi crude and product exports from both coasts over the next fortnight. Eleven people wounded in an office tower is a political fact and a security fact; it is not, by itself, a barrel.
