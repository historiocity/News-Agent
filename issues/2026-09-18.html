Three of the four big central banks are now tightening into an energy shock. Japan joined today, and its board did not agree

Macro & marketsNew subjectIndependent corroboration

The Bank of Japan raised its uncollateralised overnight call rate by 25 basis points to around 1.25% this morning, the highest in 31 years. It is the bank’s second hike in three months — the shortest gap between Japanese rate increases since the bubble era — and it came on a split vote. The yen touched 158 to the dollar. The Nikkei 225 closed up 1.38% at 65,018.95.

Governor Kazuo Ueda declined to commit to a path: the bank does not assume “a specific pace” of further increases. He framed the risk as underlying inflation overshooting the 2% target because firms have become more aggressive in setting wages and prices and medium-term expectations have risen. Core consumer inflation held near target in August. A senior BoJ official has separately described Japan’s shrinking labour pool as a slow-moving demographic shock rather than a transitory one.

Count the week. The Federal Reserve went to 3¾–4% on Wednesday, unanimously. The ECB’s deposit rate is at 2.5%. Japan is now at 1.25%. Three of the four largest central banks are tightening simultaneously into the same imported energy shock, and none of them can do anything about a refinery or a pipeline.

What this means for an American reader is duration. Japanese institutions are the largest foreign holders of US Treasuries, and their calculation of whether to hedge currency risk or buy at home turns on exactly this rate. What to watch: whether the yen strengthens from 158 or keeps sliding — a hike that fails to support the currency is the signal that Japanese money has stopped treating 1.25% as enough.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Not the decision, which every account has right, but what a split board means. One reading, carried in market commentary this morning, is that the split is bearish for the yen: dissent signals the committee is near the end of its tightening and will not defend the currency. The other reading is that a split in favour of hiking is hawkish — the bank moved despite internal opposition. This is a dispute about significance, not facts.

Why the accounts differ. Vantage and timing. The bearish reading comes from currency strategists quoted after Ueda’s press conference, whose question is the next three months of carry. The hawkish reading comes from the decision itself, published hours earlier. They are answering different questions and both are internally consistent. Note also what neither can do here: the exact tally and the names of dissenters were not obtained — the Bank’s own statement is a PDF this environment cannot parse, and the one account that reported the split sits behind a paywall.

What it means for you. The evidence supports the bearish-for-yen reading on the narrow point, because the yen went to 158 after a hike, which is the market answering the question directly. But do not convert that into a view on the terminal rate: Ueda explicitly refused to name a pace, and a governor who refuses to name a pace has not told you he is finished. What would settle it: the vote tally and the dissenters’ stated reasons, which appear in the Summary of Opinions in about ten days and in the full minutes later.

Dig deeper — why a Japanese rate rise is a US long-end story

For most of the last decade the Bank of Japan supplied the world with the one thing a bond market cannot manufacture: a large pool of savers who had no domestic alternative. With Japanese government bonds yielding nothing, Japanese life insurers, pension funds and banks bought foreign duration — Treasuries, Bunds, French OATs, Australian government paper — and hedged or did not hedge the currency depending on the cost. That flow was one of the quiet supports under long-dated government debt everywhere.

This brief has been arguing since 10 September that the repricing at the long end of the US curve is global rather than American: Bunds at their highest since 2011, JGBs through 3% for the first time in three decades, UK 30-year paper near 1998 levels, and France paying more than Italy for ten-year money for the first time on record. The control observation for that argument has been that the same direction shows up in currencies and central banks that have nothing to do with US fiscal policy. Today is another one, and a cleaner one than most: Japan raised rates because of imported energy inflation, which is the identical shock driving the American print.

The mechanism to watch is the hedge, not the headline. A Japanese investor buying a 10-year Treasury at 4.86% earns that yield minus the cost of hedging dollar exposure, which tracks the short-rate differential. When the BoJ raises and the Fed is on hold, that spread compresses and the unhedged trade gets riskier at the same time the hedged trade gets less profitable. The domestic alternative simultaneously improves. None of this happens in a week; it happens over quarters, and it shows up as weaker foreign participation in Treasury auctions rather than as a headline.

Which is why the 4 November refunding remains the date on this thread. The 10 September 30-year auction was the opposite of a warning — it stopped through the when-issued yield by 2.7 basis points, bid-to-cover 2.61 against a 2.38 average, and indirect bidders, the category that includes foreign accounts, took 79.5% while dealers were left with 2.2% against an 11.5% average. That is foreign demand arriving, not leaving. If the BoJ’s tightening is going to matter to the US long end, the place it becomes visible is the indirect share at the next long-dated auction and the composition at the November refunding, where Treasury resets the buyback floor.

And the honest limit. Today’s JGB yields were not obtained. The Bank’s statement PDF would not parse, Japanese government bond levels were not carried on the two Japanese sources that did fetch, and no reachable source printed a dated 10-year JGB yield for today. So the claim that this raises the global anchor for duration is an argument from the policy rate and the currency, not from the Japanese bond market. That is a real gap and it is worth closing tomorrow.

Correction: this brief told you the ten-year went through 5%. Treasury’s own series says it never did

Macro & marketsUS debt & fiscalPrimary source

Correction

On 14, 15, 16 and 17 September this brief printed the 10-year Treasury yield as 5.00%, 5.041%, 5.016% and 4.943%, and built a lead story on 15 September around the 10-year going “through” 5% for the first time since July 2007. Treasury’s own Daily Par Yield Curve — the official published series — shows 4.88%, 4.91%, 4.94% and 4.86% for those four dates. On the official series the 10-year has not printed above 4.94% this month. The 5% crossing did not happen in the series of record. The sourcing caveat was disclosed at the time; the number was still wrong, and it was the number the story turned on.

Treasury’s page fetched cleanly on the first attempt today, which is the part that stings: the corroborating source this brief said on Wednesday it had failed to reach was one request away. Here is the week, as published by the issuer.

What survives. The analysis does. This brief said on Thursday that a central bank hiking while the long end rallies is the market pricing the hike as inflation control rather than as a fiscal or credit problem. On Treasury’s series that is more true, not less: the 10-year fell 8 basis points from Wednesday to Thursday, 4.94% to 4.86%, and the 30-year fell 6, from 5.35% to 5.29%. The 2-year fell 5, to 4.40%. The direction was right; the level was not.

Divergence

The point in dispute. A spread of 10 to 13 basis points on the same instrument on the same four dates, between Treasury’s constant-maturity series and the vendor quotes this brief relayed. It is a dispute about facts and it is adjudicable, because one of the two sources is the issuer.

Why. Reach for the mundane explanation, and there are two. First, staleness, and there is direct evidence for it: the 4.943% printed on Thursday morning is within a third of a basis point of Treasury’s Wednesday close of 4.94%. A search-result summary of a quote page, read before the market’s own close, will often be reporting yesterday. Second, instrument: the par yield curve is a constant-maturity construction from bid-side quotes taken around 3:30pm Eastern, not the on-the-run note’s intraday high. Those two can differ by a few basis points. What neither explanation covers is 5.041%, which has no counterpart anywhere on Treasury’s curve that week — the 20-year was 5.40% and the 30-year 5.36% on that date, so it was not a tenor mix-up either. Note the offset is not uniform: at the 30-year the official series ran slightly above what this brief printed, which argues against a single systematic error and for a mixture of staleness and intraday quoting.

What it means for you, and the rule going forward. Treat Treasury’s par yield curve as the series of record for any dated level, and treat a yield figure sourced from a search summary as unusable to the basis point. This brief will do the same: the level comes from Treasury, direction may come from a ticker. What would fully settle the residual: the Fed’s H.15 daily release and an intraday chart for 15 September, neither of which was reached today. Until then the honest statement is that the September high on the official 10-year series is 4.94%, set on Wednesday 16 September, and that whether this is a multi-decade high was not verified today — the “highest since July 2007” claim travelled with the withdrawn number and is withdrawn with it.

OPM is writing the rulebook for a new political hiring schedule — and routing interns into the at-will one

US politics & policyNew subjectUnderreportedPrimary source

The Office of Personnel Management published a 23-page proposed rule this morning, Employment in the Excepted Service, RIN 3206-AO92, docket OPM-2026-0166, comments close 17 November. It amends ten parts of Title 5 and it is the implementing regulation for two years of executive orders on the civil service. A targeted search for coverage of it returned nothing at all.

Three things are in it. First, regulatory text for Schedule G, created by Executive Order 14317 on 17 July 2025. Schedule G covers “noncareer positions that are of a policy-making or policy-advocating character and are normally subject to change as a result of a Presidential transition.” Agencies may appoint to it only “upon specific authorization by OPM,” and every position carries a unique identifier — which means OPM will hold a central, countable register of them.

Second, conforming text for Schedule Policy/Career, the renamed Schedule F, and for Schedule E, administrative law judges.

Third, and sharpest: the rule would authorise Pathways Program conversions into Schedule Policy/Career. Pathways is the intern and recent-graduate pipeline. A conversion would produce “a permanent position in Schedule Policy/Career” that “does not itself confer competitive status, career tenure, or a career or career-conditional appointment.” Competitive status arrives only after two years of continuing service, and — the operative sentence — “pre-conversion Pathways service does not count toward the 2 years.”

So the entry-level pipeline into the federal government would deliver people into the schedule whose defining feature is reduced removal protection, with their clock reset to zero. What to watch: the comment file by 17 November, and whether any agency publishes a Schedule G position count.

Dig deeper — what part 302 actually does, and why the tidying matters

The least-discussed half of this rule is part 302, which governs how excepted-service jobs get filled. OPM proposes to eliminate the existing apparatus outright: “priority reemployment lists, reemployment lists, regular employment lists, Orders A, B, and C” all go, replaced by case-by-case evaluation in which agencies must identify and consider “qualified and available priority candidates before selecting non-priority candidates.” OPM’s stated rationale is that the current procedures are “more complex than necessary.”

They are. They are also enumerated, and that is the whole difference. A list with an order on it produces a record: who was on it, in what rank, and whether the agency went past them. A standard that says consider priority candidates first produces a judgement, which is much harder to contest. The rule preserves veterans’ preference, and says so, and the statutory hook — 5 U.S.C. §3320 — is real; it also preserves compensable-injury restoration rights. What it does not preserve is the mechanism by which a passed-over candidate could previously point to a document. Whether that matters in practice is exactly the sort of thing the comment file will tell you, and it is why the identity of the commenters is worth more here than their arguments: if federal-employee unions and veterans’ service organisations file on part 302 rather than on Schedule G, that is the sophisticated reading of where this rule bites.

Put it next to the other instrument this brief is tracking. On 11 September Judge George O’Toole in the District of Massachusetts blocked OPM from requiring applicants for career civil service jobs to write essays on advancing the President’s policies, holding it likely to violate the Administrative Procedure Act by infringing First Amendment speech rights. That was an ad hoc instrument bolted onto the hiring process, and a single order reached it government-wide because OPM owns the process. This rule is the opposite approach: build the thing into the regulations, through notice-and-comment, with a real 60-day window and a stated statutory basis. A properly promulgated rule is far harder to enjoin than a memo, and the standard of review is the deferential one.

The structural pattern, which is now the fourth instance this brief has logged in three weeks. Section 338 of the Tariff Act, unlitigated for 96 years, switched on against Canada. The Alien Terrorist Removal Court, dormant for 30 years, used once. The bank examination threshold doubled inside a housing-supply bill. Two structural defaults removed from nonimmigrant status in a month. In each case the authority already existed and the novelty was in using it. Schedule G fits the pattern with one difference worth noting in the government’s favour: this one is being done in the open, in the Federal Register, with a comment period, which is more procedural regularity than the other three got.

Honest limits. The rule was read in full text at the Federal Register’s text endpoint. Executive Order 14317 itself was not read; its date, citation and the Schedule G definition are quoted from this rule’s own preamble. No position counts, no cost estimate and no affected-employee estimate were extracted, and the proposal may not contain them. And the rule does not say how many Schedule G positions OPM has authorised to date — which, given that the rule creates the unique-identifier register, is the number to ask for.

Google supplied the exemptions. At least ten states introduced them, and they cover almost every chatbot anyone uses

AI & tech industryNew subjectSingle-sourced

NPR reported this morning that Google worked with state legislators and intermediaries to shape chatbot-safety bills, supplying draft language and exemptions that would put its own products outside the resulting statutes. The reporting names identical or near-identical exemption language in bills in at least ten states — Colorado, Hawaii, Arizona, Idaho, Iowa, Nebraska, New Jersey, Oklahoma, Oregon, Washington and Georgia — most of them modelled on California’s SB 243 from 2025.

The Colorado law, HB 26-1263, signed in May, is the clearest case. It excludes twelve categories, among them products for developers and researchers, commerce and customer-service assistance, chatbots in search engines, business productivity tools, features inside other software applications, and voice-activated virtual assistants. Read together, those carve-outs would place ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude and Gemini outside a chatbot-safety statute.

Two states broke the pattern. Hawaii stripped the exemptions before enacting Act 248. Arizona’s governor vetoed its bill in June, writing that she would not protect technology companies and chatbots “more than children.” A Colorado sponsor defended the language on competitive grounds — the state could not pass a bill that made it an outlier. A Hawaii legislator said the framework had been provided by Google early on.

Google told NPR it is committed to supporting “thoughtful, effective AI legislation.” At least 75 lawsuits are pending against AI developers over alleged chatbot harms, many involving children. What to watch: whether the pending California bills, due by 30 September, carry the same carve-outs.

Disclosure. This brief is written with an Anthropic model, and Claude is one of the products the Colorado exemptions would cover. Read this story with that in mind. The standing rule in this brief’s configuration is to apply the same scepticism to the reader’s own interests and to the writer’s; the exemption analysis above is NPR’s reading of the statute, and the statute is public.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether an exemption list of this kind is a loophole or a scope definition. NPR and the advocates it quotes read the twelve Colorado categories as carve-outs that hollow out the law. The bill’s sponsor reads them as the ordinary work of confining a statute to companion chatbots rather than to every piece of software with a text box. This is a dispute about significance, and both readings describe the same twelve paragraphs.

Why they land where they do. The sponsor’s interest is a bill that passes and survives; a definition broad enough to reach a customer-service widget is a definition that draws opposition from every industry in the state, and his “outlier” point is a real legislative constraint rather than a dodge. The advocates’ interest is a statute that reaches the products their clients’ children actually used — which, in the cases named in the reporting, were general-purpose assistants and character chatbots, not customer-service bots. Google’s interest needs no explanation. Note also the vantage: a bereaved parent and a plaintiffs’ lawyer are assessing the law against specific deaths; a legislator is assessing it against a floor vote.

What it means for you. The evidence favours the loophole reading on one specific test, and it is a test you can apply yourself: does the statute reach the product that generated the lawsuits? The lawsuits named in the reporting involve Character.AI, ChatGPT and Google’s own products, and general-purpose assistants sit inside the exclusions. A safety statute that exempts the products whose harms prompted it has a scope problem regardless of anyone’s motives. What would settle the broader question: the first enforcement action under HB 26-1263 or Hawaii’s Act 248, and which products the state attorney general asserts jurisdiction over. Hawaii, having removed the exemptions, is now the natural control case against Colorado.

Dig deeper — why this lands on the preemption fight, and the sourcing problem

The sourcing first, because it governs how much weight this carries. This is one newsroom. NPR’s story is syndicated across dozens of member stations and the search results look like broad corroboration; they are not. It was read here through St. Louis Public Radio because NPR’s own article pages have timed out on every direct attempt for four consecutive days. No second newsroom was found on it. The statutory text of HB 26-1263 was not read directly — the twelve exclusion categories are NPR’s enumeration — and the model-legislation drafts NPR describes were not obtained. The quotes attributed to legislators and advocates are NPR’s. This is a well-sourced piece of reporting from a serious newsroom about documents it has and this brief does not, and it is still one source. It runs under a Single-sourced label for that reason, per this brief’s standing instruction to run such stories rather than hold them.

Now the structural point, which is the reason it leads rather than sits in the shorts. This brief has been tracking a three-way fight: states legislating on AI, the Justice Department’s AI Litigation Task Force preparing preemption suits, and Congress unable to pass anything. The standing observation has been that some state approaches are much harder to preempt than others — California’s SB 813 and AB 1405, which licence third-party AI auditors, are close to the core of what states indisputably do, because licensing professionals inside your own borders is not a field the federal government has occupied.

Industry-drafted chatbot statutes are the other end of that spectrum, and they solve the industry’s problem without needing preemption at all. A federal preemption fight is expensive, slow and uncertain. Twenty near-identical state statutes with the same exclusions produce something better than preemption from a developer’s point of view: a settled national standard that the developer wrote, enacted by the states themselves, with no constitutional question to litigate. It also forecloses the future. The strongest argument in the NPR piece is not about any single carve-out — it is that once a legislature has passed a chatbot law, the subject is treated as settled and the second, stronger bill becomes much harder to move.

What this predicts, and it is testable. If the analysis is right, the Justice Department’s preemption task force should show no interest in Colorado’s law and considerable interest in California’s auditor statutes and Colorado’s attorney-general ADMT rulemaking — the revised draft of which is due 23 September, with comments closing 26 October. The conduct rules that industry did not write are the ones that get challenged. Watch also the four California bills awaiting signature by 30 September: SB 1000, which removes the one-million-monthly-user threshold from the AI Transparency Act and carries an urgency clause so it bites on signature, is the one whose scope language is worth reading against Colorado’s twelve exclusions.

One thing not to overclaim. Nothing in this reporting establishes that Google drafted the Colorado exclusions verbatim, and the Hawaii legislator’s statement about a framework provided early on is about Hawaii. “Google supplied language that appears in bills in at least ten states” is the supportable claim. “Google wrote these laws” is not, and the difference matters if you are deciding how much weight to put on it.

Sources: NPR, via St. Louis Public Radio · This brief, 11 September

Diesel’s ninth straight record, on a day crude fell. Europe is reaching for reserves; the US has a million barrels of the right stuff

Energy & gridMacro & marketsPrimary source

AAA’s national diesel average is $6.4476 this morning, a ninth consecutive all-time record — up 5.2 cents on yesterday, 39.2 cents on the week, 17.9% in thirty days and 73.9% on the year ($3.7087 a year ago). Gasoline is $4.4687 against $3.2036 a year ago. The federal weekly print is unchanged at $6.285 for the week ending 14 September; the next one is Tuesday 22 September.

Crude, meanwhile, fell. Brent was $104.80 on the feed this brief names, down fractionally on the day; WTI has been testing $99. The reason is Saudi: Aramco is working to bypass the damaged section of the East–West pipeline and is seeking to restore about half its capacity within days, with full capability in roughly six weeks. Pump stations 8 and 9 were the ones hit on 10 September.

That divergence — crude down, diesel at a record — is the whole American story in one line. The binding constraint is not barrels, it is distillate: refinery utilisation touched 98% in late August, the highest since 2018, so there is no domestic slack to convert cheaper crude into diesel.

Europe is now acting on it. President Macron said today he will convene a G7 meeting in the coming weeks on coordinating strategic stockpile levels, export cooperation and production capacity, with more than 11% of French service stations out of fuel. Germany’s coalition is reported close to agreeing a fuel price cap and a pump rebate. What to watch: whether any G7 release covers products rather than crude.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the Saudi pipeline restart is relief. The crude tape says yes — three consecutive sessions of declines on the restart plan and the Sohar and Yanbu workarounds. The retail diesel print says no, and said it again this morning, for the ninth day running. This is a dispute about significance and both measurements are correct.

Why they point opposite ways. They are measuring different molecules at different points in a chain with a bottleneck in the middle. A pipeline restart puts Saudi crude back on water toward Yanbu and out of the Hormuz queue. It does nothing about the fact that Atlantic-basin refineries are running flat out and distillate inventories are thin — East Coast distillate stocks were 19.3 million barrels at the last reading this brief carried. Timing matters too: a restart “within days” at half capacity, with full service six weeks out, reprices futures today and reaches a truck stop, if at all, in weeks. And note the asymmetry the wires keep missing — a crude glut cannot fix a refining shortage, but a refining shortage will happily coexist with cheaper crude and wider cracks.

What it means for you. Do not read a softer crude tape as relief at the pump; this brief has now said that three times and it has been right each time. The number that would mean actual relief is a distillate inventory build or utilisation breaking below the high 90s, and neither has happened. The reading the evidence supports: crude prices are normalising toward a war premium of roughly $40 a barrel over last year, while diesel is being set domestically by a refining constraint that no G7 stockpile decision touches. What would settle it: the EIA weekly distillate stocks and utilisation figures, and the September CPI in October — the first month lived entirely inside $6 diesel, where the lines to read are food-at-home and transportation services.

Dig deeper — the million barrels, and what a G7 product release would actually do

Start with the American cupboard, because it is the part nobody checks. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve holds roughly 285 million barrels, its lowest since 1982, and there is no draw authorisation. But the SPR is crude. Releasing crude into a market where refineries are already at 98% utilisation gives refiners feedstock they cannot process faster and does approximately nothing for diesel.

The federal government’s only distillate reserve is the Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve: approximately one million barrels of ultra-low-sulphur diesel, held at four sites — 200,000 barrels at South Portland, Maine; 200,780 at Boston; 300,347 at Groton and New Haven, Connecticut; and 300,000 in New York Harbour. It is released under the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, by email auction, in an emergency. It was created after the 2000 New England heating-oil spike and it is sized for exactly that: a regional winter disruption. Against national distillate consumption it is a rounding error. So when the G7 discusses releasing product stocks, understand that this is largely a European instrument — the EU and IEA member states hold obligatory stocks in product form to a far greater degree than the United States does — and that the American contribution to a coordinated product release would be small, slow and geographically confined to New England.

What Macron is actually asking for, and what it would do. Four things, per his own framing: coordinating the level of strategic stocks, cooperating on exports, supporting refinery operations, and protecting energy infrastructure from hybrid threats including cyberattack. The first two are the classic IEA collective-action playbook and they work on a price spike driven by a temporary supply interruption. The third is the one that matters for this shock and is the hardest, because “supporting refinery operations” means subsidising or de-mothballing capacity that was closed on a demand-peak assumption, and that is a multi-year decision being taken during a shortage. The fourth is new and is a tell about what European governments now think the threat model is.

The pass-through is arriving, and it is arriving through freight and farms. Cass’s August truckload linehaul index — which excludes fuel surcharges — rose 11.3% year on year, a twentieth consecutive annual increase and the largest since June 2022, with shipments up 2.1% after 42 straight months of decline. That is capacity retired during the downturn giving carriers pricing power before the fuel shock arrived; diesel reaches shippers separately, on weekly-reset surcharges indexed to the EIA print. So 11.3% is the floor under the pass-through, not the effect of $6 diesel. And the agricultural trade press is now carrying it directly: Nebraska farmers reporting that record diesel costs are eroding the gains from higher crop prices, with fall harvest — the single most fuel-intensive fortnight in the farm calendar — about to begin. Fuel is roughly 15–30% of delivered food cost. August CPI food-at-home was 0.0% month on month, so none of this is in the grocery data yet. That is the pass-through pending, not evidence against it.

Measurement discipline, restated because it bit again today. Brent quotes disagreed by nearly five dollars this morning: the market data service read $104.80 while Handelsblatt’s front-page ticker read $100.12. “Brent” is variously front-month, next-month, dated physical and contracts-for-difference, and in a backwardated market the adjacent months are far apart. The rule this brief follows: quote one feed, name it, use newspaper tickers for direction only, and prefer physical indicators — a pipeline restart, a transit count, the price at the pump — to the screen price. AAA and the EIA weekly are both used here and are not redundant: AAA is a daily card-and-station average and the fastest honest signal; the EIA figure is a Monday survey of a fixed retail panel published Tuesday, and it is the number that enters federal statistical use, rulemaking records and agency cost models.

Iran is no longer blockading the Strait of Hormuz. It is licensing it, and today it enforced the licence

GeopoliticsEnergy & gridIndependent corroboration

The IRGC Navy says it struck the Togo-flagged oil tanker Trend in the Strait of Hormuz for attempting an “illegal passage.” UK Maritime Trade Operations independently logged a tanker hit by an unidentified projectile, a fire since extinguished, and the crew safe. Al Jazeera’s live coverage reports a second vessel struck. Two unrelated observers, and on this one the author is not in doubt: Iran claimed it.

The claim is the story. Iran’s stated position is that commercial vessels may transit only on routes Iran designates, only after obtaining authorisation and coordinating with the IRGC Navy, and that traffic outside those routes is prohibited. That is not a blockade. It is a permission regime, and today it was enforced with a weapon.

This brief has been chasing exactly this since 13 September, when a single line in a single account said Iran was seeking to formalise control of the waterway including passage fees. There is still no published tariff and still no published coordinates for the restricted zone — the named trigger on this thread, unfired for eleven days. But the architecture of a toll is now visible without its price: designated routes, an authorising authority, and a penalty for non-compliance.

The traffic reflects it. Reuters preliminary data recorded four transits on Tuesday 16 September — two out, two in, no very large crude carriers and no LNG tankers — against seven on Monday and a ten-day average of 18. What to watch: a published tariff or the zone’s coordinates, either of which converts this from enforcement into an institution.

Dig deeper — why a toll is worse news than a blockade

A blockade is a wartime act with a war’s life expectancy. A toll is a property right. That distinction is the reason this brief has treated the passage-fee report as more consequential than any single strike. A closure ends when the shooting ends. A published tariff acquires beneficiaries — the authority that collects it, the pilots and agents who administer it, the shipowners who have already paid it and would rather their competitors did too — and beneficiaries defend arrangements. It also establishes practice, which is the raw material of a claim. Iran has argued for decades that the Strait of Hormuz is not subject to the transit-passage regime of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea in the way maritime states assert; Iran signed but never ratified UNCLOS. A period of years in which vessels routinely sought Iranian authorisation before transiting would be worth more to that argument than any legal brief.

What has actually been established today, stated narrowly. An enforcement act consistent with a stated authorisation regime. That is less than a tariff and more than an announcement. The things that would complete it and have not happened: published coordinates, a published fee schedule, a designated collecting authority, and evidence that shipowners are complying rather than staying away. The last is the one to watch hardest, because it is the difference between a regime and a threat.

The transit numbers, handled carefully. Four transits on Tuesday is a daily figure from Reuters preliminary shipping data and must not be pooled with Lloyd’s List Intelligence’s weekly averages, which this brief has warned about before. Two caveats travel with it. Some vessels transit with transponders disabled, so the true count is higher than the counted one. And barrels and hulls are different units: a single very large crude carrier is worth many product tankers, which is why “single-digit transits” and “millions of barrels moving” are not contradictory on their face. That said, Tuesday’s composition is the informative part — a very large gas carrier with roughly 470,000 barrels of LPG and a Panamax with about 510,000 barrels of naphtha going out, a small products tanker and a dry bulker coming in, and no VLCCs and no LNG. The crude is not moving. Separately, trade press reports at least two LNG cargoes getting through in recent days as producers push for transits, which is the first movement in that category in a while.

The claim still outstanding, and it has gone quiet. On 14 September the Energy Secretary put flows at 10 million barrels a day, “two-thirds or north of two-thirds of the previous flows,” and the President described “total control” with US escorts. Independent estimates put it at 6–9 million. Two arithmetic problems with the official figure were recorded at the time and stand: two-thirds of 10 million implies a baseline of about 15 million against the 20-plus usually cited, and 10 million is above the top of the independent range. CENTCOM said on 16 September that Hormuz is “open,” with more than 900 million barrels assisted since early May and more than 100 ships redirected — a claim, not a count. The limit of escorting. Naval escort works against boarding and against small-boat attack. The state’s missing dengue report finally published — and week 36 is the outbreak’s biggest week yet

Science & public healthFloridaSingle-sourced

The Florida Department of Health’s arbovirus surveillance report for week 36, 6–12 September, is published. This brief recorded its absence in nine consecutive issues; the DOH index now lists it, and the wait is over.

The content justifies the wait in the worst way. Hillsborough County added 39 locally acquired dengue cases in that single week, taking it to 134 since July. Statewide locally acquired cases reached 152, of which Hillsborough is close to 90%. Pinellas is at 7, up 2. Four other counties reported no new cases. Travel-associated dengue statewide stands at 186 for the year, up 26.

Thirty-nine in a week is the largest weekly increase of this outbreak, against 36 the week before and single digits in July. It happened during the third week of aerial adulticiding over South Tampa. Another low-flying helicopter run over the lower peninsula is scheduled for tonight, applying Dibrom at 0.66 ounces per acre; Pasco County is supplying a helicopter for daytime larvicide work and Manatee is also assisting. The State Surgeon General has called Hillsborough the “epicenter” of an “unusual increase.”

The death investigation — an 80-year-old South Tampa woman who died on 4 September — was still neither confirmed nor excluded as of yesterday afternoon. Elsewhere: 7 West Nile cases statewide, 2 confirmed in Duval, and 26 sentinel chickens positive across nine counties. What to watch is still not the case count. It is a second serotype.

Dig deeper — why the curve is steepening through the spraying, and what would actually change the risk

First, do not read the acceleration as proof the spraying failed. Three things make that inference unsafe. Dengue has an incubation period of roughly 4 to 10 days and a testing-and-reporting lag on top, so cases counted in the week of 6–12 September were largely infections acquired before or during the first adulticide runs of 5–6 September. Second, surveillance intensity rose sharply in the same period — an alert, press coverage and a named epicentre all increase testing, and some of the 39 is ascertainment rather than incidence. Third, a nine-week publication gap means the reported series is not a clean week-by-week measurement of anything. The honest statement is that the reported curve is steepening and that the reasons are mixed.

What the numbers do establish. A locally acquired arbovirus outbreak concentrated in one urban county at a scale specialists place as the largest on the US mainland since the 1930s or 1940s. Aedes aegypti breeds in small artificial containers around houses, bites during the day, and does not travel far, which is why the outbreak stays inside a peninsula and why the response is split between night-time adulticide over the adult population and daytime larvicide into standing water — two different operations, two different chemicals, two different aircraft profiles. Pasco lending aircraft to Hillsborough is straightforward at 7 Pinellas cases and 2 Pasco cases. It stops being straightforward if Pasco’s own count leaves single figures, which remains a wake trigger on this thread.

Why a second serotype is the number that matters. There are four dengue serotypes. Infection with one confers lasting immunity to that serotype and only brief cross-protection against the others. A second infection with a different serotype carries a substantially higher risk of severe dengue — the plasma-leakage and haemorrhagic presentations — through antibody-dependent enhancement. A single-serotype outbreak of 152 cases is a public-health problem. The same outbreak with two serotypes circulating in the same neighbourhoods, in a population now containing several hundred recently infected people, is a different risk profile entirely, and it would not announce itself in a case count. It appears in the PCR serotyping line of this very report. That line was not read here, and getting it is the single most valuable outstanding item on this thread.

Proportion on the death. One death under investigation among 152 cases is not a change in the disease. It is the first evidence of what this outbreak can do, in the age group where dengue does it. The family spoke to a local newspaper; this brief does not name her, because the family named her and nothing in the public interest turns on it.

Honest limits, and they are real. The publication of the week 36 report was verified directly — it is listed on DOH’s own arbovirus surveillance index as week 36, 6–12 September. The figures above are not from the report: the PDF was fetched and would not parse in this environment, so every number here is one NPR member station’s reading of it. That is why the story runs Single-sourced despite resting on a state document. The serotyping detail, the mosquito-pool count for week 36 and the county breakdown beyond Hillsborough and Pinellas were not obtained. And nine weeks of surveillance silence during the largest mainland outbreak in eighty years remains a fact about the system, recorded here one last time now that it has ended.
