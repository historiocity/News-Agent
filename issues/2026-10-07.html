Defense Secretary Hegseth has announced “Project Meridian: The Future of Warfare”, a study of automated and AI-powered weaponry, to be led by Elon Musk of SpaceX, Palmer Luckey of Anduril and former House Speaker Newt Gingrich.

Two of the three run companies holding billions of dollars in current defence contracts, and the study’s subject is the category of weapons those companies make. A recommendation favouring drones and space-based assets over tanks is a recommendation favouring Anduril and SpaceX over General Dynamics, and the study is the document that shapes what the department buys.

The legal question is specific and the Department of Defense has not answered it. 18 U.S.C. § 208 is a criminal statute barring an executive branch employee — including a special government employee, the category used for part-time outside advisers — from participating personally and substantially in any government matter in which they, or a company they serve, have a financial interest. A contractor is not covered by it. The department has declined to say which the three are.

That is not a technicality: it is the whole difference between an advisory role with a criminal conflict-of-interest constraint and one with none, and the department could state it in a sentence.

Note what it sits beside. On 4 October the Director of National Intelligence was named AI czar over a new Super Intelligence Force, with a 120-day report due. Within a week the administration has stood up two bodies to recommend AI policy — one under an intelligence official, one under two contractors.

What to watch: whether the department discloses the three men’s appointment status, which is the only fact that matters legally; whether any § 208 waiver is published, since waivers must be written and are public; the study’s terms of reference and deadline; and whether Meridian’s findings and the Super Intelligence Force’s report agree.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether expertise or interest dominates. The expertise reading is genuinely strong and should not be waved away: the people who actually know how autonomous weapons work are the people building them, the alternative is a study written by those who have never fielded a system, and the government has used outside industrial expertise for defence planning since the Second World War. The interest reading is that the conflict here is not incidental but exact — the subject matter of the study maps onto the product lines of the authors’ companies more precisely than in any comparable exercise.

Cause. These are not actually competing claims about the world; they are both true, which is why the disclosure question is the whole story. The ordinary resolution for “we need the expert who is also interested” is procedural: appoint them as special government employees, make them subject to § 208, require financial disclosure, and recuse them from specific decisions touching their own firms — or grant a written, published waiver explaining why the government’s need outweighs the conflict. That machinery exists precisely for this case. The silence about which status applies is what converts a normal problem into an unanswerable one, and no account reached here says whether the department has decided or is declining to say.

What it means for you. Follow the appointment status rather than the personalities, because that single administrative fact determines whether anything constrains the output. For a reader with no stake in defence stocks the channel is the budget: a study recommending a shift toward autonomous systems becomes a procurement line, and procurement lines run for decades — the aircraft and ships bought on the basis of 1990s studies are still being paid for. The second channel is doctrinal and harder to reverse: a recommendation on how much autonomy a weapon may have in selecting and engaging targets is a policy on whether a machine may decide to kill, and this brief would rather that policy were not drafted by the two people who would sell the machine. The observation that would most change the picture is a published § 208 waiver: it would confirm the three are government employees, name the conflicts, and state the government’s justification in writing — which is more accountability than silence, not less.

A $110bn merger closed yesterday and CNN changed hands. Two of the five big studios are now one company

AI & tech industry US politics & policy New thread Independent corroboration

Paramount Skydance closed its $110bn acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery on 6 October, at $31.00 a share in cash, after a contest for control with Netflix and after fending off antitrust suits. The combined company will be called Skydance.

What it now owns is the point. Two of the five largest Hollywood studios. Two major streaming services, Paramount+ and HBO Max. And the networks: CBS, CNN, MTV, TBS, Comedy Central, TNT, Food Network. The franchise list runs from Harry Potter and The Lord of the Rings to the DC Universe, Game of Thrones, Mission: Impossible and Yellowstone.

One line in that inventory is not like the others. CBS News and CNN are now owned by the same company — two of the three legacy broadcast news divisions and the original cable news network, under one proprietor. Antitrust review in this deal turned on studio output and streaming subscribers; the concentration of news-gathering is not what merger analysis measures, and it is the part a reader should care about.

The timing against today’s other media story is not a coincidence a brief should overstate, but it is a fact: on the same day a federal judge heard whether the FCC is using licence renewals to pressure ABC’s programming, a single company became the owner of CBS and CNN.

Premerger notification under the HSR Act was filed on 8 December 2025 by Larry and David Ellison as Paramount’s ultimate parents, so the review ran roughly ten months.

What to watch: whether CNN and CBS News keep separate editorial structures or are consolidated, which is the only question that will matter in a year; the carriage renegotiations, where a combined library is leverage against distributors; and layoffs, since the stated case for any merger this size is overlap.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether this is consolidation or survival. The survival reading, which the companies made and which the antitrust outcome accepted: the competitor is not another studio but Netflix, Amazon and Apple, whose content budgets dwarf any legacy studio’s, and two sub-scale streaming services merging into one is how a broadcaster stays in the business at all. The consolidation reading: the measure of market power is not who has the biggest budget but who owns the libraries, the distribution and the news divisions, and that is now more concentrated than at any point since the studio system.

Cause. The two readings use different markets, and the regulatory outcome followed from the choice of market. If the market is “subscription video” then Netflix’s subscriber count makes Skydance a challenger. If it is “theatrical distribution” or “cable networks” or “national television news” then it is a dominant firm. Note also that the deal survived a competing bid from Netflix, which is itself evidence for the survival framing — a target fought over by Netflix is not obviously being bought to eliminate competition. What no account reached here establishes is what conditions, if any, the antitrust settlements imposed, and that is the document that would resolve it.

What it means for you. Expect a price rise and a bundle. The predictable first move after a streaming merger is combining Paramount+ and HBO Max into one higher-priced service, which is how the synergy case gets paid for, so if you subscribe to both, that line in your bank statement is where this lands. The second effect is on what gets made: a company servicing $110bn of acquisition cost commissions fewer, bigger, more franchise-dependent projects, which is already the complaint about the industry and will intensify. The thing worth actually watching is the newsroom question, because it is the one with no commercial fix: whether CNN and CBS News continue to assign reporters to the same story independently. If they do not, the number of national news organisations in the United States went down by one yesterday, and nobody voted on it.

ABC told a federal judge the FCC has its head on a “guillotine” — because the licences came up for renewal years early

US politics & policy New thread Independent corroboration

A federal court in Washington heard argument on Tuesday 6 October in Disney and ABC’s First Amendment suit against the FCC, filed in August. The mechanism at issue: the Commission, under chairman Brendan Carr, ordered Disney to file renewal applications for ABC’s eight owned-and-operated stations years ahead of schedule. Disney has asked the judge to block the review.

ABC’s filing states the claim plainly: the Commission’s true target is not employment practices or the carriage of one presidential address but “the content of Plaintiffs’ programming”, and it has used the licence review “as an instrument”. In court it described the agency as holding its head on a guillotine. The FCC says the early renewal arises from an investigation into allegations of unlawful employment discrimination at Disney and ABC, is unrelated to Carr’s comments about Jimmy Kimmel or The View, and calls the suit without merit.

The legal structure is what makes this consequential beyond one broadcaster. A station licence is permission to use public spectrum, renewable on a finding of public interest — a lever that exists nowhere else in American media, since a newspaper has no licence to review.

The doctrine being tested is the unconstitutional-conditions line: whether a regulator may use a discretionary process it is entitled to run in order to achieve an end it could not pursue directly. Nobody disputes the FCC may review licences; the question is whether a review timed and framed as this one amounts to a penalty for speech.

The President has repeatedly called for ABC to fire Kimmel and has denounced The View, which is why the record on motive will matter more than the formal rationale.

What to watch: whether the judge enjoins the renewal review, which would be the first judicial check on this mechanism; whether discovery reaches the Commission’s internal deliberations; and whether the discrimination investigation produces any finding, since an empty investigation is evidence about pretext.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Why the renewals were accelerated, and the two accounts are irreconcilable. The FCC’s: an employment discrimination investigation gave cause to examine the licences, and the timing follows the investigation. Disney’s: the investigation is the vehicle and the target is programming, evidenced by the chairman’s own public statements about particular shows. This is a dispute about motive, which is the hardest kind to adjudicate and the kind courts resolve by circumstantial inference rather than by taking either party’s word.

Cause. Both parties are doing exactly what their positions require, and the asymmetry is in what each can prove. The FCC need only show a lawful basis existed; Disney must show the lawful basis was pretextual, which ordinarily requires internal documents it does not have. What tilts the record is public statement: a regulator who has commented on specific programmes while holding those programmes’ licences under review has created the circumstantial case against himself, and that is why the FCC’s brief works so hard to sever the two. Note what this brief cannot assess: it has not read the complaint, the motion, the FCC’s response or any transcript of Tuesday’s hearing, so the account of the argument is second-hand.

What it means for you. The outcome decides how much pressure a federal regulator may put on what you watch, and the mechanism generalises past broadcast. Licences, approvals and merger reviews are leverage over any regulated speaker, and a ruling that an accelerated review is permissible so long as a formal pretext exists is a template — it would apply to the company that closed a $110bn merger yesterday as readily as to ABC. If you want one thing to follow, it is whether the court orders discovery into the Commission’s internal deliberations: that is the ruling that would make the mechanism visible in this case and deterrable in the next, and it matters more than who ultimately wins.

The IRS expects 148.5 million people to claim a $1,000 Trump Account contribution, costing 71 million hours

US politics & policy Macro & markets New thread Underreported Primary source

An IRS paperwork notice published today carries numbers that are hard to credit until you read them twice. The collection covers the election to claim the $1,000 pilot programme contribution to a Trump Account — the savings accounts created as Internal Revenue Code § 530A by section 70204 of Public Law 119-21, enacted 4 July 2025. The mechanism is Form 4547 and Form 8879-TA, under temporary regulations. OMB control number 1545-2336. Comments close 7 December.

The estimates: 148,498,585 responses, at two hours five minutes each, for 71,188,310 hours of annual burden.

Take those seriously. A hundred and forty-eight and a half million responses is close to every adult tax filer in the country, and seventy-one million hours is roughly 34,000 person-years — more time, in aggregate, than many federal agencies spend on everything they do.

That arithmetic is the story rather than the policy. A benefit requiring an affirmative election, a new form and a new signature authorisation is a benefit a large number of eligible people will not claim — and the ones who do not claim will disproportionately be those without a preparer. Two hours and five minutes is also an implausible figure for a single election, which suggests the burden estimate is carrying the whole account-opening process.

What the notice does not say: eligibility rules, limits beyond the $1,000 pilot, investment options, or what happens to the money. This brief has not read § 530A or the temporary regulations and will not characterise the accounts beyond the notice.

What to watch: the temporary regulations, which are the document that actually governs; whether the election can be made on a return rather than on a separate form, which would change the uptake arithmetic entirely; and the first-year claim rate against the 148.5 million estimate.

A CIA officer invented a classified programme, bought $40m of gold bars, and told a foreign official who a US source was

US politics & policy Geopolitics New thread Independent corroboration

David J. Rush, 49, of Ashburn, Virginia, a former senior CIA official, pleaded guilty on Tuesday 6 October in federal court in Virginia to one count of wire fraud, carrying a maximum of 20 years. According to the court filing he fabricated a “highly classified” government programme to justify buying luxury property in south Florida that he intended to resell for personal profit, and separately concocted a bogus “sensitive government activity” to obtain hundreds of gold bars worth over $40m. The Justice Department puts total losses to the government at approximately $194m. Sentencing is 28 January before Judge Michael Nachmanoff in Alexandria.

Then the line that should lead every account of this and does not. Under questioning, Rush admitted providing a foreign government official with information about the identity of a secret source for the United States.

That is a counterintelligence event, and it is being resolved inside a fraud plea. Exposing a human source is the category of act that gets people killed and that ends networks; it is ordinarily charged under the Espionage Act or the Intelligence Identities Protection Act. Here it appears as an admission in a filing about gold bars, with no separate count.

The mechanism is its own indictment of a system. Classification is what made it work: a programme nobody may ask about, funded by money nobody may trace, is the perfect instrument for someone willing to invent one. The controls that catch a $194m divergence — audit, oversight, a second signature — are the ones compartmentation suspends.

What to watch: whether the plea agreement is published and whether it includes cooperation terms, which would explain a single count against $194m; whether the source disclosure draws a separate charge or was resolved in the plea; and whether any congressional intelligence committee announces a review, which is the only route by which the control failure becomes public.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a disagreement between sources but a striking mismatch between what is alleged and what was charged. One count of wire fraud, twenty years maximum, against $194m in losses and an admitted disclosure of a human source to a foreign government. The accounts reached here all carry both facts and none explains the gap. Either the government traded charges for cooperation, or the source disclosure is harder to prove than to elicit, or classification makes the graver conduct uncharg­eable in open court.

Cause. The third possibility is the most likely and the least discussed, and it is structural rather than suspicious. Prosecuting an espionage-adjacent count requires putting classified material into evidence, which the intelligence community routinely declines to permit — the Classified Information Procedures Act exists to manage exactly this and frequently resolves it by the government accepting a lesser charge. So a fraud plea may be the maximum available rather than the maximum deserved. That is a defensible prosecutorial choice and also the reason the public record of the most serious conduct will be one sentence in a filing. This brief has not read the plea agreement, the statement of facts, or any charging document, and cannot say which explanation applies.

What it means for you. Read this as a story about internal controls rather than about one man, because the fraud is the part that generalises. Anything that cannot be audited can be invented, and the compartmented-programme structure that protects genuine secrets also removes every ordinary check on spending — which is why the figure is $194m rather than $194,000. For a taxpayer the practical question is whether the agency has changed the approval chain for “sensitive activity” purchases, and nothing published answers it. The thing to watch in January is the sentencing memorandum: it is where the government must set out the whole conduct to justify the sentence, and it is the one document likely to say in public what the source disclosure cost.

The EIA weekly survey for the week ending 5 October puts US on-highway diesel at $6.199, down 18.3 cents on the week — a second consecutive decline and the largest weekly fall in the series this year. A fall this size before the release lands means the market repriced on the announcement and on recovering Hormuz flows, not on physical supply. That also means the barrels still to come are a second impulse rather than the cause of the first.

Scale discipline, because the direction is not the level: diesel is still $2.62 above a year ago and petrol $1.25 above. A 33-cent retreat from the 21 September peak has given back about a sixth of the increase since late August.

What to watch: next Wednesday’s weekly print, which is the first that could contain released barrels; whether the IEA publishes member-by-member allocations; and whether the Hormuz transit count rises, which is the only measure that would confirm the physical explanation.

US politics & policy A second trucking hours pilot in two days — this one cuts the long half of the sleeper-berth split from seven hours to five.

FMCSA published a second hours-of-service pilot today. Current rules let a driver split the required 10 hours off duty as 7 plus 2 or 8 plus 2 consecutive hours. The pilot would cut the longer mandatory period from 7 hours to 5, permitting 5/5, 6/4 or intermediate splits totalling ten. 256 drivers, 16 weeks, comments close 7 December. Monitoring is the same protocol as yesterday’s: psychomotor vigilance tests, subjective sleepiness ratings, wrist actigraphy, duty records and telematics, analysed with t-tests and chi-square.

Yesterday’s pilot would let a driver pause the 14-hour window for up to three hours. Today’s would let the mandatory rest period be halved. Same sample size, same duration, same instruments, same deadline, different rule. That is a programme, not two proposals, and the repetition is how you can tell.

The sleep science is the harder question. Consolidated sleep is not interchangeable with split sleep of the same length — a five-hour block does not reliably deliver the slow-wave sleep a seven-hour block does — which is why the actigraphy matters and why the current rule names seven.

Below the line because it is the second instance of a story already led yesterday, and nothing here is new beyond the rule being tested. It leads on the pilots’ results, or if a third appears.

US financials The Fed wants to know whether a company owned by index funds counts as a bank insider.

A Federal Reserve proposed rule would update Regulation O, which governs loans by member banks to their insiders and their affiliates’ insiders: modernised text, statutory requirements not yet reflected, and — the substantive piece — updated dollar thresholds, indexed going forward. Comments close 4 November, extended from 5 October.

The genuinely interesting item is buried in the list: the proposal would address how Regulation O applies to member banks lending to firms controlled by large asset management companies through passive investment funds.

That is the index-fund control question, and it is a real problem rather than a drafting nicety. If a passive manager’s funds hold more than 10% of a company, that company can be a related interest of the manager; if the manager also holds a large stake in a bank, the bank’s ordinary commercial loan becomes an insider loan subject to limits and board approval. World economy The Section 301 tariffs on China continue, because domestic industries asked for them — which is the statutory test.

The US Trade Representative published a continuation of actions in the Section 301 investigation into China’s technology transfer, intellectual property and innovation practices — the two actions begun on 6 July 2018 and 23 August 2018. The authority is section 307(c)(1)(B) of the Trade Act of 1974, which governs the four-year review. The actions “will remain in effect, subject to possible further modifications”, with no end date. A separate notice makes a conforming amendment to a product exclusion.

The stated reason is the statutory one: domestic industries requested continuation, saying China’s practices persist and that the tariffs provide an incentive for reform, address unfair competition, and encourage domestic investment.

Note how the test works. Under section 307 the actions lapse after four years unless a representative domestic party asks for continuation — so continuing requires only that a beneficiary says it would like to. Nobody must show the tariff worked, and the parties paying it are not the parties polled.

No rates or product coverage are stated in the notice, and this brief has not read the modification or the exclusion amendment.

Below the line because it is the procedural continuation of an eight-year-old action with no rates disclosed and no change in substance. It leads on a rate change or a modification of coverage.
