They signed Reagan's sentence on Thursday and bombed Kyiv for six hours that night

Geopolitics Continuation Independent corroboration

The first three-way summit between the American, Chinese and Russian leaders met in Shenzhen on Thursday and produced a joint statement containing one famous sentence: that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. The formula is Ronald Reagan's, first used in a 1982 address and written into the Reagan–Gorbachev joint statement after Geneva in 1985. The talks produced no breakthrough on Ukraine.

Hours after Putin left China, Russia attacked Ukraine for more than six hours with ballistic missiles and drones. At least five people were killed, three of them in Kyiv on the mayor's count. Russia's defence ministry said it struck Ukrainian military and energy facilities, port infrastructure in the Odesa region and transport infrastructure in Kyiv, using high-precision weapons from land, sea and air. Three bridges over the Dnipro in Kyiv were damaged — the BBC, reporting from Kyiv, names them as the Podilsko-Voskresensky, the Darnytskyi and the Southern, all now closed to traffic, on top of bridges hit earlier in the week.

The two accounts do not agree on the dead, and the brief is not going to split the difference. CNN had at least five killed, three of them in Kyiv on the mayor's count. The BBC, filing later, reports six killed in Kyiv and two more at a railway station in Lviv, and calls this one of the largest air attacks of the war. Mayor Vitali Klitschko told people to avoid driving into the city, said an unspecified critical infrastructure facility was damaged and said seven medical staff were injured. Take the lower figure as the floor and expect it to rise; overnight casualty counts almost always do.

Putin had said what was coming while he was still in Shenzhen. Asked about Ukraine's recent strikes on the Kerch bridge, he said “There will be no negotiations” and that Russia's response would be far more painful than what was done to the bridge. He said that at the summit which billed its three participants as a force for peace.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether a joint statement of this kind is worth anything. One reading: the words matter in themselves — the Reagan–Gorbachev formula survived Reykjavik's collapse and was followed within years by two arms-control treaties, so a phrase can outlive the summit that produced it. The other: the test is behaviour, and it was failed the same night. A dispute about significance, and the evidence for each is a different timescale.

Cause. The two readings are not really in conflict; they are measuring different instruments. A declaratory nuclear formula is about the one category of action all three states already avoid, and it costs nothing to sign — which is exactly why it can be signed by parties who agree on nothing else, and why it can later be built on. A six-hour missile campaign is about a war two of the three are party to, and the summit was never going to touch it. Reach for that before reading the bombing as a repudiation of the statement: Putin announced the strike before signing, in the same building, and nobody present treated the two as connected. That is the genuinely new information — not that the words were hollow, but that all three parties regard a nuclear declaration and a conventional war as separate files.

What it means for you. Judge the summit on arms control, which is what it was about, and judge Ukraine on the battlefield, which is where it is being decided. On the first, the thing to watch is whether any verification or notification mechanism follows the sentence, because that is what distinguished 1985 from a communiqué; the last US–Russia treaty expired in February and nothing has replaced it. On the second, nothing changed. What would settle whether the statement is real: a resumed strategic stability dialogue, a notification agreement, or China joining any numerical framework — the last of which would be unprecedented and is the only part of this that would be genuinely historic.

What to watch: whether any arms-control mechanism follows the statement; whether China accepts any numerical limit; the pace of strikes on Ukrainian energy as winter sets in; the Dnipro bridges; and whether Putin's promised response continues.

The White House replaced its cyber director in the middle of the outages

US politics & policy AI & tech industry Continuation Single source

Sean Cairncross is being replaced as White House national cyber director, a source close to the White House said on Thursday. He was nominated by this president, Senate-confirmed, and had served more than a year. Kirsten Davies, the Senate-confirmed chief information officer at the Defense Department, takes the role in an acting capacity. A White House official confirmed Cairncross is leaving the administration.

The stated reason is unusually blunt. The source said the president “fully supported firing Sean Cairncross from his position as he lacked the strong cyber credentials to perform the job”. That is a judgement about a nominee the same White House put forward and the Senate confirmed.

The timing is the whole of the story. It comes in the fortnight that produced a worldwide Microsoft outage, a five-hour nationwide FAA ground stop, a United Airlines system failure, a Chase banking outage and a multi-day failure of the NSA's own internal classified network — four of which were caused by planned changes rather than attackers, as this brief has reported since Monday. The Office of the National Cyber Director is the body that coordinates federal cybersecurity policy across agencies.

Cairncross's own leaving statement is worth reading for one word. He said he was proud of work to strengthen cybersecurity, protect critical infrastructure, confront foreign threats and “position America to lead the world in Super Intelligence” — the federal term of art this file has tracked since October, now used in a departing official's valediction. He also played a key role in the administration's dealings with major AI firms.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is accountability or scapegoating. The stated reason is competence, and a director whose tenure ends in the worst outage fortnight in years is an obvious place to assign responsibility. The objection is that none of the five failures was a cyberattack — they were a router, a software update, a cloud problem and a patch — and the national cyber director has no operational authority over any of them. A dispute about facts: what the job is.

Cause. Reach for the structural explanation first. The Office of the National Cyber Director writes strategy and coordinates policy; it does not run networks, patch servers or supervise contractors. Removing its head after a fortnight of change-control failures is therefore a category error if read as fixing the problem, and perfectly rational if read as answering a political demand for someone to have been held responsible. The second vantage matters too: the departure is announced through “a source close to the White House” using language harsher than any on-the-record statement, which is how an administration signals that a firing was deliberate without owning it. Note what the replacement tells you: the acting successor is the Pentagon's chief information officer — an operator, not a strategist.

What it means for you. Watch whether the office survives in its current form. An acting director drawn from an operational CIO role, at a moment when the administration's own explanation of the NSA outage has shifted from maintenance to a patch to artificial intelligence, is consistent with folding cyber policy back toward operations and away from independent coordination. Believe that Cairncross is out, that the stated reason is credentials, and that none of the five outages has been attributed to an attacker. What would settle it: whether a permanent nominee is sent to the Senate; whether the office's budget or statutory role changes; and whether CISA — which has refused this brief's requests for two days — publishes anything on the patch failures.

What to watch: whether a permanent nominee is named; any change to the office's remit; CISA's posture; whether NSANet restoration completes; and whether the administration's AI-blowback framing hardens into policy.

Confidence: Single-sourced on the firing — one newsroom, one source close to the White House for the reason, with a White House official confirming the departure and Cairncross's own statement on the record. Sources: CNN · 19 November

Two stays denied in one day, and one execution took more than an hour

US politics & policy Continuation Primary source

The Supreme Court issued two miscellaneous orders on Thursday, each denying a stay of execution and the accompanying petition. In Dennes v. Texas (26-6187, application 26A716) the application was presented to Justice Alito and referred to the Court; in Overton v. Florida (26-6154, application 26A686) it was presented to Justice Thomas and referred. Both were denied the same day. Neither order records a dissent.

Reinaldo Dennes was then executed in Texas, and it took more than an hour. Staff struggled to find a vein; Dennes moaned about the pain during the procedure. A judicial execution that runs past an hour is not a long wait — it is a failure of the procedure being carried out, witnessed and recorded.

The specifics are on the record and they are numbers, not impressions. The procedure was completed at 7:07pm, 63 minutes after Dennes was strapped to the gurney, and he was pronounced dead at 7:08pm at Huntsville. After repeated unsuccessful attempts to find a vein, the team inserted the needles in his left foot and the left side of his neck. Prison spokesperson Amanda Hernandez said all procedures were followed appropriately, could not say how many attempts failed, said the sites used accorded with state procedure, and suggested Dennes may have exaggerated because he began complaining before the drugs flowed and about places with no IV. That is the dispute in one sentence: the state says nothing abnormal happened, and the thing that happened took sixty-three minutes.

It was the second execution in the country that day. Florida put Thomas Mitchell Overton to death earlier — the state's record-breaking twentieth execution of the year — and his was the other stay the Court denied. Both followed Tennessee's failed execution of Christa Pike in September, who survived two doses of pentobarbital her lawyers say pooled in her arms rather than entering her bloodstream. This brief has carried capital punishment as a thread since October and has mostly recorded denials. Thursday is the first time it has been able to set the order and the outcome side by side from the Court's own file and the same day's reporting.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether an execution that takes over an hour tells you anything about the stay that was denied hours earlier. One reading: no — stays are decided on legal claims, not on predictions about venous access, and the Court was not asked to forecast the procedure. The other: yes — the standing constitutional claim in these cases is that the method risks serious pain, and a procedure that takes an hour while the condemned man moans is evidence about exactly that claim. A dispute about significance, and it is the oldest argument in this area of law.

Cause. The two readings follow from the procedural posture, not from anyone's bad faith. A last-minute stay application is decided on a record built before the execution, and a court cannot admit evidence that does not yet exist. That is why method-of-execution litigation is structurally always one execution behind: the evidence that would support the claim is generated by the event the claim sought to prevent. Reach for that before reading indifference into an unexplained denial — and note that an order list of this kind gives no reasons at all, so nobody outside the Court knows what was argued or what persuaded it. The one thing the order does establish is that no justice recorded a dissent in either case.

What it means for you. The practical question is whether Texas's account of what happened becomes a public record. Execution logs, the times recorded, and how many attempts were made at venous access are documents that exist and are sometimes released; they are what converts a reporter's account into evidence usable in the next case. Believe both orders, the denial without dissent, and the duration as reported. What would settle the method question: the execution log and the medical examiner's account; whether Texas changes its procedure; and whether the next application cites Thursday. This brief has read the two orders and nothing else, and has read no filing in either case.

What to watch: release of the Texas execution log; whether Florida carries out the Overton sentence and how; whether any justice writes separately in the next such case; and the 3 December execution already on this file's calendar.

Confidence: Primary source for the orders — both read in the original from the Court's own site. Single source for the execution account: CNN, carrying an Associated Press report with the prison spokesperson named and on the record. Sources: Order, 19 November (Dennes) · Order, 19 November (Overton) · CNN

Iran is hitting more tankers, and the oil price has fallen a fifth this month

Energy & grid Geopolitics Continuation Single source

Iran's attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz are intensifying. After a period of low-grade clashes, attacks rose in October and have continued at the higher tempo this month, on data compiled by Bloomberg. At least two seafarers are missing after attacks this week — one of them from the LPG carrier Tara Gas, which this brief has been following since Tuesday and which is still burning off Oman.

The price has gone the other way. Brent crude futures have slid about a fifth this month as Middle East exports approached pre-war levels. The International Energy Agency said last week that supply is outpacing demand for the first time since the war began. Flows appear to have pulled back in recent days, though whether that is the attacks or ordinary week-to-week volatility is not established.

Two honest limits on all of this. Damage assessment is poor because producers stay quiet about hits for security reasons, so the attack data understates severity by construction. And the IEA finding is reaching this brief at second hand for the seventh consecutive day: iea.org has returned 403 to every request since 14 November, so the agency's own figures sit underneath two stories here and have never been read.

At the American end the relief continues. AAA's national averages this morning: diesel $5.8982, down 3.16 cents overnight and 16.6 cents on the week; regular $3.6898. The diesel premium over regular is $2.2084, essentially unchanged for a third day after three weeks of records.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the attacks matter to the price. The intuitive reading says a more violent strait should raise crude; it has fallen about 20% in a month. The market reading says volume beats risk: exports near pre-war levels and supply exceeding demand swamp a war-risk premium. A dispute about facts — which force dominates — and this brief has now had to revise its own answer twice in a week.

Cause, and the revisions are worth setting out. On Tuesday this file explained the 64% tanker-rate collapse as falling tonne-miles. On Wednesday it added that part of the freed capacity was supertankers removed by attacks and replaced by smaller hulls. Today's data adds the third term: the attacks are intensifying at the same time, and the price is falling anyway, which means the supply recovery is large enough to absorb a worsening security picture. The mundane mechanism is inventory and spare tonnage, both of which buffer a flow against disruption until they are exhausted — and this brief reported on Wednesday that the ballast fleet sailing in to lift cargo is down about 40% since August. Those are the same fact seen twice: the system is coping by drawing down slack.

What it means for you. Expect the pump to keep falling and expect that to stop without warning. A market that absorbs rising attacks by consuming slack gives no gradual signal; it gives relief and then a step change. The indicator that leads is not the attack count but the ballast count and the export volume, and the one that lags is the price you pay. What would settle it: whether export flows have genuinely pulled back or merely wobbled; the ballast fleet over the next month; and next Wednesday's crack spread, which broke to $88.35 in yesterday's release. Anyone reading the IEA's actual numbers has information this brief does not.

What to watch: whether Gulf export volumes fall; the ballast tanker count; the two missing seafarers; whether Tara Gas is salvaged; and next week's EIA release.

For the first time in eighty years the World Court has found a state guilty of genocide

Geopolitics New subject Independent corroboration

The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, by nine votes to six. It is the first time in the court's eighty-year history that it has held a state accountable for genocide. The case concerns the Myanmar military's campaign in Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017, and the survivors have waited almost a decade for the judgment.

What the ruling can and cannot do. ICJ judgments are final and binding, and there is no appeal. The court has no power to enforce them. Its orders go to the United Nations Security Council, which may choose to implement a ruling by resolution — a body on which the United States, Russia and China each hold a veto, and all three of whose leaders met in Shenzhen on the same day this ruling came down.

One of the Rohingya quoted on the judgment put the distinction precisely. Murshid Alam, driven from Rakhine at twelve, said it is “the difference between being told our history was a tragedy, and being told our history was a crime” that must be answered for.

The American context is not incidental. The ruling lands while the administration has been attacking international justice institutions, and while US law — which makes genocide a federal crime and attaches consequences to a formal determination — treats such findings as significant.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether an unenforceable judgment changes anything. One reading: it is declaratory and nothing follows — the court cannot compel, the Security Council will not act, and Myanmar's military will notice nothing. The other: the finding is the remedy, because a binding legal determination of genocide by a state is a fact that every later proceeding, sanction and asylum claim can build on. A dispute about significance, and both are right about different mechanisms.

Cause. The disagreement is really about what international law is for, and the ordinary explanation is that it does different work on different timescales. Enforcement is immediate and absent here; characterisation is slow and durable. A 9–6 judgment is also worth reading carefully: six judges did not agree, which in a first-of-its-kind ruling means the legal standard for state genocide is now contested on the record by a substantial minority of the court, and that contest will be quoted in every future case. The vantage difference is between those who need a remedy now — a million refugees in Bangladesh — and those for whom the precedent is the point. Reach for that rather than treating either side as naive.

What it means for you. The channel that reaches an American reader runs through refugee policy and sanctions, not through the court. A binding genocide finding strengthens asylum and withholding claims by Rohingya in US immigration proceedings, supports designations under US atrocity-related authorities, and raises the cost of normalising relations with Myanmar's military. It also sets a precedent that can be cited against any state, which is precisely why it will be resisted by governments that are not Myanmar. What would settle whether it bites: whether the Security Council takes it up at all; whether any state moves to enforce through sanctions; whether US immigration adjudicators cite it; and what the six dissenting judges wrote, which this brief has not read.

What to watch: the dissenting opinions; whether the ruling reaches the Security Council; any state sanctions response; citations in US immigration proceedings; and whether other genocide cases are filed on this precedent.

Confidence: Independent corroboration of the ruling and the vote — CNN with a survivor on the record, and Al Jazeera carrying international reaction. This brief has not read the judgment or the dissents. Sources: CNN · Al Jazeera

A law firm has accounted for $125 million in free work, and says it could outlast the presidency

US politics & policy New subject Single source

Kirkland & Ellis has told three Democratic lawmakers, in a letter dated 5 November, how much of its $125 million pro bono commitment to causes the president supports it has actually performed: nearly $12 million of free legal work. The commitment was the price of avoiding a White House-prompted freeze-out, after executive orders targeting law firms that courts blocked as unconstitutional.

Two facts in the letter change the picture this brief and others have carried. First: the administration asked the firm to do pro bono work on the president's triumphal arch and on renaming the Kennedy Center, and the firm declined — without facing repercussions. Second: the firm told the lawmakers the commitment “was for an indeterminate period and could last beyond President Trump's term”.

The refusals are itemised, and the list is the most useful part of the letter. Besides the arch and the Kennedy Center renaming, Kirkland turned down representing a veterans' group that supported the arch, work on the Census, challenges to the Postal Service's transmitting of ballots, and representing scientists in litigation over the administration's climate policy. What it did do went to Commerce, Defense, the Postal Service, the International Development Finance Corporation and an “investment entity negotiated between the Trump administration and Japan” — more than 2,200 hours for Commerce alone.

The firm's own account of why it signed is now on the record, in its lawyer's words. Beth Wilkinson wrote that between February and April 2025 the administration “aggressively threatened actions that would have been extremely disruptive to the firms, their clients, and their employees”, that Kirkland “took the Administration's threat seriously”, and that firms feared clients with business before federal agencies would stop hiring them. She also notes the firms were being probed by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission over diversity initiatives. The seven-page letter went to Senators Blumenthal and Schiff and Representative Raskin, and the firm did not hand over the records until after the election. The same lawmakers have written to nine other firms, saying they have “substantial and profoundly troubling information that this corrupt bargain went much further than originally understood”.

Those facts together are the story. An arrangement widely described as coercion turns out to be refusable in at least some instances, and simultaneously open-ended in duration — a liability with no expiry that a future administration inherits as an asset.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether these arrangements are extortion. The case that they are: executive orders aimed at named firms, blocked by courts as unconstitutional, followed by firms pledging hundreds of millions in free work to the government that issued them. The case that they are not, as the letter now supports: the firm declined requests it did not want, suffered nothing, and characterises the commitment as its own ongoing obligation rather than a ransom paid. A dispute about facts, and the firm has just supplied evidence against the stronger version of the claim it is itself the victim of.

Cause. Reach for the mundane reading of why a firm would say this. A letter to three lawmakers who are about to acquire subpoena power is written to be read later by a committee, and a firm that says it was coerced invites questions about why it complied, while a firm that says it exercised judgement looks like a firm that exercised judgement. That is a reason to discount the letter's framing and not its facts — the dollar figures and the declined matters are checkable claims a firm would not invent to a committee. The more interesting vantage is the administration's: asking a law firm to work for free on an arch and a building renaming is not litigation, and a request of that kind tells you the commitment was being treated as a general-purpose labour pool.

What it means for you. The number to hold is the gap: $125 million pledged, about $12 million delivered, roughly a tenth, with no end date. That is the shape of a facility rather than a settlement, and it will be the subject of the oversight this brief described yesterday — the preservation letters went out the same week. Believe the $12 million, the refusals and the open-ended term, all of which come from the firm's own accounting. What would settle the coercion question: the other firms' equivalent letters, which lawmakers are seeking; whether any firm reports a consequence for refusing; and the matters actually performed, which the letter apparently does not itemise.

What to watch: letters from the other firms; whether any itemised schedule of matters is produced; whether the commitments are formally ended or transferred; and whether this appears in the January oversight agenda.

An appeals court has cleared the way to end deportation protection for Venezuelans

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

A federal appeals court ruled on Wednesday that the administration may proceed for now with removing Temporary Protected Status from Venezuelans, cancelling a lower-court order that had required the government to restore it. TPS shields its holders from deportation and lets them work; ending it removes both at once.

This is the same administration that two days ago signed a military agreement with Venezuela. On Wednesday the defence secretary called the country a “compliant security partner” in Caracas, ten months after an American military operation removed its leader. The legal basis for Temporary Protected Status is a finding that conditions in the country make safe return impossible.

The number is about 600,000 people, and the timing is today. District Judge Edward Chen blocked the expiry of Venezuelan TPS in September 2025, holding that deportation would put people in harm's way and that the administration had overstepped in trying to shorten extensions granted under the previous president — decisions he said needed “careful study and analysis”. The Supreme Court paused Chen's ruling in October 2025; that pause expired this month, and his order was due to take effect on Friday — that is, today. On Wednesday the administration filed an emergency request asking the Supreme Court to extend the pause, and then the appeals court ruled before the Court had to answer. So an order restoring protection for 600,000 people was two days from operating and now will not operate.

The appeals court ruling does not end the underlying challenges; it lets the administration suspend the protections while they proceed. This is the administration's third bid to the Supreme Court on Venezuelan TPS, and courts have separately allowed revocations for Haitians and Ethiopians. The administration's position is that TPS was always meant to be temporary, that previous presidencies extended it without cause, and that conditions in Venezuela have improved — which is the same claim as the “compliant security partner” line, made in a different forum.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether Venezuela is safe. The administration's position across two agencies is that it is both safe enough to end protected status and stable enough to be a security partner. The litigants' position is that conditions have not changed in the way the statute requires. A dispute about facts with a statutory test attached, and the government's own week supplies evidence for its side of it.

Cause, and it is not hypocrisy. The two positions are consistent if you read them as the same claim: a government the United States regards as compliant is, on the administration's account, a government whose country is no longer dangerous to return to. That is a coherent position and it is the strongest version of the argument. What makes it contestable is the evidence base — a TPS termination requires a country-conditions determination, and no such finding has been published alongside the military agreement, whose text has also not been published. The vantage difference is that courts are reviewing a determination's procedure, not the country, and the appeals court ruled on whether a district judge could order restoration, not on whether Venezuela is safe.

What it means for you. For the hundreds of thousands affected the practical consequence is immediate and work-related: TPS carries employment authorisation, and its loss removes the right to work before it removes anyone physically. For everyone else the question is whether a country-conditions finding is now a foreign-policy instrument rather than a factual one, because the same week produced a security partnership and a protection termination premised on the same assessment. What would settle it: the published country-conditions determination; the Supreme Court's disposition of the third bid; and the text of the Caracas agreement, still unreleased.

What to watch: the Supreme Court bid; whether a country-conditions determination is published; removal operations actually beginning; the Caracas agreement's text; and parallel cases for Haitians and Ethiopians.

The United States sold 31.5 million acres of seabed for nineteen cents an acre

Energy & grid New subject Underreported Single source

The Interior Department completed the first US offshore critical-minerals lease auction on Thursday, selling two lease areas covering more than 31.5 million acres of seabed off American Samoa. The areas lie roughly 150 to 240 miles east of Pago Pago, in water between 4,630 and 20,100 feet deep. The sale was run by Interior's Marine Minerals Administration, and Interior called it the first offshore critical-minerals lease sale the United States has held in the Pacific.

The money is the finding. Total high bids were $6 million — and that exactly matched the minimum asking price of $3 million for each of the two areas. Nobody bid above the floor. Across 31.5 million acres that is about nineteen cents an acre. Interior did not identify the winning bidders in its initial announcement.

It went ahead over two obstacles. There is mounting environmental opposition, and a federal court has ruled against a key part of the government's environmental review — the sale proceeded anyway.

This brief has carried deep seabed mining as a thread since October, with a hearing and a comment deadline on its calendar. This is the thread's threshold event and it arrived without a wire story.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What a sale at the reserve price means. The government's reading is a historic first step toward domestic critical-mineral supply and less dependence on foreign chains. The market's reading is written in the bids: nobody competed. A dispute about facts — whether this is demand or ceremony — and the auction answered it.

Cause. The mundane explanation is the right one and it is about cost, not politics. Nodule mining in 4,000 to 20,000 feet of water has no commercial precedent at scale, no permitted processing route in the United States, and a legal position contested in court. A bidder facing that pays the minimum or does not show, and two areas clearing at exactly the floor is what a thin field looks like. The court ruling against part of the environmental review supplies the other half: a lease whose underlying review is legally vulnerable is worth less than one that is not, and bidders priced that. Reach for both before reading the low price as the government giving away a resource — there is no established price for seabed nobody has ever mined commercially.

What it means for you. The thing that was sold is optionality, and the thing to watch is whether anyone exercises it. A lease is not a mine; the next steps are exploration permits, an environmental impact statement that survives the litigation already under way, and a processing facility that does not exist. Believe the acreage, the depths, the $6 million and the unnamed bidders. What would settle whether this becomes an industry: who the bidders are, which Interior has not said; whether an exploration plan is filed; how the court ruling on the environmental review is resolved; and whether any further sale draws a competitive bid. This brief has read no Interior document and has the figures only through the trade press.

What to watch: identification of the winning bidders; the litigation over the environmental review; any exploration plan; whether further lease sales are scheduled; and the deep seabed mining comment deadline on 14 December.

History · The sentence in Thursday's joint statement is forty-four years old, and the last time two leaders signed it they tried to abolish their arsenals a year later.

“A nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought” was first said by Ronald Reagan in a 1982 address and written into the Reagan–Gorbachev joint statement after their Geneva summit in 1985. Allan Myer, a National Security Council staffer who joined Reagan's speechwriting team after retiring from the Army, told CNN the statement “was a catalyst that Gorbachev grabbed onto”, and that the phrase took on a life of its own as the Reagan–Gorbachev principle.

What followed is the part worth knowing. The two leaders carried it into Reykjavik in October 1986, where declassified documents show they agreed to eliminate their nuclear arsenals before a dispute over missile defence collapsed the talks. The summit was judged a failure at the time, and two arms-control agreements followed within a few years, eliminating a category of weapons and cutting the larger ones.

Below the line because it is history rather than news, and this file's anti-topics list is explicit about anniversary material. It runs because it is the only available evidence about what a sentence like Thursday's is worth, and because the precedent cuts in the optimistic direction — which is not this brief's instinct and is therefore worth stating.

Confidence: Single source — CNN's analysis, with a named former NSC staffer and Charles Oppenheimer quoted. Sources: CNN · the first lead

Cyber · Britain spent a secret fortnight pulling tens of thousands of supermarket fridges off the internet, and the first wave of these attacks this year hit US military bases.

The BBC reports that since late October the National Cyber Security Centre, Defra and the Food Standards Agency have run a quiet campaign to secure tens of thousands of refrigeration units at UK supermarkets, warehouses, restaurants, petrol stations and stadiums. Thousands of controllers — each one governing dozens of fridges and freezers — were taken off the open internet, remotely or by staff pulling the network cables and reading temperatures by hand. A worker at one large chain described taking hundreds of devices offline every day for a week and said it “did feel like a race against time”. The BBC held the story until now to avoid advertising the flaw.

Three waves of attacks on refrigeration temperature controllers have already happened this year, and the first was American. In August, fridges at a small number of US military bases were affected. On 11–12 October hundreds of French supermarkets and food-processing sites were hit through Danfoss controllers — France blamed Iran, which denies it. On 1–2 November dozens of supermarkets across seven European countries were hit, Belgium and Luxembourg worst, along with a small number of French blood-bank sites.

The vendor and the researchers disagree, on the record. Ethical hackers at Omega Solutions found the flaw in controllers made by the British firm Resource Data Management; Pen Test Partners verified it. Ken Munro of Pen Test Partners said the controllers had “a similar weak password problem” to the units attacked in Europe and that it was “likely a matter of days until criminals or nation state hackers figured it out”. RDM's chief executive Ged Evans says it was “a vulnerability identified in a test environment” that “went beyond” a normal exercise because it “included dismantling our hardware”, and that patches are out and customers contacted. The researchers' answer is that the hardware is cheap to buy on eBay. RDM says there is no sign of malicious activity on its controllers.
