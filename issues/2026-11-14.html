The United States has lost its measles elimination status

Science & public health Continuation Independent corroboration

An independent commission of experts reporting to the director of the Pan American Health Organization has determined that the United States no longer qualifies as having eliminated measles. The country had held the status since 2000. It was first reported by the Wall Street Journal and confirmed to CNN by a source close to the process.

On Friday Pennsylvania and Wisconsin each reported one further measles-associated death, bringing the United States to ten this year. This brief reported the Wisconsin infant's death on 6 November and the CDC's count of 5,182 cases, the most since 1991, on 7 November.

The test is specific and this brief named it correctly a week ago. The international committee treats an infection as endemic if infections caused by the same strain are passed person to person for at least a year within the same country, determined by sequencing the genomes of viruses sampled from patients. On 7 November this brief wrote that the two available readings of "most cases since 1991" differed on "whether any single chain of transmission has run unbroken for twelve months", that this "is not a matter of interpretation and it is not inferable from a case count, however large", and that it "depends on genotype linkage between outbreaks".

The linkage existed and had been published. In August, scientists at Utah's public health laboratory put out a preprint showing the same strain circulating in the state for more than a year, accounting for more than 95 per cent of cases there — travelling from the south-west of the state to central Utah and causing more than 600 cases between June 2025 and June 2026. There have been sustained outbreaks in South Carolina and elsewhere.

Divergence. A dispute about what losing the status changes, and the two readings disagree about whether this is news or bookkeeping. Read one: it is a label catching up with reality. Transmission has been continuous for over a year; the commission has recognised a fact that Utah's sequencing established in August. Nothing about any child's risk changed on Friday. Read two: the label is the mechanism. Elimination status is what regional verification, funding priorities and international travel guidance are built on; losing it changes obligations and reporting, and it is the thing that makes a slow deterioration legible to people who do not read preprints.

Cause of the divergence. Both are right about different audiences, and the more useful question is what the determination implies about the next twelve months. Regaining elimination requires demonstrating twelve months with no endemic transmission, which means the earliest possible restoration is a year after the last chain breaks — and the chains have not broken. That is the part worth holding onto: this is not a status that flickers back when case counts fall, and vaccination coverage, not case counts, is what determines when the clock can start.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the determination, the 2000 baseline, the ten deaths and the Utah genomic finding as established. The forward question is coverage: what would settle whether this is recoverable is MMR coverage among kindergarteners by state, which the CDC publishes and which this brief has never fetched — cdc.gov has returned 403 here on every attempt for a week. That is the gap, and it has not closed: every figure in this story comes from reporting about American public health data rather than from the data.

What to watch: PAHO's formal statement and the commission's reasoning; state-level MMR coverage figures; whether the CDC publishes a response; the weekly case count from here; and whether other countries change travel guidance for the United States.

The IEA says the oil market turns to surplus this quarter

Energy & grid Continuation Single source

The International Energy Agency said on Friday that the world oil market will return to surplus in the fourth quarter, as Middle East exports recover faster than global demand despite continued attacks on shipping. It had previously forecast a fourth-quarter deficit. Supply will average 105 million barrels a day in the quarter, 1.7 million above forecast demand.

One number in the report is directly about this brief's longest-running story. Of the record release from members' emergency reserves pledged in March, 71 million barrels remain to be made available by the end of February — including 15 million barrels of diesel, explicitly to ease market tightness.

Alongside it, from the freight side: CMA CGM's net profit more than tripled to \$2.32 billion as freight rates surged, and the trade press reports Gulf oil exports recovering while Hormuz shipping struggles.

Divergence. A dispute about whether a surplus ends the diesel squeeze, and the two readings come apart on the difference between crude and products. Read one: yes, and it is already happening. A 1.7 million barrel a day surplus is the end of the scarcity that made the margin; crude has already fallen to \$95.97 from \$105.59 in a week; stocks built 4.1 million barrels; the squeeze is unwinding on schedule. Read two: the surplus is in crude and the squeeze was never about crude. This brief's own explanation since 8 November is that the margin came from freight and war-risk premiums forcing heavy-crude discounts that US refiners captured. A crude surplus with shipping still under attack widens that discount rather than closing it.

Cause of the divergence. The IEA's own framing supports the second reading more than the first: it says exports recover despite continued attacks on shipping, which is a statement that the supply recovery and the shipping risk are running in parallel rather than one resolving the other. And the strongest single datum this week is on that side: CMA CGM tripling its profit on surging freight rates is the freight premium showing up in a company's accounts. Against that, the agency releasing 15 million barrels of diesel specifically to ease tightness is an authority with better data than this brief acting as though product scarcity is the binding problem — which is the first reading's strongest evidence.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the crude surplus as forecast and the product squeeze as unresolved, and stop treating movements in crude as evidence about diesel. The settling observation is whether the crack spread narrows from \$97.99, not whether WTI falls — last week it widened \$3.79 while crude fell \$9.62. The dated test is next Wednesday's EIA release, and the new one is whether those 15 million barrels of diesel actually reach the market before the end of February.

What to watch: the crack spread; the timing and destination of the 15 million barrels of diesel; whether Gulf export recovery continues; container and tanker freight rates; and whether the IEA revises again.

The man setting the benchmark tanker rate wrote "nonsense rate" in the file

Energy & grid Continuation Single source

This brief named this story as its negative check — the largest thing seen and not opened — on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday, and said on Friday that the repetition had become the failure it had criticised elsewhere. It was read first this morning.

At the Baltic Exchange, Christopher Loveday was responsible for helping produce TD3C, the world's benchmark rate for shipping oil. On 23 March he wrote "Nonsense rate" in an internal document. On 25 March: "Still a nonsense." On 31 March, filling in his section of the spreadsheet that sets a benchmark underpinning billions of dollars of freight derivatives: "b/s rate."

The communications are public because of a London case brought in April by the oil trader Mercuria Energy Group, which is seeking a declaration that the Baltic was wrong to keep publishing TD3C in the initial phase of the war, from March to July. The filings show that numerous people involved in the rate expressed reservations as publication continued in the war's early weeks, and that one senior staffer described the company as "so rotten." They also put a spotlight on the Baltic's guidance allowing derivatives contracts underpinned by TD3C to be used in setting the benchmark itself — met, the account says, with shocked derision by some of the industry's biggest players.

The institution is smaller than its function. Founded in 1744 as the Virginia and Baltick Coffee House on Threadneedle Street, the Baltic Exchange employed just over thirty people last year and made a pre-tax profit of £379,000. It is owned by Singapore Exchange. Despite the name it is not an exchange: its main role is to collect assessments from a small group of brokers. The Baltic says its decision was justified and that any breach of its obligations is for its regulator, the Financial Conduct Authority, rather than private litigation. Closing arguments are due this week; the judge may take months.

Divergence. A dispute about how much this damages the numbers, and this brief has a direct interest because it has reasoned from freight data for a week. Read one: the contested window is not this brief's window. Mercuria's claim concerns March to July. The freight-driven explanation of the American diesel margin that this brief has run since 8 November concerns September to November, and nothing in the filings says TD3C was unreliable in the autumn. Read two: what the case exposes is structural, not seasonal. A benchmark produced by thirty people from broker assessments, with guidance permitting derivatives priced off the index to feed back into the index, has a circularity problem that does not switch off in August.

Cause of the divergence. The two readings turn on whether the March–July failure was caused by the war's disruption of actual fixtures — too few real voyages to assess — or by the methodology. If it was thin trading, autumn rates are probably sound and this brief's reasoning survives. If it was the circularity, every TD3C print is suspect and so is anything built on it. The filings reportedly contain enough to distinguish these, and this brief has read one Bloomberg account of them and not the filings.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the quoted messages, the dates, the claim and the institution's size as established, and treat this brief's own freight-based explanation as resting on a benchmark now under judicial examination — which is weaker than it looked a week ago, though not refuted. The settling event is dated: closing arguments this week, then a judgment that may take months. The better near-term move is to stop depending on a single benchmark: CMA CGM's reported results and the IEA's export figures are freight evidence that does not come through TD3C at all.

What to watch: the closing arguments and the judgment; whether the FCA says anything; whether the Baltic changes the guidance on derivatives feeding the index; and whether Singapore Exchange comments.

The intercepts were in the spring, and Rubio wrote a memo

AI & tech industry Continuation Single source

Yesterday this brief led on Jensen Huang resigning while the attorney general said no conclusions had been reached and the intelligence did not prove Alice Huang was a spy. A day later the sequence has a date and a document.

In the spring, US intelligence intercepted communications indicating that an executive of Megaspeed — a company under investigation for possibly diverting sensitive technology to China — was having an affair with a US person. The American was "masked", the standard procedure protecting the identity of US persons whose communications are picked up while monitoring foreign targets. Officials who saw the raw intelligence requested that the name be unmasked. It was Jensen Huang.

Secretary of State and National Security Adviser Marco Rubio then drafted a memo summarising the intelligence and circulated it to a very small group of senior officials, including at the White House. Unmasking an American in intelligence reporting is unusual, requires a request by a very senior official, and goes through a formal process.

Divergence. A dispute about what the memo proves, and it is sharper than yesterday's because the two readings now conflict with public statements. Read one: the government knew and acted. An unmasking request, a memo from the national security adviser and distribution to the White House describe a government that treated this as a serious counterintelligence matter months before any of it was public. Read two: the government knew and concluded nothing. The same government said this week, through its attorney general, that no conclusions have been reached about who Alice Huang is or is not, and the President said he did not know whether she was a spy.

Cause of the divergence. These are reconcilable, and the reconciliation is the point. Raw intercepts showing an affair with an executive of a company under investigation are enough to justify unmasking and a memo, and not enough to establish espionage. The memo summarised intelligence; it did not adjudicate it. What the new reporting adds is not proof of spying but a timeline: the exposure was known at the top of the US government in the spring, and the company's board apparently was not told until the press had it. That is a question for Nvidia's disclosure, not for the FBI.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the intercepts, the unmasking and the memo as reported fact from unnamed sources, and the espionage question as exactly where it was: unresolved by the government's own account. The settling evidence is unchanged — a charge, an Entity List addition, an indictment, a licence revocation. The new and answerable question is governance: when did Nvidia's board learn, and from whom. If the national security adviser circulated a memo in the spring and the board found out in November, that is the story.

What to watch: when Nvidia's board was informed; whether any charge follows; whether Megaspeed is added to the Entity List; and whether the unmasking itself becomes a subject of congressional interest in January.

Confidence: Single source — CNN, resting entirely on "sources familiar with the matter" for the intercepts, the unmasking and the memo. This brief has no document, no named official, and no confirmation from the State Department, the intelligence community or Nvidia. That is thin sourcing for a claim about classified material, and it is the reason the divergence box does not treat the memo as establishing anything beyond its own existence. Every flight in the country was grounded for five hours

AI & tech industry New Independent corroboration

The Federal Aviation Administration issued a nationwide ground stop on Friday, halting departing flights across the United States, citing "extraordinary circumstances and reasons for safety". The FAA says it was caused by a telecommunications outage. Thousands of flights were grounded. It was lifted on Friday evening after five hours, the longest ground stop in 25 years.

It came the day after the Microsoft outage this brief led on yesterday, which left Outlook and Teams down across several countries for more than a day and which Microsoft has still not explained. This brief opened a thread yesterday called cloud concentration and outages. It is now two days old and has two entries.

Divergence. A dispute about whether these two outages are one subject. Read one: coincidence, and different in kind. An email platform failing and an air traffic telecommunications system failing have nothing in common but the word outage; one is a commercial cloud service, the other is federal infrastructure with its own network, and treating them together is pattern-matching. Read two: the same brittleness. Both are single points of failure whose loss stopped ordinary activity across a continent within minutes, and in both cases the operator has described the fault in terms that do not name a cause.

Cause of the divergence. The honest position is that this brief cannot tell, and the reason it cannot is specific. "Telecommunications outage" is a category, not a cause: it covers a severed fibre, a failed router, a misapplied configuration and an attack equally well, and the FAA has said which category without saying which event. Without that, any claim about a common pattern is a guess. What is not a guess is the consequence: a five-hour national ground stop is the longest since 2001, and the comparison that reaches is to the day the airspace was closed deliberately.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Take the ground stop, its duration, its stated cause-category and its ranking as established; treat any connection to Thursday's Microsoft failure as unsupported. The settling documents exist in both cases and neither has appeared: the FAA's incident account, and Microsoft's post-incident review. If both arrive and both describe an internal configuration or capacity failure, the brittleness reading gains; if either names something else, it changes substantially. This brief will not assert the pattern before then.

What to watch: the FAA's explanation of what failed; whether the NTSB or Congress asks; Microsoft's post-incident review; the compensation and rebooking picture through the weekend; and whether any other national system reports a similar fault.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — Al Jazeera's explainer and CNN, both reporting the FAA's own statements. This brief did not reach the FAA directly, has no flight-count figure of its own, and the "longest in 25 years" ranking is as reported rather than checked here. Trump will sit down with Xi and Putin together on nuclear weapons

Geopolitics New Single source

The White House said on Friday that the President will meet jointly with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin next week, on the sidelines of the APEC summit in China. It would be the first trilateral meeting of the three countries' leaders. The stated subject is nuclear non-proliferation.

"The President has made it clear that, among other topics, he will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world. This proliferation, President Trump believes, is very dangerous," a White House spokesman, Olivia Wales, told reporters. Officials played down the chance of an agreement, describing the talks as a starting point.

Two facts set the stakes. The last nuclear arms treaty between the United States and Russia expired in February. And China has been rapidly building its arsenal — the reason a bilateral framework no longer describes the problem.

Divergence. A dispute about what the format is for. Read one: arms control requires it. With the US–Russia treaty expired and China expanding, no two-party agreement can constrain the three largest arsenals; a trilateral is the only structure that matches the problem, and starting one is substantive whatever it produces next week. Read two: the format is the product. A first-ever meeting of these three leaders is an event in itself, announced by a president eleven days after losing both chambers of Congress, with officials simultaneously lowering expectations of any agreement. On that reading the picture is the point.

Cause of the divergence. The readings differ on whether to weight the structural logic or the timing, and both are real. The structural case is strong and predates the election: the February expiry left the United States and Russia without a treaty for the first time in decades. The timing case is also strong: this brief has reported four separate reversals or repositionings by this administration in nine days — on Iran, on the election result, on oversight — and an announcement that cannot be falsified for a week fits that pattern. What would distinguish them is whether anything was negotiated in advance, and nobody is saying.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the meeting as announced and its content as unknown, and do not describe it as arms control talks until someone describes what is on the table. The settling observations are close: whether a joint statement is issued, whether any verification or numerical commitment appears, and whether China — which has historically declined trilateral arms control precisely because its arsenal is smaller — says anything about why it agreed. That last is the most informative and the least likely to be reported.

What to watch: any Chinese or Russian confirmation of the agenda; whether a joint statement follows; what happens to the expired treaty's framework; and whether the Senate is briefed.

Confidence: Single source — CNN, quoting a named White House spokesman. This brief has no Chinese or Russian confirmation, no agenda, and nothing from the State Department; the characterisation of China's historical position on trilateral arms control is this brief's own and is not sourced here.
