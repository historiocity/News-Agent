The Supreme Court left the mail-ballot rule blocked, and Kavanaugh said why: there is no time

US politics & policyPrimary source

The Supreme Court denied the government’s emergency stay application in United States Postal Service v. California, No. 26A305, on Monday 14 September — after this brief published. The unsigned order says the government “is unlikely to succeed on the merits” of its challenge to the District of Massachusetts preliminary injunction, and that the equitable factors do not favour a stay. Justice Kavanaugh concurred in a single paragraph. Justice Alito dissented, joined by Justice Thomas.

The rule is section 705.24 of the Domestic Mail Manual, “Ballot Mail for Federal Elections”, finalised in late August under a March 2026 executive order. It requires states that use the mail to run elections to adopt pre-approved ballot-envelope designs, apply a barcode unique to each voter, and upload their voter rosters to a Postal Service electronic portal before election mail will be carried.

This brief has argued since 8 September that the stay posture, not the eventual merits ruling, would decide whether the rule touched November. That held, and it resolved against the government. The injunction stands and the midterms run on existing ballot-mail practice nationwide.

What to watch: the rule is not dead. Kavanaugh’s stated ground is timing, and timing expires. He wrote that the Postal Service may well have the authority to issue the rule but that election officials “do not have sufficient time” to implement it before November. Merits litigation continues, and nothing in Monday’s order stops the same rule being live for 2028.

Divergence

The point in dispute. NPR and CNBC headline this as the Court rejecting the mail voting restrictions. SCOTUSblog headlines it as a denial of the request to implement parts of the rule. That is a dispute about significance, not about facts — both descriptions are accurate.

Why they land differently. The government’s application was not a request to enforce the whole rule. It sought relief as to specific provisions, principally envelope design and the voter-roster upload. SCOTUSblog writes for a readership that tracks what was asked for; the wires write for one that wants to know whether mail voting changes this year. Neither is spinning; they are answering different questions.

What it means for you. Operationally the wire framing is the one that matters: nothing in the rule applies to any state’s November ballots. But carry SCOTUSblog’s precision forward, because it tells you what the government will try next — a narrower application, on a longer runway, aimed at the provisions it thinks are severable. The observation that would settle which reading ages better is whether the Postal Service refiles in 2027 on the same provisions or on all of them.

Dig deeper — the emergency docket is deciding these cases on clocks, not on power

Kavanaugh’s concurrence is the most informative paragraph in the order, because it separates two questions the headlines merge. He is not saying the Postal Service lacks the power to regulate ballot mail. He suggests the opposite — that it may well have it. What he says is that election officials cannot reasonably implement a rule of this kind in the weeks remaining. That is a holding about administrative runway, and it is the third time in a month this brief has watched a major federal instrument turn on when it arrived rather than on whether it was authorised.

Alito’s dissent takes the opposite view on both halves. He argues the government met the standard for a stay, that the Postal Service has “broad authority to regulate the mail”, and that being unable to implement before November is itself a permanent injury to the government. He dismisses the practical timing objection by noting that some states support the rule and are able to comply — which is a real point, and the reason the government’s next application will probably be pitched at the states that want it rather than nationwide.

The procedural history is worth holding because this brief got one thing wrong about it earlier in the month and one thing right. Wrong: the 7 September issue tracked application 26A297, which the Solicitor General had already withdrawn on 6 September, refiling against the newer 4 September injunction as 26A305. Right: from 8 September this brief said repeatedly that the absence of an administrative stay was not neutral — that it meant the district court’s injunction continued to operate, and that the interim posture would decide November. Eight days of silence on that docket turned out to be the decision.

Two loose ends. NPR reported on 14 September that a second judge had blocked the restrictions while the Supreme Court review was pending; that order has still not been read here, and with the stay denied it matters less than it did, but it is the reason the government’s supplemental brief was filed after briefing had closed. And the First Circuit had already denied a parallel stay, so the government went to the Supreme Court having lost the interim question twice.

For a reader in Florida specifically: Florida is not a plaintiff and its election administration was not the subject of the injunction, but the injunction is nationwide, so the portal-upload and barcode requirements do not apply to any Florida county this November either.

Eighteen states have been restricting what food stamps buy. Today the public was invited to comment

US politics & policyNew subjectUnderreportedPrimary source

Today’s Federal Register carries eighteen separate notices from the Agriculture Department’s Food and Nutrition Service, one per state, opening public comment on SNAP “demonstration projects” that amend the statutory definition of food to exclude things like soda, energy drinks, candy and prepared desserts. Florida is document 2026-18887: requested 29 May 2025, approved 4 August 2025, implemented 20 April 2026. Texas is 2026-18885 — sweetened drinks and candy, implemented 1 April 2026, later modified to preserve naturally sweetened beverages and medical-grade electrolyte drinks. Comments on all of them close 15 October.

The notices exist because of a court. On 22 June, Judge Amy Berman Jackson of the DC district court vacated USDA’s approval of the same kind of waiver in five states in Aragon v. Rollins, on two grounds: that USDA leaned on the wrong statutory authority — section 17(b) lets it test the programme’s administrative efficiency, not recipients’ diets — and that it skipped required public-notice procedures. USDA noticed an appeal to the DC Circuit on 20 August.

Read today’s notices against that and the shape is plain. Every one states its purpose as testing changes “that may increase programmatic efficiency and improve the delivery of SNAP benefits to raise levels of nutrition” — efficiency first, nutrition second, in the order the statute rewards. And not one of the five states Jackson voided — Colorado, Iowa, Nebraska, Tennessee, West Virginia — appears in today’s batch. This is an agency curing the procedural half of a ruling for the waivers nobody sued over, while appealing the substantive half.

What to watch: the comment file by 15 October; whether the DC Circuit reaches the section 17(b) question at all; and whether anyone sues over a re-noticed waiver, which is the test of whether notice after the fact cures anything.

Dig deeper — what the eighteen are, and why comment-after-implementation is the interesting part

The eighteen states noticed today are Arkansas, Florida, Hawaii, Idaho, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia and Wyoming. The exclusions are not uniform — Florida’s reaches soda, energy drinks, candy and prepared desserts, which is broader than Texas’s sweetened drinks and candy — and each waiver runs an initial two years from implementation. By the reporting available here, twenty-two states had approvals as of March 2026, so today’s eighteen are most of the surviving set rather than all of it.

The mechanics matter more than the list. These restrictions operate at the point of sale, through retailer item-eligibility files, which means the compliance burden lands on grocers and the confusion lands on the household at the register. Florida’s has been running since 20 April. A comment period opening on 15 September, closing 15 October, on a restriction already five months into a two-year pilot, is not a consultation about whether to do it. It is a record being built.

That is exactly why it is worth your attention rather than a shrug. Jackson’s ruling had two independent holdings. The notice holding is curable and USDA is curing it. The authority holding — that Congress did not authorise USDA to redefine what counts as food under SNAP, and that the demonstration power is about administrative efficiency — is not curable by paperwork, which is why it is on appeal. Today’s uniform “programmatic efficiency” phrasing is the agency positioning the unchallenged waivers to survive whatever the DC Circuit says about the challenged ones.

The honest limits on this item. What has been read here is the Federal Register full text of the Florida and Texas notices and the API listing of all eighteen; the Aragon opinion itself has not been read, and its two holdings are described from the Food Research & Action Center’s account and trade coverage, not from the opinion. The count of twenty-two approved states is secondary reporting. If any of that is load-bearing for you, the opinion is docket 1:26-cv-00861 in the DC district court.

The reason this is marked underreported: it appeared in the Register, in eighteen pieces, on a day when the political press had a Supreme Court order and a Fed meeting. Eighteen notices are eighteen small documents and one large policy, and the large policy is visible only if you read the whole day’s output rather than the entry you came for.

The federal diesel number crossed $6 for the first time, and none of it is in groceries yet

US fuel pricesPrimary source

The EIA weekly retail survey for the week ending Monday 14 September landed: on-highway diesel $6.285 a gallon, regular all grades $4.319. The previous week was $5.967. The record in this series before this month was $5.810, the week of 20 June 2022 — so the official federal number is now 8.2% above its all-time high, after a single-week rise of 31.8 cents, or 5.3%.

This was the named trigger on this thread. The EIA weekly is the first federal print to cover the $6 diesel weeks, and it confirms what AAA’s daily series had been saying since 10 September rather than contradicting it. AAA today: diesel $6.2694, a sixth consecutive all-time record; $6.2301 yesterday, $5.9012 a week ago, $5.4396 a month ago, $3.6858 a year ago — up 70.1% year on year. Gasoline $4.3289 against $3.1777 a year ago, up 36.2%.

The mechanism has not changed and is worth repeating because it is the part that gets misread. Diesel is being set by refining and inventory, not by the headline barrel. Refinery utilisation touched 98% in late August, the highest since 2018, so there is no domestic slack to absorb a distillate shortfall; that is why diesel set records on days crude fell. Fuel is roughly 15–30% of delivered food cost.

What to watch, and it is a specific thing: August CPI food-at-home came in at 0.0% month on month, so none of this has reached the grocery shelf. September CPI, published in October, is the first month lived entirely inside $6 diesel. The lines to read are food-at-home and transportation services.

Dig deeper — why the official series and the daily series are not two measurements of the same thing

It is worth being precise about what just happened, because a reader who watched AAA break $6 on 11 September could reasonably ask why the EIA print is news four days later. The two series are not redundant.

AAA’s daily number is a credit-card and station-level average, published every morning, and it is the fastest honest signal available. The EIA weekly is a survey of a fixed retail panel taken every Monday, published the following day, and it is what enters federal statistical use — it is the number that appears in Short-Term Energy Outlook revisions, in agency cost models, and in the record when someone argues about diesel in a rulemaking or a hearing. The AAA record told you the price. The EIA print is the price becoming an official fact.

The gap between them also tells you something. EIA has $6.285 for the week ending 14 September; AAA has $6.2301 for the same day and $6.2694 today. Those are consistent: EIA’s panel skews toward truck-stop and highway retail, AAA’s toward the broader station population, and a week-ending survey and a daily average will not match to the cent. What matters is that they agree on direction and on magnitude, which removes the last reason to treat the AAA run as a data artefact.

The structural point this brief has been making since 14 September stands and is unchanged by the print: the binding constraint is Atlantic-basin distillate refining capacity, not crude. Utilisation at 98% means the US cannot refine its way out of this in the near term, and the largest financed addition — Dangote’s expansion from 700,000 to 1.4m barrels a day, funded by a share sale that closes 13 October — is a 2029 proposition at best. There is no supply-side relief this winter. That is the honest summary and repeating it is not padding; it is the thing most likely to be wished away.

One caution on the y/y figures. 70.1% year on year is arithmetic against a September 2025 base of $3.6858, which was itself a low year for diesel. The number is correct and it is also flattered by the base. The more useful comparison for a household is the month: $5.4396 to $6.2694, or 15.3% in thirty days, which is the rate at which freight contracts actually reprice.

The ten-year reached 5.04%, the highest since July 2007, on day one of the Fed’s meeting

Macro & marketsIndependent corroboration

The ten-year Treasury yield rose to 5.041% today, the highest in nineteen years. It touched 5% on Monday and reversed; today it went through. The FOMC’s two-day meeting began this morning, with the statement and the dot plot due tomorrow at 2pm Eastern. CME FedWatch has a 25 basis point increase at better than 92% — which would be the first rise since 2023.

Two things land tomorrow morning, and the second is being overlooked. Census publishes August retail sales at 8:30am, five and a half hours before the decision. A committee that is about to raise rates into an energy shock gets its last read on whether the consumer is absorbing it or flinching, on the same morning, with no time to change the statement.

This brief has held since 10 September that the yield move is a global term-premium repricing rather than a US fiscal event, and today supplied more of the same control observation. Handelsblatt’s front page has bond yields named as investors’ principal worry, the DAX down more than a thousand points in twelve days and off 0.22% today at 25,384.85, the Euro Stoxx 50 down 0.53%, and the German government discussing measures against rising fuel prices that its own economists doubt will work. Same shock, different central bank, different currency, same direction in long yields.

What to watch tomorrow is not the 25 basis points, which is priced. It is the dot plot — whether the committee signals one more this year or a pause — and the dissent count. Three officials dissented for a hike in July; a dissent in the other direction tomorrow would be the more informative surprise.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Two live readings of the same yield: an American fiscal story — deficit near $1.9tn, net interest around $1.0tn, debt above 101% of GDP — or a global repricing of term premium on long-dated government debt everywhere. The dispute is about cause, and it has a practical consequence: under the first reading better US fiscal news helps, under the second it does not.

Why the accounts differ. Mostly vantage. US-focused commentary is reading a US instrument and reaches for US causes; European desks see Bunds at their highest since 2011, JGBs above 3% for the first time in three decades and French ten-year money costing more than Italian for the first time on record, and reach for a common shock. Both are describing real data. The timing also matters: the fiscal reading was formed before the energy shock had run a fortnight.

What the evidence supports. The global reading, and it has now survived several days of tests it could have failed. The specific observation that would break it is American underperformance — a tailed 30-year auction, or US yields rising on a day Bunds and JGBs fall. Neither has happened; the 10 September 30-year stopped through by 2.7 basis points with indirects at 79.5%. Watch for it anyway, because it is the cleanest single signal on this thread and the 4 November refunding is where it would show up.

Four banking agencies moved to scrap the 2023 outsourcing framework, eight days after doubling the exam threshold

US financialsNew subjectPrimary source

Federal Register document 2026-18859, published today: the OCC, the Federal Reserve, the FDIC and the National Credit Union Administration jointly propose guidance that would rescind and replace the 2023 Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships, along with the supplemental community-bank resources and the statements on bank–fintech arrangements. A companion, 2026-18852, is a simplified guide for traditional community banking organisations. Comments close 16 November.

The agencies’ own diagnosis is the interesting part, because it is an admission. They say the 2023 guidance, although written in principles-based language, came to be applied as a one-size-fits-all checklist. The replacement would tailor oversight to the magnitude and likelihood of actual harm, prioritise higher-risk relationships over process compliance, and strike language implying that newer providers are inherently riskier — which is a direct answer to banks that said the framework made fintech partnerships unattractive regardless of the partner.

Taken alone this is defensible and probably right; a checklist reading of principles-based guidance is a real and well-documented cost. Taken in sequence it is something else. On 14 September the interim final rule raising the extended-examination threshold from $3bn to $6bn took effect, moving 188 institutions from a twelve-month to an eighteen-month on-site cycle. Today the same agencies propose loosening the framework governing what those institutions outsource. Two reductions in supervisory intensity, on substantially the same band of banks, eight days apart.

What to watch: the comment file before 16 November, and specifically whether any of the four agencies files a separate statement or dissent; whether the final version restores anything from the bank–fintech statements rather than simply deleting them; and the still-unpublished aggregate assets of those 188 institutions.

Dig deeper — why the $3–6bn band is where this actually bites

Guidance is not a rule. Nothing here is enforceable on its own terms, and a bank cannot be cited for violating it. What guidance does is set what examiners look for, which in practice is most of what a mid-size bank experiences as regulation. So a rewrite of third-party guidance is a rewrite of the examination conversation, and it arrives for these institutions at the same moment that conversation is scheduled to happen half as often.

The band matters for a specific structural reason. Institutions between $3bn and $6bn in assets are large enough to run meaningful outsourced programmes — deposit-gathering arrangements, sponsored card and payments programmes, lending-as-a-service partnerships — and small enough that the internal audit and vendor-management function is a handful of people. That is precisely the population the 2023 guidance was drafted for after a run of bank–fintech failures where the bank could not say with confidence who held the customer relationship or the ledger of record. Rescinding the fintech-specific statements does not make those questions go away; it removes the document that told an examiner to ask them.

There is a third piece arriving in the same season, and it is the one to watch for interaction. The CLARITY Act’s unresolved stablecoin-yield fight is about deposit flight from community banks — banks argue a yield-bearing dollar token is a deposit substitute that pulls funding out of exactly this asset band. The current bill text directs the Treasury Secretary to restrict such rewards only if substantial deposit flight occurs, which is a trigger rather than a prohibition and requires the harm to be demonstrated first. Meanwhile OCC stablecoin rules remain targeted for November against a statutory deadline already missed on 18 July. So the same band of banks is being given a longer examination cycle, a lighter outsourcing framework, and a funding-model challenge whose safeguard only activates after the damage is measurable.

None of that is an accusation of coordination. Three different proceedings under three different statutes converging on one asset band is the ordinary way regulatory pressure ends up mis-aimed — nobody is looking at the sum. Recording the sum is the point of noticing it here.

What would change this assessment: a comment file showing bank trade groups themselves asking for the fintech statements to be retained, which would be the industry telling the agencies they were load-bearing. Note that a quiet comment file tells you less than usual on the examination-cycle rule, because that threshold was statutory — but it tells you a great deal here, because this one is entirely discretionary.

EPA finalised the power-plant carbon repeal, and announced it to G20 energy ministers in Houston

Energy & gridNew subjectIndependent corroboration

Administrator Lee Zeldin signed the final Partial Repeal of the Carbon Pollution Standards for Fossil Fuel-Fired Electric Generating Units on 14 September, announcing it at a gathering of G20 energy ministers in Houston. It removes the 2024 requirements that existing coal plants and new gas plants sharply cut carbon dioxide from the 2030s, largely by installing carbon capture and sequestration. EPA puts the avoided industry cost at more than $300bn. A separate, related step was announced alongside it, aimed at constraining a future administration’s ability to regulate greenhouse gases from power plants at all.

The US channel for this reader is the grid, not the climate. On 10 September FERC issued show-cause orders to all six jurisdictional market operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE and NYISO — requiring them to revise how they interconnect data centres and other large loads or defend the rules they have, docket RM26-4. Those orders left cost allocation open, and cost allocation is what reaches a household through a state rate case. Taking a compliance cost off existing coal and new gas changes what is economic to build to serve that load, and therefore what a state commission is eventually asked to allocate.

Be careful about what this is not. Repealing standards that bite in the 2030s does not change a 2026 electricity bill. What changes is expected future cost — which is exactly what capacity markets and interconnection queues price years ahead, so the effect is real and slow rather than absent.

What to watch: publication in the Federal Register, which is where the actual text and that “separate step” become readable rather than described; state attorneys general filing suit, which on a rule of this size is close to certain; and any ISO or RTO filing that cites the repeal in its response to the show-cause order. That last one is the point where this becomes a rates story.

Dig deeper — the two halves of the announcement, and which one is the durable move

The repeal itself is the expected half. A 2024 rule that required carbon capture on existing coal and new baseload gas was always going to be a target, it was already under litigation, and its compliance deadlines sat far enough out that reversing it costs nothing in the near term. Undoing it restores roughly the pre-2024 position. An incoming administration could redo it, at the price of a full rulemaking and several years.

The second announcement is the one worth watching, and it is also the one this brief cannot yet describe honestly. It was characterised as a step to prevent future administrations from regulating climate pollution from power plants. If that means what it sounds like — a finding going to EPA’s authority under section 111 rather than to the content of a standard — it is a structurally different and much more durable move than the repeal, because it would have to be undone by relitigating the authority question rather than by writing a new rule. That is the same pattern this brief has now catalogued several times this month: the consequential action is the one that changes what an agency may do, not what it has done.

Until the text publishes, that is inference from press characterisations and is labelled as such. The rule did not appear in today’s Register; a signing is not a publication, and the effective date and the severability language — which on the section 338 proclamations turned out to carry most of the litigation strategy — are not yet readable.

On the $300bn. That figure is EPA’s estimate of compliance cost avoided by industry, which is a real and calculable quantity. It is not a net benefit and is not presented as one; the same rulemakings carry monetised estimates of forgone health and climate benefits that the repeal also removes, and those are not in the number. Environmental groups’ statements today object to the policy rather than to the arithmetic. Treat $300bn as an accurate answer to a narrow question.

The grid connection is not decoration. The bipartisan House Ratepayer Protection Act, H.R. 9340, would direct state commissions to consider requiring nonresidential loads of 100MW or more at a single site to pay the full incremental cost of the generation, transmission and distribution built to serve them. It cleared House Energy and Commerce 52–0 on 21 July and has had no floor vote. One of its two lead sponsors is Kathy Castor, whose district is Tampa. If large-load cost allocation is going to be settled anywhere, it is in that bill or in the six operators’ RM26-4 responses — and both are now being decided against a generation mix whose expected compliance cost just fell.

The objection window closed, and the register still shows no hearing on the school vaccine rule

FloridaPrimary source

The state’s own rule history for 64D-3.046, checked this morning at flrules.org, shows exactly one 2026 entry: the notice of proposed rule, 24 August, Volume 52/164, notice 31292411. The comment-and-hearing-request window closed yesterday, Monday 14 September. There is no notice of hearing and no notice of change.

At least two professional bodies filed timely hearing requests — the Florida Chapter of the American College of Physicians on 9 September, and the Florida Osteopathic Medical Association. Under Florida’s Administrative Procedure Act a timely request inside the statutory twenty-one days obliges the Department of Health to hold a hearing.

Yesterday this brief said the absence of a notice on the deadline was not evidence of refusal, because notices publish on a lag and a request filed on the closing day could not yet appear. That is one day weaker today and still correct. The point at which it stops being correct is adoption: if the department files for adoption having never noticed a hearing, that is the challengeable act — an invalid exercise of delegated legislative authority, heard at DOAH. A hearing does not buy a different rule. Agencies hold obliged hearings and adopt anyway. What it buys is a transcript, and a transcript is the raw material of the challenge.

What the rule does, unchanged: it drops Hib, hepatitis B, varicella and pneumococcal from school entry requirements, and broadens the religious exemption to cover “moral or ethical beliefs”. The measles, polio, DTaP and mumps mandates are statutory and survive — but the widened exemption applies to them too, so both framings you will see are correct.

Standing reminder: the 12 December Panama City hearing date that surfaces in search results traces to a November 2025 notice, not to a granted 2026 hearing. Do not use it.

Grid. FERC approved a new version of the reliability standard governing physical security of critical transmission stations — CIP-014-4 — in today’s Register.

Document 2026-18868: an order approving North American Electric Reliability Corporation Reliability Standard CIP-014-4. The Register listing carries no abstract, so what can honestly be said is narrow: the order exists and the standard is approved. CIP-014 is the family that requires transmission owners to identify substations whose loss could cause instability or cascading outages, have that identification verified by an unaffiliated third party, and then evaluate threats and implement physical security plans for them.

Why it belongs in a brief that tracks AI capex reaching household bills: the physical-security standard is one of the few places where the grid’s reliability rules name specific substations as critical. The same substations are the ones large co-located loads want to connect behind, which is the subject of the show-cause orders FERC issued to all six market operators on 10 September in docket RM26-4. A revision to which stations count and what protecting them requires is a cost input to exactly that question.

The FERC and DOE reliability technical conference docket, AD26-8-000, also remains live; a second supplemental notice published on 14 September with no conference date.

Below the line because approving a periodic revision of an existing reliability standard is ordinary regulatory housekeeping until someone shows it changed a cost or a connection decision, and the abstract does not say what changed from CIP-014-3.

Underreported. Treasury opened the 2026 New Markets Tax Credit round today — $5bn of allocation authority, competitively awarded, and one of the larger federal subsidies for development in low-income areas.

Document 2026-18883, from Treasury’s Community Development Financial Institutions Fund: a notice of allocation availability inviting applications for the calendar year 2026 round of the New Markets Tax Credit Program, with $5 billion in allocation authority and a competitive award procedure.

The NMTC works by granting community development entities the right to raise investment that earns a 39% federal credit over seven years, in exchange for deploying it into businesses and real estate in low-income census tracts. Five billion dollars of allocation authority translates into roughly $1.95bn of credits and, in practice, a larger volume of financed projects — grocery stores, health centres, manufacturing facilities and mixed-use development in places conventional lending underprices.

It is underreported for a dull reason: the annual notice is a procedural document and the awards are announced much later, so the money is visible only at the end. For a reader in Pasco and Pinellas the relevance is concrete — both counties contain qualifying tracts, and allocatees choose where to deploy.

Below the line because opening an application window is not a decision and no dollar reaches any project today. It would become a story if this round’s awards concentrate somewhere unexpected, or if the authority is cut in a future budget.

Confidence: Primary source. Sources: Federal Register notices, 15 September.
