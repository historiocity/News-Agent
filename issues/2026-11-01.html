Three oil companies made $36 billion in a quarter, and that is yesterday's missing answer

Energy & grid Macro & markets Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief established that the American diesel price is being set in the international product market rather than by any US constraint, and said plainly that why the world is paying this much was a question it had not answered. Three earnings calls this week answer it.

ExxonMobil earned $15.4 billion in the quarter, more than double a year ago. Shell earned $10.8 billion, nearly double. Chevron earned $9.9 billion, up 180 per cent. Together, just over $36 billion in three months.

The cause given is the one this brief guessed at and could not source: the war in Iran. Less oil is moving through the Middle East, so oil everywhere else is worth more. Brent is around $113 a barrel against roughly $70 before the war. Dan Pickering of Pickering Energy Partners puts the mechanism in one sentence: "The majors are able to sell their barrels that they produce in the U.S. or Latin America or Europe for much higher prices. So the Middle East's loss is generally the gain of oil and gas companies in the U.S. and Europe." Demand for crude and for refined products has barely moved.

What the companies are doing with the money is the part that matters for next year. Shell ran one of its largest buybacks ever; Exxon declared its highest quarterly dividend. Pickering says the majors are otherwise in a wait-and-see stance and are not spending it on new drilling: "This is a period where the high prices are driven more by geopolitical events than anything else… You put the money in the bank and watch to see what happens as we move into 2027."

That is the answer to the question a reader actually has, which is when this ends. High prices normally cure themselves by calling forth supply. Here the producers have decided the price is political rather than structural, and are returning the windfall to shareholders instead of drilling. On their own account, the supply response that would bring the price down is not being funded.

At the pump this morning: diesel $6.3039, down 0.62 cents; regular $4.1732, down 1.05. The spread is $2.1307, an eighth consecutive record.

What to watch: whether capital expenditure guidance for 2027 changes on any of these calls; whether Brent holds above $110; and whether the Defense Production Act refining determination of 28 October produces an award, since public money is the other way supply gets funded.

The WTI series and the AAA prices were read at source this morning. The inference about the supply response is this brief's own, built on Pickering's description of the companies' stance. Four in ten names on the government's list are not on California's voter roll

Courts US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

The Justice Department's case against California rests on a list of 20,930 people it says are "likely non-citizens" registered to vote, delivered to the state in early October. On Saturday two data scientists from UCLA's Voting Rights Project, in an analysis filed with the court by the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights, reported what is on it.

8,880 of the names — 42 per cent — are not listed anywhere in California's current voter files. Many of the rest were either confirmed as US citizens or may correspond to citizens. A list of registered voters, four in ten of whom are not registered voters, is not a list of anything.

This brief has reported the case for six days and the courts have now answered twice. Judge David Carter refused the district court request on Wednesday, finding no legal basis and an unreliable list. On Friday the Ninth Circuit rejected the complaint a second time and called it unconstitutional: "We decline the United States's invitation to contravene the Elections Clause by undermining the Constitution's plain text."

The Elections Clause is the whole of it. Administering elections is the states' job, and all fifty states already prohibit non-citizen voting in federal elections and run checks against it. The analysis also supplies a comparison: Nevada received a similar federal list and found that none of the names on it were non-citizen voters.

What remains in motion is not the lawsuit. Grand jury subpoenas to Los Angeles registrar staff, reported here yesterday, are returnable on 4 November, and the Department has told California officials that tabulating flagged ballots risks making them "aiders and abettors to a federal crime". Two courts have now said the civil theory is wrong; the criminal exposure the Department has described to county employees does not go away because a court of appeals disagreed with the lawsuit.

What to watch: whether the 4 November appearances proceed now that the Ninth Circuit has ruled; whether the Department seeks Supreme Court review before Tuesday; and whether the Nevada and Washington state lists are analysed the same way.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. The UCLA figures are from a filing reported by Al Jazeera and the Associated Press; this brief did not read the filing, the Ninth Circuit opinion or the underlying list, and the 8,880 and 42 per cent figures are the plaintiffs' expert analysis, not an agreed fact. The quotation from the appeals court is as reported. Sources: Al Jazeera / AP · 29 October · 31 October

Six point three billion dollars left Binance in twenty minutes

Cyber & security Macro & markets New subject Independent corroboration

At 4:05 p.m. eastern on Thursday, attackers reached the accounts of customers of Binance, the largest cryptocurrency exchange in the world. In about twenty minutes they took just over $6.3 billion. On Saturday the FBI confirmed that North Korea was responsible.

The unit is identified as TraderTraitor, a North Korean military hacking group with a long record of this, and the attribution rests on the sophistication and the speed. Ari Redbord of TRM Labs, which has helped trace the funds, calls it "the largest bank robbery in human history": "Imagine a world where someone could walk into a bank and walk out with $6.36 billion in stolen funds and then move those funds at the speed of the internet."

About $3 billion has been frozen by Binance working with other exchanges, which means roughly half is gone. The exchange says it will repay affected customers, without saying how. How the attackers got into customer trading accounts has not been explained, and that is the only question that matters for anyone with money on an exchange.

The freeze is the structurally interesting part and it cuts both ways. Half the stolen funds were stopped because a handful of large exchanges can collectively blacklist addresses — which is to say the system that is supposed to be permissionless has a chokepoint, and it worked. Anyone who holds crypto because no intermediary can freeze it has just watched intermediaries freeze $3 billion.

For North Korea the arithmetic is the point. A single twenty-minute operation produced several billion dollars for a state whose entire annual export earnings are a fraction of that, and the half that was not frozen is not recoverable by any process this brief is aware of.

What to watch: whether Binance explains the access vector, which it has not; whether the $3 billion frozen is actually returned, which is a legal question and not a technical one; and whether any regulator treats the chokepoint that worked here as an argument for formalising it.

Confidence: Independent corroboration, with an FBI attribution. Figures and quotations are NPR's; this brief did not read the FBI statement, any Binance disclosure, or TRM Labs' tracing. The $6.3bn and $6.36bn figures both appear in the reporting and are not reconciled here. The observations about chokepoints and North Korean export earnings are this brief's own. Sources: NPR

A year in federal prison costs $47,564, and a halfway house costs $43,499

Courts US debt & fiscal New subject Primary document Underreported

Once a year the Bureau of Prisons publishes a number almost nobody reads. The Cost of Incarceration Fee for fiscal 2025, in Monday's Federal Register: $47,564 a year, or $130.31 a day, to hold one person in a federal facility. It is computed by dividing the Bureau's facility obligations, excluding activation costs, by inmate-days, and multiplying by the days in the year.

The second figure in the notice is the one worth sitting with. A year in a Residential Reentry Center — a halfway house — costs $43,499, or $119.18 a day. That is $4,065 less than prison, a saving of about 8.5 per cent.

Reentry centres are routinely described as the cheap alternative to custody, and on the government's own accounting they are not. A halfway house has no perimeter, no armed staff, no secure housing units and no prison infirmary, and it still costs nine-tenths of what a prison costs. Either the federal prison system is unusually efficient at the custodial part, or reentry centres are expensive in ways the comparison hides — contracting margins, small facilities, low occupancy. The notice does not say, because it is a fee determination and not an analysis.

What the fee is for: 28 CFR part 505 allows a court to assess the cost of incarceration against a defendant who can pay. So this is the number a sentencing judge uses, and it is also the per-head price of a policy. At $47,564 a head, every thousand additional federal prisoners costs $47.6 million a year.

What to watch: how the figure moves next year, since it has been rising faster than inflation in recent years; whether the reentry centre gap narrows further; and whether anyone in Congress uses the per-head number in the appropriations cycle.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full — it is one page. Scheduled for Monday 2 November and not yet published, read early from GovInfo. The claim that the figure has risen faster than inflation is from memory of prior years' notices and was not verified against them this morning; the explanations offered for the small prison-to-halfway-house gap are this brief's own speculation and are labelled as such. The government is asking who still has an idle drug factory

Health Trade & supply chains New subject Primary document Underreported

The Office of Management and Budget has reopened a request for information that asks a narrow and revealing question. It wants to hear about "new and unused (idle)" domestic, US-based and near-shore production capability for finished dose forms, active pharmaceutical ingredients and key starting materials tied to the ASPR 86 List of essential medicines.

Unpack the three terms and the policy is visible. Key starting materials are the chemicals an API is built from, and they are the layer of the pharmaceutical supply chain that has moved furthest offshore — a US plant making a finished tablet from an imported API from an imported starting material is domestic in name only. The ASPR 86 List is the government's own catalogue of the medicines whose absence would constitute an emergency. And the word idle means the question is not "who would build" but "who already built and stopped".

That last word is the admission in the document. An agency canvassing for mothballed plant is looking for capacity that can be restarted in months rather than constructed over years, which is the behaviour of a government that thinks it may need these medicines sooner than a new factory could be financed, permitted and validated. It also implies the government does not already know what idle capacity exists, which for 86 essential medicines is itself the finding.

The original request ran for 30 days and closed on 26 October. OMB has reopened it to 27 November, saying interested parties need more time. A reopening usually means the response was thin.

What to watch: whether anything is published from the responses, which RFIs often are not; whether this feeds a Defense Production Act determination, as refining did on 28 October; and the ASPR 86 List itself, which this brief has not read.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full. Scheduled for Monday 2 November and not yet published; the original RFI of 25 September (91 FR 60983) and the ASPR 86 List were not read. The foreign-trade zone board has started saying no

Trade & supply chains New subject Primary document Underreported

A foreign-trade zone lets a manufacturer bring components into the United States without paying duty until the finished product leaves the zone — and sometimes at the finished product's lower rate. Getting production approved inside one normally runs through a fast notification track that clears quietly. On 28 October the FTZ Board decided three of them, and two were refused.

Stadler US Inc. was refused for passenger rolling stock at its subzone in Salt Lake City — a European manufacturer building rail cars in Utah, told after seven months that "further review of the activity is warranted" and that it must now file a full application for production authority under section 400.23. AuroraTe LLC was refused for galvanizing at a zone in Texas. Tesla was approved for battery storage products and components at Brookshire, Texas — but "on a limited basis".

Three decisions, two refusals and a conditional approval, is not how this process usually reads. The notification track exists because most of these cases are routine; refusing one does not deny the activity, it pushes the applicant from a months-long administrative notice into a full application with hearings and a formal record, which is a year or more of delay and a different burden of proof.

Why it matters beyond three companies: an FTZ is how a manufacturer makes US assembly viable when the components are tariffed. With section 232 duties on steel, aluminium and now the findings on wind and robotics, and 50 per cent section 338 duties on Canadian goods, the zone programme is the pressure valve — and the board appears to be tightening it in the same weeks the tariffs are being extended. Passenger rail cars are a pointed example, since there is very little domestic capacity.

What to watch: whether refusals keep outnumbering approvals in the coming weeks, which would make this a policy rather than three decisions; whether Stadler files the full application; and what "limited basis" means in Tesla's authorisation, which this brief did not read far enough to say.

Confidence: Primary documents, read in full — each is a short notice. Scheduled for Monday 2 November and not yet published. This brief has not established the FTZ Board's normal approval rate, so "not how this process usually reads" rests on the structure of the programme rather than on a measured baseline; that baseline is owed before the pattern claim is repeated. Sources: FR 2026-22300 · FR 2026-22299 · FR 2026-22301

The District of Columbia and the Commonwealth of Virginia have sued to block construction of the triumphal arch the President has proposed near the capital. This brief noted the adjacent dispute on 26 October, when a judge halted renovations at the Trump golf course in Washington and the President said the work would start next month regardless — and on Friday a judge ordered an inquiry into possible Justice Department misconduct in that case.

That answer is in the complaint.

Courts The President has lost a round in the Epstein-linked Wall Street Journal case.

A court has ruled against the President in his litigation against the Wall Street Journal over its reporting connected to Jeffrey Epstein. The decision is reported as a loss on a legal battle within the case rather than a final judgment.

Defamation suits by public officials against newspapers are mostly lost, and lost slowly, which is frequently the point of bringing them. What determines whether this one matters is which motion was decided — a dismissal on the merits ends it, an anti-SLAPP ruling can carry fee-shifting, and a discovery ruling can be the opposite of a defeat for a plaintiff who wants the process to continue.

Below the line because this brief cannot say which it was. The headline establishes the outcome and not the posture, and the posture is the story. On the owed list.

Health Nigeria is investigating two more suspected Ebola cases.

Health authorities in north-east Nigeria are investigating two suspected Ebola cases. Nigeria is the fourth country reached by the Democratic Republic of the Congo outbreak — a count this brief got wrong on 21 October, said was five, and corrected on 24 October.

Two suspected cases are not two confirmed cases, and the distinction is the whole of it at this stage: suspected-case investigations in countries on alert resolve negative far more often than positive. What would make this significant is confirmation plus any link that is not traceable to a known chain, which is the definition of community transmission and the thing Kenya was able to rule out when it reported its second death.

The outbreak context has not improved. The WHO's ring-vaccination trial began in Bunia on 24 October using a vaccine licensed for a different strain, and on 26 October a nurse answering an alert in Bafwasende was killed — access, not medicine, being the constraint.

Below the line because suspected cases are not news until they are confirmed or excluded, and because this brief has already published one error on this outbreak by being quick with a number.

Confidence: Reported, single source. Sources: Al Jazeera · 24 October

US politics The clocks went back, so the Senate did not act.

This brief has carried the line "1 November, the clocks go back unless the Senate acts" in its dates-ahead list since early October. The clocks went back at 2 a.m. this morning. The Senate did not act.

Permanent daylight saving time has passed the Senate by unanimous consent before, in 2022, and died in the House. The pattern since has been a bill that attracts attention each autumn and no floor time. The practical effect of this morning: sunset in Washington moves to before 5 p.m., and stays there until March.

Below the line because the absence of a vote is not an event, and because a dates-ahead entry that resolves by nothing happening still deserves the two sentences that close it rather than being quietly dropped from the list. It is removed from the dates ahead below.
