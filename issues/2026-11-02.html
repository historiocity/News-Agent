The Alaska suit is dead, and the secret ballot killed it

Courts US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

US District Judge Sharon Gleason, an Obama nominee, has thrown out the Justice Department's attempt to block certification of Alaska's Senate result if the Republican incumbent loses. The ruling, released Sunday, holds that the federal government lacks the ability to bring the suit at all — it rested on the preamble of the National Voter Registration Act — and that the order it sought would likely violate Alaskan voters' right to a secret ballot.

The sentence that does the work: "The absence of any explanation from the United States as to how its requested relief could redress its alleged injury while simultaneously maintaining the secrecy of the ballot is telling."

That is the objection this brief raised on 26 October, in the lead that first covered the suit. The words then were: "Ballots are secret. There is no lawful way to ask a voter who marked 'Daniel J. Sullivan' which Sullivan they meant, and the suit does not say how the investigation it demands would be conducted." It is set out here not as a boast but because the brief also said it could not rule out the Department's own reading, and a court has now done that for it — which is the ordinary and correct division of labour.

One detail this brief has had slightly wrong and corrects in passing: the second candidate is Daniel Sullivan Jr., running unaffiliated, one of four Senate candidates on Alaska's ranked-choice ballot. Alaska's Republican Party backed an earlier state-court effort to keep him off it; the Alaska Supreme Court ruled in June that he has the right to run.

It is not over as a matter of posture. The Department is appealing; spokesperson Kiersten Pels said "Alaskan voters deserve a fair election, not to be misled in an attempt to rig outcomes." Sophia Lin Lakin of the ACLU's Voting Rights Project, which helped defend the state: "DOJ's attempt to throw out Alaska's election results before a single ballot was counted was lawless from the start… a reckless waste of taxpayer money aimed at silencing hundreds of thousands of Alaska voters."

What to watch: whether the Ninth Circuit takes the Alaska appeal on any timetable that matters, having already rejected the California version twice; whether the Los Angeles grand jury subpoenas returnable Wednesday proceed; and what the Department does on Tuesday night.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. Gleason's quotation and the holding are as NPR reported them, with the ruling linked in that report; this brief did not read the ruling. The 26 October passage is quoted from this brief's own archive. Sources: NPR · 26 October · 1 November

Correction: the cost of a federal prisoner rose 0.85%, and the halfway house got cheaper

Courts US debt & fiscal Correction Primary document

Yesterday this brief reported the FY2025 cost of incarceration — $47,564 a year per federal inmate, $43,499 in a residential reentry centre — and added, in the "what to watch" line, that the figure "has been rising faster than inflation in recent years". The confidence line said that came from memory rather than from the prior notices. The prior notice was read this morning.

From the FY2024 determination, published 15 December 2025: $47,162 a year ($129.21 a day) for a federal facility, and $43,703 ($119.73 a day) for a reentry centre.

So year on year: the prison figure rose $402, or 0.85 per cent. The reentry centre figure fell $204, or 0.47 per cent. Neither is faster than inflation on any measure. The claim was wrong, and it was wrong in the direction that makes a story more interesting, which is the direction a brief should be most suspicious of its own memory.

Two things survive the correction and one is strengthened. The gap between prison and halfway house widened, from $3,459 (7.3 per cent) to $4,065 (8.5 per cent) — because the reentry figure fell while the prison figure rose. So yesterday's substantive point, that reentry centres cost nearly as much as prisons on the government's own accounting, holds and is becoming more true, not less.

And the flatness is itself worth something. A federal prison system holding its per-inmate cost to 0.85 per cent growth in a year is either unusually disciplined or is absorbing the difference somewhere the fee calculation does not show — the formula excludes activation costs and divides by inmate-days, so a falling population raises the per-head number and a rising one lowers it. This brief does not have the population series and will not guess at it twice in two days.

What to watch: the federal inmate population across FY2024 and FY2025, which is the denominator and would say whether the flat cost is efficiency or arithmetic; and next year's determination, which on the current cadence arrives around December.

Confidence: Primary documents, both read in full. FR 2026-22340 (FY2025) and FR 2025-22777 (FY2024), the latter located this morning by searching the Federal Register for the notice series. The inmate population series was not pulled and is named as the missing denominator rather than guessed at. Sources: FR 2026-22340 · FR 2025-22777 · 1 November (contains the corrected claim)

Four of this year's six trade-zone refusals landed in the last four days

Trade & supply chains Continuation Primary source Underreported

Yesterday this brief reported that the Foreign-Trade Zone Board had refused two of three production notifications on 28 October, called it "not how this process usually reads", and said in its own confidence line that it had not measured the board's approval rate and that the baseline was owed before the claim was repeated. The baseline was measured this morning, from the Federal Register's own index.

Across 2026 the board has published 27 authorizations of production activity and 6 refusals, against 126 notifications of proposed activity. So among decided notifications the refusal rate for the year is about 18 per cent — higher than this brief assumed, and on its own a reason to soften the claim.

The dates are the finding. Of those six refusals, four were published in the last four days: Aprinnova and Valero Energy Corporation on 30 October, and AuroraTe and Stadler US today. The other two were both in June and both concern the same applicant, Catalina Components at FTZ 75, published three days apart — which is arguably one matter, not two. So the honest statement is that the board refused one or two things in the first ten months of 2026 and four things last week.

One of the four matters more than the others for a thread this brief has run all autumn. Valero Energy is a refiner, and its refused notification was filed on behalf of a partnership at FTZ 116. A zone lets a refiner bring in crude or blendstock without paying duty until product leaves. Refusing that in the same fortnight the President declared refining capacity a defence shortfall under the Defense Production Act is at least a tension, and this brief does not know what the notification asked for, because the document was identified by title and not read.

What to watch: whether refusals keep coming this week, which is what would turn four days into a policy; what the Valero notification actually proposed; and whether Stadler files the full application for its passenger rail cars.

Confidence: Primary, counted programmatically. The figures come from querying the Federal Register API for every 2026 notice matching "Production Activity" — 216 documents, classified by title. That is a title-based count and may miss decisions worded differently. The Valero and Aprinnova refusals were identified by title and not read, and the claim about what a zone does for a refiner is general knowledge, not from the document. Sources: FR 2026-22300 · FR 2026-22236 (Valero) · 1 November · 28 October

Senator Chris Murphy has had a stroke

US politics Health New subject Independent corroboration

Senator Chris Murphy of Connecticut, 53, was taken to hospital on Saturday afternoon after experiencing numbness in his arm while at his son's hockey game. A stroke was diagnosed at a local hospital; he was transferred to Johns Hopkins for monitoring. His chief of staff, Allison Herwitt, says he is responding well to treatment and is expected to make a full recovery.

The detail in that account is worth pausing on, because it is the public-health point. Arm numbness is one of the recognised stroke signs, and Murphy's outcome so far reflects what happens when someone acts on it within the window in which treatment works. The signs are usually taught as BE FAST — balance, eyes, face, arms, speech, time.

The political context is immediate and the brief will not overstate it. Murphy has been one of his party's most active campaign surrogates this cycle, is treated as a possible 2028 presidential candidate, and has built a profile on foreign policy and on oversight of executive power. He returned recently from a multi-stop Middle East tour and warned that the President would dramatically escalate the war on Iran after the midterms — a prediction this brief has had on its calendar since September from other sources, and which Tuesday's result now bears on.

He is also the latest in a run of congressional health crises. Senator Mitch McConnell missed months of Senate work this year during a long hospital stay and rehabilitation; Representative Tom Kean of New Jersey was absent for months and later said he had been treated for depression. In 2022, Senators Ben Ray Luján and Chris Van Hollen and the then-candidate John Fetterman were all hospitalised for strokes.

That list is the structural story and it is about arithmetic. A Senate decided by one or two seats is also decided by who can be present, and an institution whose members are on average among the oldest in the democratic world will keep producing these absences. Nothing in the chamber's rules allows a vote to be cast by a member who is not there.

What to watch: Murphy's prognosis and when he returns; whether any Senate business turns on his presence in the weeks after the election; and the age profile of whoever is sworn in in January.

Confidence: Independent corroboration of an office statement. Everything about Murphy's condition comes from his chief of staff's statement as reported by NPR; no physician, hospital or independent account appears in what was read, and "expected to make a full recovery" is the office's characterisation. The stroke-sign explanation is general knowledge. Sources: NPR · NPR on stroke signs

The diesel premium has stopped setting records

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

For eight consecutive mornings this brief has reported the gap between diesel and regular petrol at a new high. This morning it narrowed. Diesel is $6.2921, down 1.18 cents; regular is $4.1694, down 0.38. The spread is $2.1227, against yesterday's $2.1307.

One day is one day, and this brief has spent the past fortnight learning that lesson expensively on this exact thread. What can be said is narrow: diesel has now fallen on five of the last six days, and it fell faster than petrol this morning, which is the first time that has happened since 23 October.

It is consistent with the finding established on Saturday and completed yesterday — that the price is set by an international refining margin rather than a domestic constraint — because a margin that extreme is the kind of thing that eventually attracts enough product to close. It is equally consistent with nothing at all. The crack spread reading that would settle it is weekly and the next one arrives on Wednesday, which reaches this brief on Thursday.

What to watch: Wednesday's EIA release, for the crack spread and the export figure; whether the spread falls below $2.10, which would be the first real break; and the oil majors' capital spending guidance, which is what decides whether the margin gets competed away in 2027 or persists.

Confidence: Primary. AAA read at source this morning; the fifteen-point series is this brief's own record of its own daily readings, which is a published audit trail rather than a vendor file. No claim is made here about why the spread narrowed, because one day of data cannot support one. Sources: AAA · 31 October · 1 November

Geopolitics The UN's Western Sahara peacekeeping mission has ended after 35 years.

MINURSO — the United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara — has ended after 35 years. It was established in 1991 to monitor a ceasefire between Morocco and the Polisario Front and to organise a referendum on the territory's status. The ceasefire it monitored broke down in 2020. The referendum it existed to run was never held.

A mission named for a referendum, wound up without one, is the plainest possible statement of an outcome. Western Sahara is Africa's last entry on the UN's list of non-self-governing territories, and the practical effect of the mission's closure is that the dispute stops being internationally supervised at all.

Below the line because this brief read a headline and not the Security Council resolution, and the terms on which a 35-year mission is terminated — what replaces it, what the vote was, who abstained — are the entire substance. It is today's negative check and is the largest story here left unopened.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: NPR

US politics A Democratic congresswoman has endorsed the Republican in Michigan's Senate race.

Representative Haley Stevens, a Democrat, has endorsed the Republican candidate in Michigan's Senate race, declining to support her own party's nominee Abdul El-Sayed. Stevens lost the Democratic primary to El-Sayed; the seat is one of those that decides control of the chamber.

A sitting member of Congress endorsing across the aisle in a race that may determine the Senate majority is rare and it is not symbolic. In a chamber decided by one or two seats, an endorsement that moves even a small number of Democratic voters in Michigan is a vote on control, not a statement about a candidate. It also tells you something about how the primary went that no amount of post-election analysis will.

Below the line because this brief read the headline and not the endorsement — the reasons Stevens gave are the story, and whether they are about El-Sayed's positions, the primary, or something local determines whether this is a feud or a realignment.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: NPR

Regulatory An FCC rule became effective today and applicable three weeks ago.

The FCC's Wireline Competition Bureau has published a correction to its own notice of 1 October, withdrawing "all amendatory instructions inadvertently included" in it. The rules concerned are from a March Report and Order reducing the regulatory barriers to retiring legacy copper networks in favour of internet-protocol infrastructure — the network change disclosure and discontinuance rules.

The dates are the oddity. One amendatory instruction, at §63.62, "is effective on November 2, 2026, and applicable as of October 15, 2026." A rule that becomes effective today and applies from eighteen days ago is a small piece of retroactivity, created by an administrative error in an effective-date notice and fixed by another notice three weeks later.

Nothing turns on it for most readers. It matters to a carrier that filed — or did not file — a network change disclosure between 15 October and today, for whom the governing rule has just been established after the fact.

Below the line because it is a correction to a notice of effective dates, which is about as far down the regulatory stack as this brief goes. It is here as an example of how a deregulatory rule actually arrives: not in the order, but in the third notice about the order.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full. Sources: FR 2026-22334

Method What this brief will and will not be able to tell you on Wednesday morning.

Polls close across the country on Tuesday between 7 p.m. and 1 a.m. eastern. This brief is written and published between about 4 and 6 a.m. So Wednesday's issue will be produced a few hours after the last polls close and before most races are called.

What that means in practice: House and Senate control may not be known, close races will not be resolved, and anything this brief says about a margin will be a count in progress. The useful thing available at 4 a.m. is not a result but a state of play — which races are uncalled, what the outstanding ballot pools look like, and whether any of the certification litigation has moved overnight, which is the thread this brief has actually been following for a week.

The commitment, stated now rather than improvised then: no projection this brief cannot source to a named organisation, no seat count presented as settled while counting continues, and the certification thread treated as the story it has been — three courts in eight days have now rejected the same theory, and the question Wednesday is whether that holds under a result.

Below the line because it is housekeeping, and because a reader is owed the limits of a 4 a.m. brief before the morning it matters rather than afterwards.
