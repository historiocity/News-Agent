A federal judge called the $100,000 H-1B charge an unlawful tax in June. It is back on Monday, for another year

US politics & policy AI & tech industry New subject Primary source

The President signed two H-1B instruments on Friday. The first, a proclamation titled Restriction on Entry of Certain Nonimmigrant Workers, suspends the entry of H-1B specialty-occupation workers from outside the United States “except for those aliens whose petitions are accompanied or supplemented by a payment of $100,000”. It takes effect 21 September 2026 — tomorrow — and expires 21 September 2027. The authority claimed is INA §§212(f) and 215(a). The Homeland Security Secretary may waive it for an individual, a company or a whole industry in the national interest.

The second is an executive order requiring State, Labor and Homeland Security to weigh “whether the employer sponsor directly or indirectly engaged in layoffs within the previous year or plans future layoffs” of similarly situated American workers when adjudicating a petition, and to draw wage and industry data from Commerce, Education and the Small Business Administration. It gives Labor's Wage and Hour Division 30 days to start reviewing labour condition applications already filed for enforcement action.

The charge is not new. An identical one was imposed on 19 September 2025. On 8 June 2026 Judge Leo Sorokin of the District of Massachusetts granted summary judgment against it in California v. Trump, holding it was not an entry restriction but a tax, which the President cannot levy. The First Circuit declined to revive it on 24 July, and USCIS stopped collecting.

What to watch: whether the states move this week, and whether the new text differs from the old one at all.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether Friday's proclamation is a renewal or a re-issuance. The White House fact sheet describes it as renewing a measure “first imposed on September 19, 2025” — policy continuity, a twelve-month instrument reaching its expiry and being extended. The immigration bar's reading, filed within hours, is that it “does not appear to directly respond to the earlier court ruling; rather, it renews the challenged fee requirement” — i.e. the same instrument re-signed into the teeth of a vacatur. This is a dispute about significance, not about facts. Both descriptions are of the same document.

Cause. The mundane explanation covers it: the two speakers are answering different questions. The White House is describing what the policy does, where a twelve-month term ending and being extended really is a renewal. Counsel are describing where the policy stands in court, where the operative fact is that a district judge vacated it and an appeals court refused to put it back. Neither is being misleading; they are not addressing each other.

What it means for you. The question that decides it is narrow and answerable: is the operative section drafted differently? Sorokin's holding was about the instrument itself — that a charge of this size, framed as the price of admission, is a revenue measure the President has no power to impose. A redraft could in principle respond to that; simply re-signing cannot. The text read for this story uses the same construction as the 2025 original: entry suspended, except on payment. On that reading the administration is not curing the defect, it is betting on the First Circuit appeal and buying a year of collection while it runs. The observation that settles it is the plaintiff states. If California and its co-plaintiffs are back before Sorokin within days rather than weeks, they have read the two documents side by side and concluded they are the same instrument. If they take their time, there is something new in it. Honest limit: this brief read the 2026 proclamation and the fact sheet directly but has not read Sorokin's opinion, and has not compared the two proclamations clause by clause.

Dig deeper — the layer that gets deference, and the layer that gets reviewed

Read what Sorokin actually vacated. USCIS's own notice says the June order struck down “the agency guidance implementing this payment requirement”. That is the pattern this file has now recorded four separate times in three weeks, and Friday is the fifth. A presidential instrument — a proclamation, an executive order, a §212(f) suspension — is reviewed with enormous deference and is hard to enjoin. The agency paperwork that makes it operate is an ordinary administrative act, reviewed under the Administrative Procedure Act, and is where challengers win. So the presidential layer survives and can be re-signed indefinitely; the implementing layer is knocked down and has to be rebuilt each time.

The same architecture is visible everywhere this brief has been looking. The Congressional Research Service flagged it in the Graham Act: delegating rate modifications to the Trade Representative rather than to the President may expose them to APA review, so the headline tariff is insulated and the fine-tuning is not. It is why OPM's excepted-service rule is being built through notice-and-comment rather than bolted on as guidance — a single District of Massachusetts order reached the loyalty-essay requirement government-wide precisely because it was guidance. And it is why the §338 Canada proclamations carry a severability clause: if a court strikes a ban, the goods fall back to a 50% duty rather than to duty-free, so winning buys an importer very little.

Two things to hold about the substance rather than the procedure. First, the government's own figures are strong evidence the 2025 charge worked as intended: H-1B registrations from large IT outsourcing firms down 92%, consular processing requests down nearly 97%, and FY2027 registrations down almost 40% after wage-based selection. Whether that is a policy success depends on what you think the programme is for, but nobody should argue the measure was cosmetic. Second, it does not touch renewals or people already here — USCIS has been explicit that a current H-1B holder can leave and re-enter. The burden falls on hiring from abroad, which is where the smaller and newer employers are, because the large ones have already moved the work offshore rather than paying.

A disclosure this brief owes on a story like this: it is written with a model built by an American AI company, and AI labs are among the heaviest users of exactly this visa category. The coverage above should be read with that in mind, and judged against the same standard as coverage of the reader's own employer.

Crude on the screen is under $100 and a physical cargo costs $20–30 more. Diesel took an eleventh record; petrol stopped dead

Energy & grid Macro & markets Underreported Independent corroboration

AAA's national diesel average this morning is $6.5050 — an eleventh consecutive all-time record, up 1.84 cents on Saturday, 17.3% in thirty days and 75.8% on the year. Petrol is $4.4761 against $4.4759 yesterday. That is two hundredths of a cent. The petrol leg has stalled and the distillate leg has not, and the gap between them is now $2.03 a gallon.

The tape says this should not be happening. Brent on the screen read $98.54 on Friday, below $100 for the first time in weeks. But the screen is the front-month futures contract, and it has come loose from the cost of an actual barrel. Dated Brent — the physical North Sea assessment, priced on cargoes that exist — was around $122 on 15 September, roughly $14 over futures, and physical cargoes have since been reported trading more than $20 above. One trade account this weekend put dated crude above $131.

The thing in between is freight. The Trafigura Group's chief economist: “It has never been this expensive to move oil around.” Supertankers are unobtainable in some load ports. Shipping Houston crude to Asia is reported to add about $26 a barrel — $52m on a single cargo. So refiners are buying whatever is nearest: Japanese processors have taken Alaskan crude that does not suit their plants because it is close.

What to watch: the EIA weekly print on Tuesday 22 September, and a distillate inventory build. Neither has come.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a disagreement between newsrooms — a disagreement between two numbers both called “the price of Brent”. Friday's screen quote was $98.54 and falling; physical cargoes were assessed $20–30 higher and rising. A reader looking at a ticker and a reader looking at a cargo assessment this week would form opposite views of the direction of the oil market.

Cause. They measure different objects, and this is the ordinary explanation rather than a motivated one. The front-month futures contract settles on delivery into a specific place at a specific time, and it is the instrument that financial participants trade. Dated Brent assesses cargoes of actual North Sea crude with actual loading dates. In normal conditions the two sit within a dollar or two, because anyone can arbitrage between them by chartering a ship. That arbitrage is what has broken. When there is no vessel to hire at any price, a barrel afloat near the buyer and a barrel in a futures contract are not substitutes, and the spread between them is the freight market's opinion of itself. Note also that the freight figures themselves diverge wildly in the trade press — $200,000, $800,000 and $1.2m a day all appear for “the VLCC rate” this month, because they are different routes, different assessment dates and different rate conventions. Do not treat any single one as the number.

What it means for you. Two practical things. One: a headline that crude has fallen below $100 is not evidence of relief at the pump, and this brief has now been right about that four days running for a reason that is finally legible — the barrel did not get cheaper, the contract did. Two: the honest way to read this market from here is to stop watching the screen and watch three physical quantities instead: US distillate inventories, refinery utilisation (still in the high 90s, so there is no slack), and the freight rate on a named route. A distillate build or utilisation breaking below the high 90s would be real relief. A falling futures price on its own would not be. This brief's standing rule — a ticker may be used for direction only, never for a level — was written for a different reason in mid-September and turns out to have been protecting against this.

Dig deeper — why Saudi crude is being pushed back into the strait Iran is licensing

The freight squeeze is not weather. It is the product of a specific sequence this file has been tracking for ten days, and the last link closed this week. On 10 September drone strikes launched from Iraq hit pump stations 8 and 9 on Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline — 1,200km from the eastern oilfields to Yanbu on the Red Sea, and the single largest physical bypass of the Strait of Hormuz. Riyadh shut it on the 11th. Aramco is now bypassing the damaged section and seeking roughly half capacity within days, full capability in about six weeks.

In the meantime the barrels have to go somewhere, and they are going through Hormuz. Aramco is reported to be redirecting roughly 60 million barrels through the strait during September and October, with some cargoes transferred ship-to-ship off Sohar in Oman. That is the mechanism: the pipeline outage converts a short, cheap, safe pipeline haul into a long, expensive, dangerous sea haul, at exactly the moment vessels became unobtainable. It also puts a great deal more Saudi crude into a waterway where Iran began enforcing a permission regime on 16 September, striking the Togo-flagged tanker Trend for what the IRGC Navy called an illegal passage.

The attack pattern is now measurable and it is aimed at the machinery. Two further tankers were hit by unattributed projectiles on 16–17 September; crews were safe. Trade reporting of the wider pattern since 6 July puts 22 of 35 projectile strikes on the southern Omani route, and the great majority of the incidents have struck engine rooms. That is not an attempt to sink ships or to burn cargo. Disabling a vessel in a channel is cheaper, more deniable, and far more effective at persuading an owner not to sail — which is exactly what the transit counts have been showing, and it is why escorts help less than they appear to. US-facilitated movements on the southern route have averaged about 24 vessels a day over the past week, well below normal.

What this does not yet tell you. There is still no published tariff, no published coordinates for Iran's restricted zone, and no war-risk premium print for a named Gulf passage — the outstanding item on this thread for six consecutive runs. And the structural answer to record diesel, Atlantic-basin refining capacity, is financed and three years away. Nothing in this week's news shortens that.

The Graham Act is law. It gives the President a 100% tariff on Russian-oil buyers four days before Xi lands

US politics & policy Geopolitics Independent corroboration

The President signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 on Friday 18 September. It passed the Senate 86–11 in August and the House 262–159 on 16 September. It also extends the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996 through 2031.

This brief has argued since Thursday that it should be read as a tariff statute rather than a sanctions one, and the signature does not change that. §112 requires the President to act within 30 days to impose duties of up to 500% on all Russian goods — with no floor, so the rate is his to pick anywhere in that range. §113 is the live end: secondary tariffs of up to 100% on other countries, within 30 days, triggered by being a top-five importer of Russian crude or gas and making new purchases after enactment, or by being a top-five facilitator of evasion. China and India are on that list.

Enactment starts a 30-day clock that expires around 18 October. Xi Jinping arrives at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday and is at the White House on Thursday.

The tell is §113(g): a written justification must go to six congressional committees at least ten days before any secondary duty is imposed or adjusted. That notice is the first hard evidence of whether the authority is being used or held.

What to watch: the §112 rate; the first §113(g) notice, or its conspicuous absence through Thursday; and whether Beijing raises it in the room.

Dig deeper — the same week London widened its war-risk map and Ukraine sent 1,600 drones at Moscow

Three things happened to the Russia file in four days and they belong together. On Friday the President signed a statute arming him with secondary tariffs against Russia's oil customers. Also on Friday, London's Joint War Committee widened its high-risk listing to cover the whole of the Black Sea, where previously only Russian and Ukrainian coastal waters were listed. And overnight into today, Ukraine launched what Moscow's mayor called the largest drone attack ever on the capital — Sergei Sobyanin says more than 1,600 drones were intercepted, 450 of them heading for Moscow — hitting the Moscow Oil Refinery and killing at least two people in the region, on the third and final day of Russia's parliamentary elections. Zelenskyy confirmed ordering it.

The JWC item is the one to hold on to, and for an odd reason. This brief has spent six consecutive runs failing to obtain a dated Joint War Committee notice for the Red Sea or the Gulf — the single most valuable outstanding item on two threads, because a broker's quoted rate for a named passage is the only honest measure of whether a chokepoint is actually closed. The committee has now moved, publicly and on a date, and it moved on the Black Sea. Premiums there are reported to be adding hundreds of thousands of dollars to a seven-day voyage. Two readings, and this brief cannot choose between them on today's evidence: either the Gulf listings are already so wide that there is nothing left to add, or the Black Sea genuinely deteriorated faster. Either way the instrument works and can be reached, which is the useful finding.

The refinery strike is the link to the story above. A damaged Russian refinery is Russian distillate off the market, in a world already short of distillate, at a moment when tankers to move any replacement are unobtainable. It is also the second refinery outage in a fortnight, after Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline. That is the wrong direction for a US diesel price at $6.51.

One open question on the record, and it is this brief's own. The file carries this statute as S.5025, taken from the Congressional Research Service's section-by-section analysis. The wire accounts of Friday's signature give the number as H.R. 5334. A House number is the more likely vehicle, since the Constitution's Origination Clause requires revenue measures to start in the House and this is a tariff bill — probably a Senate companion and a House vehicle with the same text. It is not resolved here: congress.gov has returned 403 for seven consecutive days, so the enrolled bill cannot be checked. Flagged rather than quietly fixed.

DHS circulated a 78-page list of places that print ballots in other languages, attached to a prosecution checklist

US politics & policy New subject Single-sourced

An internal Department of Homeland Security email, obtained by NPR, shows DHS personnel circulating a 78-page document listing the counties and states that provide election materials and ballots in languages other than English. Attached alongside it was a Department of Justice criminal checklist setting out the requirements for prosecuting voter-related charges.

The email was written last year. It surfaced now because the Democratic National Committee sued over a public-records request and a tranche of material was released; NPR obtained it from there.

Two things should be said plainly, in both directions. The email is heavily redacted and nothing in it refers to enforcement activity at any of the listed places. There is no document here showing an operation, a target or a plan. Equally, the two attachments are what they are: a map of jurisdictions defined by the language their voters read, paired with instructions for charging voters with crimes. Those are the materials someone put in the same message.

DHS's response, when asked, was that a person who votes illegally should not be able to “use a purported lack of knowledge” of English to escape prosecution. That is a coherent answer to why a prosecutor might care about language. It is not an answer to why the list is organised by jurisdiction.

What to watch: the rest of the DNC document production; whether any listed jurisdiction reports contact; and the midterms, seven weeks out.

Dig deeper — what the list actually is, and why it is a poor proxy for anything

The underlying list is not a DHS invention. Jurisdictions provide ballots in languages other than English because §203 of the Voting Rights Act requires it wherever a single language-minority group passes statutory thresholds of citizen voting-age population and literacy. The Census Bureau publishes the determinations. So the 78 pages are, in substance, a published federal list of places with large numbers of citizens whose strongest language is not English — because that is the eligibility test the statute uses.

That is what makes it a bad instrument for the stated purpose and a revealing one for any other. If you wanted to find non-citizens on voter rolls, §203 coverage is close to useless: it is calibrated on citizens by construction. If you wanted a geography of naturalised and language-minority voters, it is the best-maintained list the federal government publishes. Nothing in the email says which use was intended, and this brief is not going to assert one. But a reader should understand that the two possible uses are not equally well served by the same document, and it is the second one the document is actually good at.

Sourcing, and it is thin. One newsroom has this. NPR's own article pages have timed out on every direct attempt for five consecutive days, so this brief reached the reporting through member stations and search — and a story syndicated across dozens of member sites is one source, not dozens. The email itself has not been read here, only reported. Under this brief's standing instruction single-sourced stories run rather than being held, with the label attached; this is one of them.

Why it runs as a lead rather than a short item. Because the subject is the administration of an election seven weeks away, and because the material is a document obtained through litigation rather than a characterisation by an interested party. It was also carried forward in this file from 18 September, when NPR's outage cost it a slot. It is being run late rather than dropped.

Medicare is letting AI deny care in six states, and paying the vendors per denial

Science & public health AI & tech industry New subject Underreported Primary source

Documents obtained by the Electronic Frontier Foundation through a Freedom of Information Act suit, and reported on 15 September, describe the launch of WISeR — Wasteful and Inappropriate Service Reduction — a Medicare pilot that uses AI to approve or refuse prior authorisation for certain procedures. It began in January, runs to 2031, and operates in New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Texas, Arizona and Washington. Covered items include skin substitutes and epidural injections for pain.

Three findings, in rising order of consequence. The launch was rushed: about a month before going live, the vendor Innovaccer warned the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services that it intended to ship software lacking full functionality and not fully tested, citing insufficient time for end-to-end testing. The denials are substantial: two vendors together refused 5,944 prior-authorisation requests in the first three months.

And the structural one: vendors are paid for the requests they deny — though not for denials later overturned on appeal. That is a contingent-fee arrangement pointing in one direction, with a partial correction that only operates on the minority of decisions anyone appeals.

The documents do not show what the systems were trained on, how they were tested for bias or accuracy, or how often refusals were reversed.

What to watch: a published overturn rate. Without it, nobody can tell a working programme from a broken one.

Dig deeper — why the missing overturn rate is the whole argument

The appeal-reversal rate is the only number that distinguishes the two stories you could tell about WISeR. If a small share of refusals are overturned, the system is identifying genuinely inappropriate claims and the fee structure is doing what a performance contract should. If a large share are overturned, the system is generating refusals that do not survive contact with a human reviewer, and the payment arrangement is rewarding exactly that, because the clawback only bites on the cases patients and providers bother to contest. In commercial insurance the contested share is historically small — most denials are never appealed — which means an overturn rate computed on appeals badly understates the error rate on the whole population. That asymmetry is the reason the number is worth demanding rather than merely noting.

Two things to keep proportionate. Prior authorisation is not new and is not confined to AI — traditional Medicare has used it selectively for years, and Medicare Advantage plans use it heavily. And the covered categories here, skin substitutes especially, have a documented history of aggressive billing, which is a real reason to check claims. The novelty is not that Medicare is checking. It is that the checking is automated, the automation was shipped untested by the vendor's own warning, the vendor's revenue rises with refusals, and the training data and accuracy testing are not disclosed to anyone including the people refused.

Why this belongs in a brief about AI policy rather than a health brief. Every AI-governance fight this file tracks — California's auditor statutes, Colorado's algorithmic-decision rules, the FDA's refusal on 17 September to exempt AI imaging software from premarket review, the state chatbot laws with industry-drafted exemptions — is an argument about whether an automated decision must be independently verifiable. WISeR is the federal government operating an automated decision system that affects whether individual people get medical procedures, and the verification documents had to be prised out by a FOIA lawsuit. That is the strongest available answer to anyone arguing the verification question is hypothetical.

Sourcing note: the documents were obtained and published by the EFF, an advocacy organisation with a stated position on this technology. The figures above are theirs and STAT's reading of them; the underlying records were not read here. The 5,944 figure and the Innovaccer warning are specific and checkable enough that they will not survive long if wrong.

The state has confirmed dengue killed the Tampa woman — and the curve may finally be bending

Florida & Tampa Bay Independent corroboration

The Florida Department of Health has confirmed dengue as the cause of death of the 80-year-old Hillsborough County woman who died on 4 September. She died of complications including septic shock and multi-organ failure, after being bitten near her home on Bayshore Boulevard. She is reported to be the first person in the Tampa Bay region killed by the virus in close to a century.

That closes a question this brief has carried open since 12 September, when the investigation was announced and the department would say nothing further. Local reporting named her at the time and does so now; this brief has not, and does not, because the public interest turns on the confirmation rather than on her identity. Nothing about that has changed.

The second finding is the one that changes the picture. Health reporting on the confirmation notes signs that the Hillsborough outbreak may be waning. It sits alongside the current ledger: 152 locally acquired cases statewide this year, the second-highest total on record, with more than 130 of them in Hillsborough — a county that normally sees a handful.

Proportion carefully, as this file has each time. One confirmed death in 152 cases is not a change in the disease. It is the first measurement of what this outbreak can do.

What to watch: the week-37 report, and the PCR serotyping line.

Dig deeper — what “waning” can and cannot mean here

Be careful with the word. This brief spent nine consecutive issues recording that the state's weekly arbovirus report had not published, then two days ago recorded week 36 — 6 to 12 September — as the outbreak's largest week, with Hillsborough up 39 in seven days to 134 and the statewide locally acquired total at 152. A report showing the steepest week of the outbreak and health officials saying the outbreak may be waning are not in conflict, because they are about different moments: week 36 counts infections largely acquired in late August and early September, before and during the first aerial adulticide runs over South Tampa on 5–6 September. Dengue has a 4–10 day incubation and then a testing and reporting lag. What officials can see now is not in the published series yet.

Three reasons to hold the optimism loosely, all of them recorded here before the good news arrived. Surveillance intensity rose sharply in the same period — an alert, a named “epicenter”, far more testing — so some of the reported rise was ascertainment rather than incidence, and by the same logic some of any fall will be attention moving on. A nine-week publication gap means the reported series is not a clean week-by-week measurement of anything. And seasonality is doing work no spray truck can claim credit for: Aedes aegypti activity falls with the end of the wet season regardless of what anyone does.

The number that would actually change the risk picture is still not the case count. It is a second serotype in the PCR line. Dengue's danger is concentrated in second infections with a different serotype, where prior antibodies make severe disease more likely rather than less. Seventy-one of the week-34 cohort were serotyped. That line is in the weekly report, the report is a PDF that will not parse in this environment, and obtaining it remains the single most valuable outstanding item on this thread.

The state ledger, checked this morning: the Department of Health's arbovirus surveillance index still ends at week 36. Week 37 has not published.

Florida & Tampa Bay Florida TaxWatch has come out against Amendment 3, warning the homestead exemption increase shifts the bill to everyone else.

Macro & markets The Bank of Japan ran a rate check and pulled the yen back to the upper 156s — the first of this thread's currency triggers to fire.

Two days after raising the uncollateralised overnight call rate 25bp to around 1.25% — the highest in 31 years, on a split vote — the Bank of Japan conducted a rate check, and the yen strengthened to the upper 156 range against the dollar, reversing the slide to 158 that followed the decision.

A rate check is a central bank telephoning dealers to ask where the currency is trading. It buys nothing and commits to nothing. It is also the conventional last step before intervention, which is precisely why it works often enough to be worth doing. The market read it as a warning and the market may be right.

This thread's wake triggers included the yen strengthening from 158 or sliding further, and this is the former. The others remain unfired: the Summary of Opinions and the minutes, which would give the vote tally and the dissenters' reasons, and a dated 10-year JGB level, which this brief still has not obtained. Without that yield, the claim that BoJ tightening raises the global anchor for duration rests on the policy rate and the currency, not on the Japanese bond market.

Below the line because a rate check is a gesture, not a transaction, and the thread's substantive question — whether Japanese institutions stop bidding for long-dated Treasuries — shows up in auction participation over quarters, not in a currency move over a weekend.
