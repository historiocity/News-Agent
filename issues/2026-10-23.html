The ten-year fell 17 basis points in a day and went back under 5%

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Continuation Primary source

Treasury's Thursday print, read by enumerating the column headers and taking cells by position: ten-year 4.96%, down 17 basis points; two-year 4.65%, down 16; twenty-year 5.32%, down 15; thirty-year 5.29%, down 13. The whole curve moved together, and it is the largest single-day fall this brief has recorded.

The ten-year is back below 5% for the first time since 23 September, when it crossed at 5.11 on the way up. The month's high was 5.31 on 5 October. From there to 4.96 is 35 basis points, and 17 of them came yesterday.

Equities went the other way and the reason is specific. The Nasdaq fell 2.6 per cent, its steepest since 5 June; the S&P 500 fell 1.2 per cent, its worst since 29 July. Every one of the Magnificent 7 except Apple lost ground. Meta fell nearly 7 per cent after raising its 2026 capital spending guidance to $135bn–$145bn while missing on profit — the AI-capex question put as plainly as a market can put it. Amazon −4.6, Nvidia −4.4, Tesla and Alphabet −3.1, Microsoft −2.2, Intel and AMD about −8, Broadcom −4.3. The Nasdaq is 5.8 per cent off its 6 October record.

And oil rose into it. Brent futures gained 4 per cent on Thursday and went above $114.20 on Friday morning, the highest since May. Equities down, oil up, the whole Treasury curve bid: that is a risk-off day with two drivers, and the second one is the next story in this issue.

One detail for the thread this brief has run all month. The twenty-minus-ten hump widened back to +36 basis points after two sessions at +35 and +34. On a day when every tenor moved more than a dozen points, the hump moved two — the strongest evidence yet that it is structural rather than noise.

A source says the US and Israel are planning to strike Iran after the midterms

Geopolitics Continuation Single-sourced

A person briefed on American and Israeli war planning told NPR on Thursday that the two governments are preparing for a possible attack on Iran in early November, after the US midterm elections. The same person said Israel had initially planned to strike in mid-October, about two weeks before its own elections on 27 October, and that the President objected.

The United States is said to be amassing forces to be ready by early November — with the caveat the source supplied, passed on intact: the preparations could equally be pressure for a diplomatic deal. Those are not distinguishable from outside, and force movements serve both.

Iran answered two ways. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told state media: "We are just as prepared for diplomacy as we are for war," adding that any scenario was conceivable. Either the administration has one timetable with two possible contents, or two accounts of it are circulating.

Iran has blocked the Strait of Hormuz since, targeting commercial vessels; the US has blockaded Iranian ports and oil exports. American strikes have degraded Iran's air force, air defences and navy; Iran has struck US bases in the Gulf and Jordan. Netanyahu, seeking re-election on Tuesday, is campaigning on toppling the Iranian government.

What to watch: the Israeli election on 27 October and whether its result changes the timetable; visible force movements, which are the only part of this that can be checked from outside; and 3 November, now carrying two incompatible predictions.

Treasury has written the rule that stops anyone owning 350 houses from buying another

US politics & policy US financials New thread Underreported Primary source

Treasury has proposed the rule implementing section 1001 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which prohibits large institutional investors from purchasing single-family homes, subject to exceptions. The statute exists; this is the document that says who it catches.

The threshold is the number to hold on to. A "large institutional investor" is an entity with direct or indirect investment control of not less than 350 single-family homes. "Investment control" reaches through corporate structure: it includes owning or controlling more than 25 per cent of any class of equity interests in the entity that owns the home, unless that entity is a passive investor. Homes bought in an excepted purchase after enactment do not count toward the 350.

The penalty has teeth. A violation draws a civil penalty of up to the greater of $1 million or three times the purchase price of the property — per violation, which on a portfolio acquisition is per house.

The exceptions are where the lobbying will be, and the proposed rule is largely an exercise in defining them. It clarifies "program", "build-to-rent program", "renovate-to-rent program", "homeownership program", "program to boost homeownership", "engaged in the business of", "passive investor", "purchase price of the single-family home" and what counts as a structural or core system. Build-to-rent is the significant one: an investor who finances new houses and rents them has added to supply, which is the policy case for letting it continue, and is also the obvious route around a purchase ban.

Separately, section 1001(c)(8) requires large institutional investors to file an annual notice to HUD listing the single-family homes they control. HUD will prescribe the form; this rule does not.

What to watch: how the final rule draws the build-to-rent line, which decides whether the ban binds; whether the 25 per cent look-through survives comment, since it is what stops the 350 being evaded through subsidiaries; and HUD's notice form, which is the only place the holdings become public.

Confidence: Primary source. The 350-home threshold, the 25 per cent investment-control test, the civil penalty, the list of defined terms and the HUD notice requirement were read from the proposed rule itself. The comment deadline was not extracted, and the rule's regulatory impact analysis and the full text of the excepted purchases were not read. Sources: Treasury, 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act section 1001 proposed rule

Chip cards are coming to food stamps, eight days after this brief asked when

US politics & policy Continuation Underreported Primary source

On 15 October this brief led on criminals taking as much as $233m from food-stamp cards by skimming, on a USDA Inspector General report, and said the vulnerability was that SNAP still ran on magnetic stripes while the rest of American payments had moved on. A proposed rule published this morning does something about it.

The USDA's Food and Nutrition Administration proposes to modernise Electronic Benefit Transfer technology and align it with consumer payment industry standards. The rule's own account of the problem is unusually direct: EBT replaced paper coupons in the 1990s, when magnetic stripes were the predominant payment technology, and "EBT cards utilizing magnetic stripes have fallen behind newer consumer payment methods in terms of security, placing household SNAP benefits at risk."

What it would permit is a menu rather than a mandate: an online magnetic stripe card as now, an offline smart card holding the balance on the card itself, a chip card to the EMV standard, a contactless digital wallet with a stored card, or any similar method. The rule defines EMV in its own glossary — Europay, Mastercard and Visa, the integrated-circuit standard that American retail banking adopted a decade ago.

The distance being travelled is the story. Chip cards became the US retail standard in 2015, when liability for card-present fraud shifted to whichever party had not adopted them. The people whose benefits are stolen are the one group for whom that shift never happened, because the states issuing the cards carried no such liability — and, as this brief reported on 15 October, most states do not replace stolen benefits.

Comments close 22 December 2026.

What to watch: whether the final rule mandates chip or merely permits it, which is the whole difference; who pays for reissuing cards and upgrading state EBT host processors; and whether replacement of stolen benefits is addressed anywhere, because a secure card does not help anyone already robbed.

Confidence: Primary source. The proposed changes, the quoted assessment of magnetic stripes, the list of permitted access methods, the EMV definition and the comment deadline were read from the rule itself. The rule runs to roughly 290,000 characters and this brief read the summary and the technology provisions; cost estimates, the implementation timetable and any state-funding provisions were not read. The $233m and the non-replacement point are from the 15 October issue. Sources: USDA FNA, SNAP EBT Card Security Measures · 15 October

The federal stablecoin regime has a rulebook and a date: 18 January

US financials New thread Underreported Primary source

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has issued the final rule implementing the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act — the GENIUS Act, enacted 18 July 2025 and codified at 12 U.S.C. 5901 and following — for the issuance of payment stablecoins by entities under the Comptroller's jurisdiction. It takes effect on 18 January 2027.

That sentence is worth unpacking because the word "final" is doing a lot of work. The statute has been law for fifteen months and has been a framework without a rulebook for most of them; the agency that charters national banks has now written the part that says what a bank or a federal branch may actually do. From January, issuing a payment stablecoin inside the national banking system stops being a question of statutory interpretation and becomes a question of compliance with a rule.

The Act's own definition is in the preamble and is narrower than the word suggests: stablecoins are digital assets — digital representations of value recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger, such as a blockchain. Payment stablecoins are the subset the Act regulates, which is the subset intended to be used to make payments rather than held as a speculation.

This brief is reporting the existence, the authority and the date, and not the substance. The rule runs to roughly 1.5 million characters — the longest document this brief has encountered — and only its summary and background were read. The reserve requirements, the capital treatment, the permissible activities, what happens to an issuer in resolution and the treatment of foreign issuers are all in there and none of them is reported here.

What to watch: the reserve and capital provisions, which decide whether a bank-issued stablecoin is economically worth issuing; whether the Federal Reserve and the FDIC follow with parallel rules for the institutions they supervise; and 18 January, which now shares a date with the CBP postal compliance deadline and the NIH women's health division.

Confidence: Primary source for the rule's existence, issuing agency, statutory authority, scope and effective date, read from the document on GovInfo. Explicitly not read: everything else in it. A 1.5-million-character final rule deserves a full reading and did not get one this morning; it is named here so the reader knows it exists and so this brief owes it. Sources: OCC, implementing the GENIUS Act

Science & public health FDA has proposed action levels for cadmium in baby food.

The Food and Drug Administration has issued draft guidance proposing action levels for cadmium in processed food intended for babies and young children. Cadmium is one of the heavy metals — with lead, arsenic and mercury — that accumulate in root vegetables and rice and that congressional investigations put into baby food several years ago.

An action level is not a legal limit. It is the concentration at which FDA will consider a product adulterated and may act, which gives manufacturers a target without creating a standard that must be met. That distinction decides whether this changes anything, and this brief did not read the draft guidance, so the proposed concentrations, the foods covered and the comment period are unstated here.

The same issue carries a second FDA draft guidance on hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls for human food, which is the general food-safety framework the cadmium levels would operate inside.

Below the line because the numbers are the entire content of an action-level document and they were not read.

US politics & policy A crash-dummy rulemaking lands the day after the women's health executive order, and the pairing is not a coincidence worth missing.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed amendments to Part 572, Anthropomorphic Test Devices — the regulations specifying crash test dummies — concerning the 50th percentile adult male restraint test device.

Yesterday's executive order established a women's health division at NIH and listed conditions where women have been under-researched. The 50th percentile adult male dummy is the standing example from a different field: American crash standards were written for decades around a dummy representing an average man, and studies of real crashes have repeatedly found women more likely to be injured at equivalent severity. Whether the test device or vehicle design causes that gap is contested, but the dummy is where the argument starts.

This brief read the title and not the proposal, so whether these amendments bear on that question at all — they may be purely technical updates to an existing device's specification — is unknown. The pairing is offered as a thing to look at, not a finding.

Below the line because it rests on a title and an inference, which is the weakest material this brief runs.

US politics & policy The President has been ordered to sit for a deposition in his defamation suit against the BBC.

A court has ordered the President to sit for a deposition in the defamation lawsuit he brought against the BBC. A sitting president giving sworn testimony in his own civil case is unusual enough to be worth a paragraph whatever the underlying claim.

The mechanism is the point. A plaintiff who sues puts himself inside the discovery system: he cannot assert a reputational injury and then decline to be questioned about it. That is why defamation suits brought by powerful people are risky for them, and why deposition orders are often the moment such cases are abandoned.

This brief has no court, no docket number, no date for the deposition, no indication whether it will be recorded or sealed, and no sight of the order. Nor does it have the substance of the suit beyond its parties.
