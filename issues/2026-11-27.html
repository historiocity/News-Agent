Every federal contractor must now tell the White House before answering Congress

Government & oversight New subject Underreported Primary source

Executive Order 14438, signed on 20 November and posted on the White House's site, requires every federal contractor — and every subcontractor working on a federal contract — to notify the White House and the contracting agency of any pending or future congressional request touching their communications or work with the executive branch, and then to obey whatever instructions they receive about withholding material the White House or the agency says is privileged.

The order rests on the Federal Property and Administrative Services Act and, expressly, on “the common law.” Its stated grievance is specific: certain congressional leaders have been “harassing private companies” that contract with the government. Its remedy runs through the contract rather than the Constitution. Section 4 instructs agencies to consider, against a contractor that does not comply, “termination or cancellation of contracts” and “debarment or suspension.” The Office of Management and Budget is to write guidance on how agencies verify compliance. A severability clause anticipates that part of it will be struck down.

This is the instrument this brief has been recording as owed since the start of the month, when it existed only as a reported intention. It is now numbered, signed and operative on its own terms — contractors “must immediately” comply — but it has not yet appeared in the Federal Register; it was not among today's 137 documents. Publication is what starts the clocks that OMB guidance will hang on, so the gap between signature and publication is the thing to watch, along with the first contractor to receive a withholding instruction and say so.

Divergence — what the order can and cannot do

Substance. A dispute about significance, not facts: the text is short and public. The order's own framing is efficiency — avoiding “duplicative and wasteful” parallel litigation when a contractor and the government answer the same subpoena separately. Read against its Section 1, the operative effect is narrower than the framing and wider than the efficiency claim: it does not create any new privilege, and Section 6(c) says it creates no enforceable right at all; what it creates is a contractual duty to wait.

Cause. The vantage explains the two readings. From inside the executive branch, a contractor who answers Congress before the government can assert privilege has pre-empted a decision that was the government's to make. From outside, the same sequence is the only one in which a congressional request reaches a company that would rather not be in the middle. Both descriptions fit the text; they differ on who owns the delay.

What it means for you. Believe the narrow reading of the law and the wide reading of the practice. The order cannot enlarge executive privilege, and a court asked to enforce a withholding instruction would have to decide the privilege question on its merits. But the order does not need to win that argument to work: debarment is an existential threat to a contractor and a privilege fight is not, so the rational contractor notifies and waits whatever the law ultimately says. What would settle it is the first refusal — a contractor that complies with a subpoena anyway, and what the contracting agency then does. Until then, treat any contractor silence in front of a committee as explained by this order rather than by the contractor.

The automatic-braking standard is to be postponed two years, because no car the agency tested could pass it

Transport & safety New subject Underreported Primary source

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration proposed this morning to move the compliance date of Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 127 — forward-collision warning, automatic emergency braking and pedestrian automatic emergency braking on every light vehicle — from 1 September 2029 to 1 September 2031. Small-volume manufacturers, final-stage manufacturers and alterers get a further year, to 2032. The notice runs 34 pages at 91 FR 76683; comments close on 26 January 2027.

The ground is the agency's own test data, and it is blunt. Of six vehicles tested in 2023 for the 2024 final rule, “no vehicle avoided contact in all trials conducted” — which is one of the standard's requirements. A 2023 Toyota Corolla Hybrid avoided contact in each scenario on most attempts, and still made contact on some. Ten model-year 2025 vehicles have since been through the agency's crash-test programme, and those preliminary results are in the notice.

The standard is not discretionary in origin: the 2021 infrastructure act directed the Secretary of Transportation to issue it. The rule was finalised in May 2024, substantially upheld against petitions for reconsideration that November, and challenged by the Alliance for Automotive Innovation in the D.C. Circuit in January 2025, where it remains.

What to watch: whether the comment file produces a manufacturer saying plainly that it can meet the 2029 date, which would undercut the premise; and the model-year 2025 test reports as they land in the public docket.

Divergence — what the delay costs

Substance. A dispute about facts, and it is adjudicable from the document. The 2024 analysis concluded the rule would prevent 362 deaths and 24,321 injuries a year. Today's notice cites that figure, then says it cannot quantify the benefits and costs of its own proposal because it cannot quantify the baseline, and concludes the delay would have “no incremental impact on safety benefits or costs.”

Cause. The zero rests on one assumption, stated in the notice: manufacturers who can comply will do so regardless of the deadline, to compete for safety-conscious buyers, and those who cannot will comply as soon as they are able, so the deadline never binds. That is a coherent argument and it is also the argument against having the standard. The counter-vantage is the rule's own history — Congress legislated a mandate in 2021 precisely because voluntary fitment had not produced systems that performed to a measured bar.

What it means for you. The evidence supports the agency on feasibility and not on cost. Six of six vehicles failing is a real finding about the 2029 date, not an excuse. But “no incremental impact” is a conclusion the notice disclaims the ability to compute, reached about a standard whose own file carries an annual casualty number; two years against 362 deaths a year is arithmetic the document declines to do, in either direction. Treat the delay as probably warranted on engineering grounds and unsupported on the safety accounting. What would settle it: the model-year 2025 results in the docket. If current cars are close to the bar, the lead-time argument weakens sharply.

Sources: Federal Register, NHTSA proposed rule, 91 FR 76683, 27 November 2026; full text via govinfo.gov (read in full).

A presidential memorandum orders the Attorney General to speed up executions

Law & courts New subject Underreported Primary source

A memorandum to the Attorney General, signed 23 November, filed for publication on the 25th at 11:15am and published this morning at 91 FR 76953, directs the Justice Department to do two things.

First, to seek federal jurisdiction and the death penalty for three categories of crime beyond those already listed in the January 2025 executive order on capital punishment: first-degree murder of a minor; murder committed in the course of rape, sexual abuse, sexual exploitation of a child or sex trafficking; and murder committed in the course of kidnapping or human trafficking.

Second, to carry out federal death sentences “as expeditiously as possible,” by prioritising the prompt resolution of direct appeals and habeas petitions, opposing “dilatory litigation tactics,” seeking expedited judicial consideration and eliminating administrative delays.

Two features of the instrument matter more than its language. It is a memorandum, not an executive order, so it carries no number and amends nothing — it adds categories to the January 2025 order without touching that order's text. And it states that it creates no right enforceable by anyone, is subject to the availability of appropriations, and that the Justice Department pays for its own publication: three clauses that, read together, mark it as an internal instruction rather than a legal change.

The timing is worth recording. The memorandum was signed on 23 November and filed for publication on the 25th, two days before a federal holiday weekend, which is a disclosure window that reliably produces the thinnest coverage of the year. This brief found it by reading the whole Federal Register issue rather than by seeing it reported.

What to watch is not the Department's rhetoric but its filings: whether motions to expedite start appearing on federal capital dockets, and whether any court grants one.

Divergence — who controls the delay

Substance. A dispute about significance. On its face the memorandum reads as a change in federal capital practice. Read against what it can reach, most of what it addresses is not the executive branch's to decide: the length of a habeas proceeding is set by statute, by the courts' own calendars and by the volume of claims, none of which a memorandum touches.

Cause. The two readings come from different objects. Treat the document as an announcement and it is a policy shift; treat it as an instruction and it is a direction about litigating posture — what the government argues, how fast it asks to go, what it declines to consent to. Nothing in the text binds a judge, and the document says as much in its general provisions. The audiences differ too: the first three sections read outward, the fourth reads like counsel's redraft.

What it means for you. The second reading is the one the text supports. The measurable consequence is in the categories, not the speed: directing prosecutors to seek federal jurisdiction for child-victim and trafficking homicides is a charging decision, which is entirely the executive's, and it will show up as new capital notices in cases that would otherwise have stayed in state court. The expedition half will show up only if courts cooperate. What would settle it: count federal capital charging notices over the next two quarters, and count granted motions to expedite. The first number will move; the second is the test of the memorandum.

Sources: Federal Register, Memorandum of 23 November 2026, 91 FR 76953; full text via govinfo.gov (read in full); White House listing.

Petrol is down sixteen per cent in a month. Diesel is still half again what it cost a year ago

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

AAA's averages this morning: regular petrol $3.5476 a gallon, against $4.2661 a month ago — a fall of 16.8% in four weeks, and now only 17.3% above the $3.0255 of a year ago. Diesel $5.7048, against $6.3521 a month ago: down 10.2%, and still 51.4% above the $3.7690 of last November.

The two fuels are not having the same year. Petrol never approached its record, $5.0165, set in June 2022. Diesel set its all-time record this September — $6.5276 on the 22nd — and is 12.6% below it. The weekly retail series from the EIA, charted below, puts the whole war into one line: $3.477 in the first week of January, $3.897 on 2 March, $4.859 on 9 March, a peak of $6.529 on 21 September, $5.695 on 23 November.

Crude is the reason prices are falling and not the reason diesel is high. Brent ran from nearly $120 at the end of October to about $90 on Wednesday, gained some $2 on Thursday to its best settlement since 6 November, and stood at $96.61 this morning, with West Texas Intermediate at $85.78 — a three-week losing streak about to end on supply fears. Demand has gone with it: European diesel consumption this year is running below 2020, with double-digit monthly falls in Italy and France and a 10% year-on-year decline in Germany over the first eight months.

What to watch: the OPEC+ meeting on Sunday, and whether the EIA's price series catch up next Wednesday.

Divergence — three prices moving three ways

Substance. A dispute about significance between the crude screens and the shipping market, and both are right. Crude has lost roughly a quarter of its price in a month and physical cargo markets look comfortably supplied; tanker freight is at or near record highs and a used supertanker now costs more than a new one (see the sixth lead). Read the first and the fuel crisis is ending. Read the second and it is not.

Cause. The war did not take away crude; it took away two other things. It took away distillate — refining capacity in the Gulf plus sustained strikes on Russian refineries — which is why diesel and not petrol broke its record. And it took away tanker capacity, by lengthening voyages: shuttle runs and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman have stretched the average round trip to 23 days against an optimal nine or ten, so the same barrel consumes more than twice the shipping. Crude price reflects whether the barrel exists. Freight reflects how far it has to travel to get here. They are answering different questions.

What it means for you. Expect the pump to keep falling into December on the crude lag — the month-ago comparison has further to run. Expect the diesel premium to be stickier than the petrol fall, because closing it requires voyage lengths to normalise rather than crude to cheapen, and nothing in this week's news shortens a voyage. The observation that would settle it is the mix of transits through Hormuz: fewer very large carriers and more small tankers, which is what the tracking data now shows, is the signature of a workaround, not of a recovery.

A number this brief is not printing

The diesel crack spread has appeared in this file for weeks. It is not here today, and for the second day running. Of the six EIA weekly series tracked, the two the spread is computed from — spot West Texas Intermediate and ultra-low-sulphur diesel at New York Harbour — are still dated 13 November; the other four updated to the 20th or 23rd. A fortnight's lag would be tolerable in a quiet market. It is not tolerable across a move of this size: the stale WTI reads $90.78 while the live screen reads $85.78. Computing a spread from a crude price five dollars above the market and a product price of the same vintage would produce a figure that looks current and is not. The last honest reading stands at $88.35 as of 13 November, and it is labelled with that date rather than today's.

The storm took the power out for nearly a million homes and moved the parade to Saturday

Weather & infrastructure Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief reported that the hundredth Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade had been postponed for the first time in its history, on a forecast of gusts near 50mph and heavy rain. The forecast verified.

More than 900,000 homes and businesses were without power on Thursday evening, most of them on the East Coast, and New Jersey and parts of New York were placed under states of emergency. Flight tracking recorded more than 2,000 flights to or from US airports delayed and more than 400 cancelled. The National Weather Service had wind advisories out for northern Georgia, most of the mid-Atlantic and southern New England; its active-alert count stood at 554 this morning. Two people were pulled from their cars in flooding in New York City, where the mayor said there was no destination worth a reader's safety, and similar rescues were reported in other coastal communities. The parade will now step off at 8:30am Eastern on Saturday.

The postponement was the story that could be reported in advance; it is also the one that was reported everywhere, because it has a date and a landmark in it. The outage is the one with a cost in it, and it is still open. Nearly a million customers without power going into a holiday weekend is a bill that lands later, in restoration overtime, in spoiled stock and in the regulatory filings that follow a multi-day outage, and none of those numbers exist yet.

Nor is the weather finished. The National Hurricane Center reports no tropical cyclones in the Atlantic, so this was an extratropical system, and the 554 alerts still live this morning are the tail of it rather than something new.

What to watch over the weekend: restoration curves rather than wind speeds, and whether the rescheduled parade draws the crowds a Saturday morning in late November can support.

Divergence — whose number for how many lost power

Substance. A dispute about facts, and a small one worth naming because it recurs. Wire copy this morning put the figure at “nearly a million” and at “more than 900,000” in the same report, both sourced to the same crowd-tracking site. This brief could not read that site directly — it refused an automated request — so the number here is the one the wire carried, not one verified at source.

Cause. Outage counts are snapshots of a moving quantity and they are assembled from utility dashboards that update on different schedules. “Nearly a million” and “more than 900,000” are the same measurement rounded for two different sentences, taken at a peak that had probably already passed by the time it was published. Neither is wrong; neither is a count of households, since the unit is the metered customer.

What it means for you. Treat any single outage figure from a storm as accurate to about one significant digit and already historic. The number that matters is the restoration curve, which is published later and by state regulators rather than by wires, and which is where a storm turns into a story about a utility. If you want one figure to hold on to, hold the 554 active weather alerts, which is a direct count from the National Weather Service's own interface and not a rounded aggregate.

A fifteen-year-old supertanker now costs more than a new one

Shipping & trade New subject Underreported Primary source

A fifteen-year-old very large crude carrier is worth $165m. Ordering a new one costs $132m. The used ship is 25% dearer than the new ship, and more than three and a half times the $46m that a fifteen-year-old carrier has averaged over the past decade. The figures are from CMB.TECH's third-quarter report, published Tuesday, citing Clarksons data.

The inversion has a mechanical cause. Middle Eastern oil exports have largely recovered — combined Saudi, Iraqi, Emirati, Kuwaiti and Qatari shipments reached 10.9m barrels a day in October, against 6.7m in April — but much of that recovery runs through shuttle voyages and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman. CMB.TECH counts 141 tankers now dedicated entirely to shuttling, 72% of them very large carriers, and puts the average round voyage at 23 days against an optimal nine or ten. The barrels move; the tonnage that moves them is removed from the wider market.

The earnings follow. The company's Euronav division averaged $149,945 a day on very large carriers in the third quarter, against $30,486 a year earlier, and $635,199 a day so far in the fourth with 59% of available days booked. Suezmaxes are booked at $377,164. Quarterly net profit was $373.9m against $17.3m.

What to watch: the orderbook. Very large carriers on order now equal 33.1% of the trading fleet, Suezmaxes 29.3%.

Dig deeper — the United Nations put a number on the same effect yesterday

UNCTAD published its Review of Maritime Transport 2026 on Thursday, and its headline finding is the same phenomenon measured from the other end. Global seaborne trade will register zero growth this year, after 1.9% growth to 12.3bn tonnes in 2025; a 3.2% recovery is forecast for 2027. Yet tonne-mile demand — cargo multiplied by distance — is forecast to grow 1.5%, because ships are sailing further. The agency attributes the pattern to simultaneous disruption at four of the world's most important chokepoints, and its acting secretary-general observed that trade is slowing as the cost of disruption rises.

Two numbers in that report are the reversal risk that CMB.TECH's own report also flags, from a different sector. The containership orderbook now stands at 36% of the trading fleet by gross tonnage, against 11% at the start of the decade, and UNCTAD warns of significant overcapacity if carriers return to the Suez Canal en masse. Global shipyard output is forecast up 14% this year, driven mainly by Chinese capacity. The same logic applies to tankers: a third of the very large carrier fleet is on order, and an eventual normalisation of Hormuz would release the 141 shuttle tankers back into the market at roughly the moment those newbuildings start delivering.

Two structural figures are worth keeping. 42% of the world's merchant ships are more than twenty years old, which is why a fifteen-year-old hull is a prime asset rather than a tired one. And vessels able to run on alternative fuels account for just 9.4% of fleet tonnage, which is the measure of how little of the decarbonisation transition has actually been built — and of why regulatory uncertainty keeps showing up in these reports as a reason owners will not commit.

One caution on the earnings. The $635,199 a day is a partial-quarter average on 59% of days booked, reported by a company with an interest in the number being impressive, and it includes the effect of selling ships into a hot market — $100.2m of the third-quarter profit was gains on two Suezmax sales, with about $190.8m more expected in the fourth from three further sales. Strip the asset sales and the operating business is still extraordinary, but the headline profit is not all freight.

Divergence — tight tonnage, flat trade

Substance. A dispute about facts that resolves cleanly once the units are right. The shipping market reports the tightest tonnage in memory. UNCTAD reports zero growth in seaborne trade. Both are measured correctly; they measure different things, and the gap between them is exactly the war.

Cause. Trade volume is tonnes loaded. Tanker demand is tonnes multiplied by distance, and then divided by how fast a ship can turn around. Volume is flat and distance is up 1.5% by UNCTAD's forecast — but the binding constraint is neither, it is waiting time: a 23-day round voyage against an optimal nine or ten means a single ship does less than half the work it used to. That is a capacity loss of more than 50% on the affected trade, invisible in any tonnage statistic.

What it means for you. Freight rates are not telling you that the world wants more oil; they are telling you that the fleet has been made inefficient. This is why the sixth lead and the fourth lead point in opposite directions and both are right, and it is why the diesel premium will outlast the crude fall. The observation that would settle the reversal question is the shuttle count: when the number of tankers dedicated to shuttling starts falling from 141, the inversion unwinds, and a third of the fleet arriving new from the yards will unwind it hard.

A projectile killed a worker at Jazan, and the refinery Saudi Arabia built to avoid Hormuz is now a target

Energy & grid Continuation Independent corroboration

A hostile aerial projectile hit the Jazan refinery on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast on Thursday, killing one worker — a Nepali national — injuring several others, starting a fire and suspending some operations. The description is the Saudi energy ministry's own. The Saudi-led coalition in Yemen attributed the strike to the Houthis and said it had destroyed a ballistic-missile launch platform at Hodeidah port that had been used for it. Jazan can process up to 400,000 barrels of crude a day.

The day before, the Houthis claimed attacks on Ras Tanura — the kingdom's largest refinery and a major crude export terminal — and on Red Sea ports.

Geography is the point. Ras Tanura faces the Gulf and ships through the Strait of Hormuz. Jazan faces the Red Sea, and the East–West pipeline that feeds the kingdom's Red Sea coast exists precisely so that Saudi crude can reach the world without passing Hormuz. Striking both in two days is an attack on the primary route and on its designed alternative. That is a different proposition from either strike alone, and it bears directly on why tanker tonnage is scarce: a bypass that is itself under fire is not a bypass that shortens anyone's voyage.

What to watch: whether Jazan's suspended units restart within days, and whether Saudi crude loadings shift again between coasts.

Divergence — what “some operations” conceals

Substance. A dispute about facts that cannot yet be resolved, and the unresolved part is the material part. The ministry says a fire started and “some operations” were temporarily halted. It does not say which units, how much of the 400,000 barrels a day is affected, or for how long. Market reporting treated the strike as a supply risk and oil rose; the official statement supports neither a large nor a small number.

Cause. The asymmetry is deliberate on both sides and ordinary. A national oil company has every reason to minimise disclosed damage to a refinery and no obligation to quantify it, and an attacking party has every reason to overstate. The coalition's announcement of a destroyed launch platform is a response to the same incentive from the other direction — it answers the question of retaliation rather than the question of damage. No independent assessment of Jazan's throughput was available this morning.

What it means for you. Do not price this strike from the headline. The verifiable facts are a death, a fire and an acknowledged partial suspension at a 400,000-barrel plant; the unverifiable one is how much refining is actually offline. What would settle it is observable within about a week, and not from statements: Saudi product export loadings from Jazan, and whether Red Sea crude loadings fall while Gulf loadings rise. If the kingdom quietly moves barrels back towards Hormuz, that is the measure of the damage, and it would also tighten tanker demand further.

The Lukoil price is $10bn in securities, and nobody can touch it for five years

Energy & grid Continuation Underreported Single-sourced

Correction — yesterday's framing

Yesterday this brief reported the Lukoil International sale and said flatly that “the price was not disclosed,” and that the absence of any valuation of the swapped restricted shares was “the single number that would make the deal assessable.” The first half was right as of yesterday morning and wrong by this morning: the consideration is $10bn in securities. The second half was wrong in a more useful way. A headline value was never the number that would make this deal assessable, and printing it today does not make it so — because of what follows.

The consortium led by Todd Boehly's Eldridge has signed an agreement to take all the equity of Austria-based Lukoil International in exchange for restricted shares in Lukoil and other Russian companies, with the consideration put at $10bn. The structure that matters is this: the consideration is to be held in a restricted custodial account for five years, or until sanctions are lifted.

So Russia's seller receives paper it cannot realise for half a decade or until a political decision is taken in Washington. That is not a sale price in any ordinary sense; it is an option on sanctions relief, and the $10bn is its face value, not its market value. What the assets are worth and what the sellers are actually getting remain two different questions, and the second is still unanswered.

One new asset detail: the portfolio includes a 19.99% stake in the BP-operated Shah Deniz gas and condensate development in Azerbaijan's sector of the Caspian — a producing interest in a field that supplies Europe through the Southern Gas Corridor, which is a different sort of asset from a filling-station network and was not in yesterday's description. The precise composition of the upstream being transferred has still not been disclosed.

The US International Development Finance Corporation will take an ownership interest on behalf of the US government; its size is still not public, and it is the figure that decides whether this is a policy instrument with a token government presence or a state investment in a Russian-built oil business. OFAC approval and due diligence remain outstanding, as does the question of what the Austrian holding company's European refineries do in the meantime.

What to watch: the DFC's stake, and whether OFAC conditions the custodial arrangement.

Divergence — is $10bn a price

Substance. A dispute about significance, and this brief has been on the wrong side of it. The figure now reported is a consideration of $10bn in securities. Whether that is a “price” depends entirely on the custodial term, which was reported in the same sentence and is the less quoted half.

Cause. Deal coverage reaches for the headline number because it is the comparable, and because a five-year custodial lock is a structuring detail rather than a figure. The consortium has an interest in the number being large, since it demonstrates that the assets were not acquired cheaply from a distressed sanctioned seller; the seller has an interest in it being large for the same reason in reverse. Neither side has an interest in emphasising that the paper is frozen, because that is the part that invites the question of whether this is a purchase or a parking arrangement.

What it means for you. Read $10bn as a notional, not a payment. The deal's economics turn on a probability nobody has published: the chance that sanctions on Lukoil are lifted inside five years. Price that high and Russia has sold assets for a valuable claim; price it low and Russia has swapped operating assets for escrow. The observation that would settle it is OFAC's decision and its conditions — specifically whether Treasury permits the custodial account at all, and on what release terms. Yesterday's judgement that this deal is not assessable without a number was right; it was wrong about which number.

Shipping · Seventeen tankers have been struck in the Strait of Hormuz since 1 November, and a seafarer missing since the 17th has been confirmed dead.

The Joint Maritime Information Center's running count reached seventeen tankers struck this month, with the most recent confirmed attack on Wednesday — an unidentified tanker hit while transiting, crew accounted for, no pollution reported. Three tankers were struck on Sunday and a bulk carrier attacked in the Gulf of Oman. Shipping · Eight international shipping associations have written to the EU's foreign policy chief urging governments to refuse any compulsory charge for passing through an international strait.

Energy · OPEC+ ministers meet on Sunday with an independent audit of every member's production capacity in hand — and each member has seen only its own result.

Consumer safety · The Consumer Product Safety Commission's maximum civil penalties rise to $140,000 per violation and $20.35m for a related series, after 1 January.

The notice published this morning raises the ceilings from the $120,000 and $17,150,000 set on 1 December 2021. The adjustment is not discretionary: the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 requires the Commission to publish a revised schedule of maximum authorised penalties every fifth calendar year, and this is that publication. The Commission voted 3–0 to approve it. The statutes covered are the Consumer Product Safety Act, the Federal Hazardous Substances Act and the Flammable Fabrics Act. In practical terms the per-series cap is the one that binds: a recall involving thousands of units is a related series, and $20.35m is the most the Commission can extract for it however many units were sold. Against the revenue of a large importer, that remains a cost of doing business rather than a deterrent, which is the argument that recurs every five years when this notice appears.

Below the line because it is a statutorily mandated inflation adjustment with no policy choice in it; the figure is worth knowing, the event is not.
