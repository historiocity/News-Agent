Negative check: the SEC proposes to scrap federal shareholder-proposal rules and leave them to state law

US financials US politics & policy New subject Underreported Primary source Negative-check

In today’s Federal Register the Securities and Exchange Commission proposes to rescind Rule 14a-8 under the Exchange Act — the federal rule that has, for decades, forced public companies to include qualifying shareholder proposals in their proxy materials — and to leave “determinations about the role of shareholder proposals to State law and company governing documents.” Comment deadline: 20 November 2026.

The same package amends Rule 14a-4 to expand when a company may exercise discretionary voting authority on proposals that will be presented at a meeting but are not in the company’s proxy materials, while giving shareholders a way to elect to block that discretionary voting on their own shares. A companion proposed rule, “Proxy Solicitation Modernization,” would among other things eliminate the requirement that registrants deliver an annual report to security holders and shorten the minimum broker-search period — also comments through 20 Nov.

Rule 14a-8 is the machinery behind climate, labour, governance and political-spending proposals that asset managers and activists have used for a generation. Rescinding it does not ban proposals; it moves the fight into Delaware charters, bylaws and state courts — and into whoever controls the board’s proxy card.

What to watch: the comment file; whether large index managers (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) file as a bloc; any state legislative response that re-creates a mandatory-inclusion regime; litigation timing if the Commission finalises.

Divergence — investor voice vs management control

What is disputed. Whether federal proxy access is a disclosure/governance floor that markets need, or a federalisation of what corporate law left to states and charters.

Why they differ. Issuers and some Republican commissioners have long called 14a-8 a cost centre for political proposals. Pension funds and NGOs treat it as the cheapest formal channel into the annual meeting. Both can be true about different proposal classes.

What it means for you. Core US financials and policy. This came from the standing FR RULE/PRORULE sweep, not a beat tip — the overnight wires did not lead with it. If finalised, ESG and governance fights migrate to state law and private ordering.

Dig deeper — why this is bigger than “anti-ESG”

Rescission is not a narrowing of submission thresholds or a longer ownership period — those were prior fights. It is removing the federal inclusion mandate. A bylaw that simply refuses all shareholder proposals would, under this proposal, face state fiduciary and charter constraints rather than an SEC no-action process. That is a different game: more room for dual-class and controlled companies; more pressure on institutional holders to vote with their feet or sue in Chancery.

The 14a-4 discretionary-voting expansion is the operational half. Even if a shareholder forces a proposal onto the floor under state law, management’s ability to cast unmatched proxies for or against it is what often decides the tally. The proposal’s individual opt-out is real but easy to miss in retail accounts.

Fed and BoE are asking banks what they lent to trading firms after Jane Street’s reported $15 billion hit

US financials Macro & markets AI & tech industry New subject Single-sourced

Reuters, citing the Financial Times on Monday, reports that the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England are asking global banks about their exposures to large trading firms after turmoil at the AI-focused hedge fund Situational Awareness — run by former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner — contributed to a reported $15 billion hit at Jane Street. The fund was forced to sell most of its public-equities portfolio to Citadel Securities after a sharp sell-off in AI and chip stocks. Central banks want risk appetite, how bank exposure evolved intraday, and how risk controls operated.

Separately last month, the SEC subpoenaed Wall Street banks including Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Citigroup and Bank of America over Situational Awareness’ trading, leverage, margin calls and lender communications. Reuters could not independently verify Monday’s FT report; Fed, BoE and Jane Street did not immediately comment outside business hours.

What to watch: any formal supervisory letter or horizontal review announcement; whether the SEC subpoena expands past Situational Awareness to prime-broker AI books generally; Jane Street or bank 10-Q language.

Divergence — idiosyncratic loss vs systemic plumbing

What is disputed. Whether this is one fund’s concentration risk or a signal that AI-equity financing through non-bank trading firms is now large enough to worry prudential regulators.

Why they differ. Trading firms intermediate bank balance sheets; when one blows up, the question is always whether the banks saw the intraday path. That is what the questionnaires target.

What it means for you. Core US financials with an AI-capex financing channel. Not on yesterday’s Grok agenda. Labelled Single-sourced on the FT→Reuters chain until a primary regulator statement.

Dig deeper — the AI sell-off transmission

July’s AI/chip drawdown turned a concentrated long book into forced sales through a market-maker. The $15 billion figure traces to August Reuters exclusive sourcing; Monday’s new fact is the supervisory follow-up, not the original loss. Keep those dates straight.

Bessent proposes a US–China AI incident-notification channel ahead of Thursday’s Trump–Xi summit

AI & tech industry World economy Lab safety New subject

After Sunday’s New York talks with Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng and USTR Jamieson Greer, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told reporters the US has proposed a “notification mechanism” inside a new US–China AI dialogue — covering AI-related incidents that rise to a national-security level — for Presidents Trump and Xi to consider at this week’s Washington summit (Thu–Fri 24–25 Sep). Both sides agreed to meet again. Greer said US export controls on advanced AI chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment were not on the agenda for the AI-mechanism talks.

Xinhua’s readout was thinner: “frank, in-depth and constructive exchanges” on trade and investment, with AI mentioned only briefly. Handelsblatt’s Monday front carried the Meldemechanismus framing. The concrete proposal is the new fact; yesterday’s Grok had the meeting itself.

What to watch: whether Thursday’s joint statement names a channel, a ministry pair, and an incident definition; any chip-control linkage that Greer said was off the table Sunday.

Divergence — hotline vs guardrails

What is disputed. Whether an incident-notification line is meaningful cooperation or summit theatre that leaves racing, export controls and lab evaluation untouched.

Why they differ. Washington can sell a hotline as safety without conceding compute. Beijing can accept talks language without accepting inspectable limits. Greer’s carve-out of chip controls is the tell.

What it means for you. Core AI + US–China channel. Upgrades yesterday’s “they met” item into a dated deliverable to watch on Thursday.

Dig deeper — what “incident” would have to mean

Bessent’s public gloss is national-security-level AI incidents — closer to a nuclear or cyber hotline metaphor than to commercial model-card sharing. Without a shared definition (escape, critical infrastructure compromise, military misidentification), the mechanism is a phone number. Pair with the military AI near-miss from yesterday’s edition: a false boarding plan is exactly the class of event a bilateral channel would claim to handle — if either side would actually call.

DHS extends the Mass Influx Finding for 180 days — Secretary Mullin’s first such notice

US politics & policy New subject Primary source

Monday’s Federal Register publishes a DHS Finding of Mass Influx of Aliens extending the prior finding for another 180 days, effective immediately. It is signed by Markwayne Mullin, Secretary of Homeland Security — his first extension in this series. The chain of prior findings runs from Acting Secretary (23 Jan 2025) through Secretary Noem’s extensions to 17 Sep 2026; this notice continues the authority under which DHS may request state and local law-enforcement assistance in administering immigration law (8 U.S.C. 1103(a); 28 CFR 65.83).

Operative facts cited in the notice: ICE detention population 67,022 against capacity 80,700 (83% occupancy as of 12 Aug 2026); 2,120,058 aliens on the non-detained docket with final removal orders (as of 11 Aug); continued weekly Southwest-border encounter volumes; and the D.C. Circuit posture in RAICES limiting direct repatriation under the Invasion Proclamation so that DHS must still process many aliens through expedited removal. The finding requests assistance from state and local governments in all 50 states and may expire sooner if the Secretary finds circumstances changed.

What to watch: whether states accept or condition assistance; any updated CBP nationwide-encounters print that the notice cites; litigation challenging the finding’s factual predicates.

Divergence — emergency finding vs steady-state tool

What is disputed. Whether “mass influx” still describes the border given lower encounter totals than 2021–24 peaks, or whether detention, non-detained docket and court limits on the Invasion Proclamation independently justify the finding.

Why they differ. The statute and regulation turn on DHS capacity and endangerment findings, not only raw encounter counts. Critics read serial 180-day renewals as a standing mobilisation authority.

What it means for you. Primary FR notice — substance, not personnel colour. Clears the US-policy bar; not on yesterday’s Grok menu.

Dig deeper — what the finding actually authorises

It does not itself rewrite asylum law. It is the predicate for requesting state/local officers to perform immigration-officer duties with their superiors’ consent. The practical stakes are workforce surge and interior/border resource allocation — especially the notice’s explicit trade-off between Southwest-border detention beds and interior criminal-alien priorities.

A lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI and Google illegally agreed to slow AI

AI & tech industry US politics & policy New subject

AP reporting carried Monday: a complaint filed Friday in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI/xAI and Google made an illegal agreement to coordinate a slowdown in AI development, reducing the value consumers get from paid AI subscriptions. The suit points to 12 Sep — when Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published “We Must Pace the Frontier” and Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis publicly agreed in substance — and to an earlier July 2026 multi-lab staff statement acknowledging competitive pressure not to slow unilaterally and calling for government support of a global slowdown.

Amodei’s essay itself flagged antitrust risk and asked for a narrow government waiver for certain safety conversations. Altman publicly said OpenAI welcomes a federal safety framework but does not need an antitrust exemption to start the work. The Trump administration’s growth-first line (see AI Force accountability item) cuts the other way.

What to watch: motions to dismiss; whether DOJ/FTC open a parallel inquiry or file a statement of interest; any lab walk-back of public “pace” language.

Divergence — parallel speech vs agreement

What is disputed. Whether same-day CEO statements plus a prior staff open letter are concerted action under Sherman Act standards, or parallel public advocacy.

Why they differ. Antitrust plaintiffs need agreement, not merely identical hopes. Labs will argue the essay invited government mediation precisely to avoid private coordination.

What it means for you. Core AI + policy. Turns the pacing debate into a docketed case. Not a reprise of the essay itself.

Sources: The Hindu / AP · 21Alive / AP · Anthropic pacing essay context (prior issues)

Anthropic is embedding Accenture evaluators inside the lab — $1 billion each over five years

AI & tech industry Lab safety New subject Primary source

Anthropic’s own 18 Sep post — now getting Monday trade-press amplification — announces a partnership with Accenture (led by Faculty) to place embedded evaluators inside Anthropic with access “comparable to an employee’s”: red-teaming, alignment assessments, safeguard testing, and the ability to watch models through training and deployment decisions. Anthropic and Accenture each expect to invest at least $1 billion over five years. Anthropic is funding Accenture’s work directly for now; it is also in dialogue with METR and other nonprofits for separately funded pilots. The partnership is explicitly non-exclusive.

This is the operational half of Amodei’s pacing essay commitment to embed evaluators — and it lands in the same news cycle as the antitrust slowdown suit and Bessent’s bilateral incident channel. Standards for access and reporting “do not yet exist,” Anthropic says.

What to watch: first public evaluator report or incident disclosure under the arrangement; whether OpenAI or Google announce parallel embeds; any SEC/FTC attention to evaluator independence when the lab pays the bill.

Divergence — independent vs funded-by-lab

What is disputed. Whether a lab-funded embed with employee-comparable access is independence or a sophisticated internal audit branded as third-party.

Why they differ. Anthropic argues no pooled/government funding exists yet, so direct funding is urgency. Critics note the payer sets the ceiling on what gets published.

What it means for you. Standing lab-safety thread. Primary document fetched; not run in the 19–20 Sep Grok editions.
