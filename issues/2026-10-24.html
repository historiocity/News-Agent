Jet fuel has never been this expensive, and Thanksgiving fares are up 30%

Energy & grid Macro & markets Continuation Independent corroboration

This brief has reported the diesel price for six weeks and has twice failed to show where it lands on anyone outside a filling station. Here is where. The average US jet fuel price reached $4.90 a gallon on Thursday — the highest single-day average the tracking firm Argus has recorded, and more than twice what it cost a year ago.

Jet fuel and diesel come out of the same cut of the barrel. Both are middle distillates, both are made from the fraction refiners have been short of since September. The distillate squeeze this brief has been tracking through the price of diesel has been doing the same thing to kerosene, and the pass-through is now visible in a consumer price.

The numbers on fares are specific. Thanksgiving airfares are up about 30 per cent on last year and Christmas tickets more than 20 per cent, according to Hayley Berg, lead economist at the travel firm Hopper, who puts fares at a ten-year high and attributes it to fuel. Ryanair chief executive Michael O'Leary expects European fares to rise a further 10 to 20 per cent next year.

What is not happening is demand destruction, and the airlines are saying so plainly. Delta chief executive Ed Bastian told this month's earnings call that consumers have offered a "limited amount of resistance" and still find airfare "reasonably affordable". Berg's phrase for the holidays is that travel is "sticky". That is an industry describing pricing power.

On the ground, AAA this morning: diesel $6.3374, up 2.02 cents overnight and 9.8 cents on the week; regular petrol $4.2957, up 0.31. The premium is back out to $2.0417 after three days of narrowing.

What to watch: whether the next EIA weekly reading confirms diesel's renewed rise; airline hedging disclosures in the coming earnings, which decide how much of this hits 2027 fares; and whether fare increases of this size start showing up in the CPI airline fares index.

The outbound investment screen is being widened from one country to six

Trade & supply chains Tech & AI policy New subject Primary document Underreported

Since the outbound investment rules took effect last year, Americans putting money into advanced chips, quantum computing or artificial intelligence have had to check one list of jurisdictions: the People's Republic of China, including Hong Kong and Macau. A Treasury proposed rule loaded into Monday's Federal Register adds five more. Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, and Venezuela would become countries of concern alongside the PRC.

The rule implements Subtitle C of the Comprehensive Outbound Investment National Security Act, enacted as Title VIII of the Defense Production Act. It keeps the two-tier structure. Transactions with foreign persons engaged in notifiable technologies — parts of semiconductors and microelectronics, and parts of artificial intelligence — must be reported to Treasury after the fact. Transactions involving prohibited technologies — the most advanced semiconductor work, quantum information technologies, and AI systems above defined thresholds — are banned outright.

What makes this more than a list update is the statutory architecture it reveals. The country list is now set by statute rather than by executive order, which makes it harder for a future administration to shorten and harder for this one to lengthen without Congress. And Treasury is asking whether the technology categories should grow: the rule seeks comment on adding categories beyond the three the statute names, which is the department asking the public to help it argue for more authority than it was given.

One entry dates the drafting. The rule names "Venezuela under the regime of Nicolás Maduro Moros". Reporting today refers to Maduro's capture by the US military earlier this year, which this brief has not independently verified and has never covered. If that is right, a rule published Monday is screening investment against a government that no longer exists, and the conditional clause may be doing the work of switching the designation off.

What to watch: whether comments challenge the Venezuela entry on exactly that ground; whether Treasury's request to expand the technology categories draws industry opposition; and whether the Cuba designation interacts with the policy in the fifth story below. Comments close 7 December 2026.

A rule about helping you stop robocalls lets the caller decide how you say stop

Regulatory New subject Primary document Underreported

The Federal Communications Commission has issued a final rule titled "Enhancing the Ability of Consumers To Control the Robocalls and Robotexts They Receive". Read the summary rather than the title and it does three things, and at least two of them narrow what a consumer can do.

First, a caller may now treat a revocation of consent as applying only to the category of message it was directed at. Under the rule as it stood, telling a company to stop ended consent for its informational robocalls generally. Under this one, a consumer who replies STOP to a delivery notification can be read as having revoked consent to delivery notifications and nothing else, leaving appointment reminders and account alerts running.

Second, a caller may designate an exclusive means of revoking consent. If the company says revocation happens through a web portal, replying STOP to the text is no longer sufficient. The burden of knowing the right channel moves from the sender to the recipient.

Third, the rule amends the exemption for financial institutions, the category that generates a large share of the calls consumers describe as unwanted but that are legally not robocalls at all.

This brief has made the observation three times this month and will make it again: the title of a rule is written by the agency and the summary is written by the lawyers. A CBP rule titled "Indefinite Suspension of the De Minimis Exemption" turned out to extend a compliance date. This one is named for consumer control and hands the caller two new ways to limit it. Effective 25 November 2026.

What to watch: whether state attorneys general treat an exclusive-means designation as an unfair practice under state law; whether the category-specific revocation reading survives the first challenge; and the volume of complaints in the first quarter after 25 November.

Confidence: Primary document. Document 2026-21821, scheduled for Monday 26 October and not yet published, read early from GovInfo. The summary and effective date were read; the full rule, including the Commission's reasoning and any dissent, was not. The characterisation of what each change does is this brief's reading of the summary text. Sources: FR 2026-21821 · 22 October on reading the summary, not the title

A vaccine trial has begun in the outbreak's epicentre, and it is testing the wrong strain on purpose

Health Continuation Single-sourced

Correction. The 21 October issue called Nigeria "the fifth country" reached by this outbreak. It is the fourth. See the note at the top of this issue.

The World Health Organization's director-general, Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, announced on X that the first participants have been vaccinated in Bunia, in Ituri province — the centre of the Democratic Republic of the Congo's seventeenth and worst recorded Ebola outbreak.

The scientifically interesting part is what is in the syringe. The vaccine being given is Ervebo, which is licensed against the Zaire strain of Ebola. The strain circulating is Bundibugyo. Ervebo is not approved against it and is being given on the hypothesis that it offers cross-protection — which the trial is designed to test. The trial, called Tumaini, is a single framework able to evaluate several vaccines at once by ring vaccination, immunising the contacts of confirmed cases and the contacts of those contacts, and it is structured to bring in two Bundibugyo-specific candidates as they become available.

That design is an admission as much as an innovation. There is no licensed vaccine for the strain actually killing people, so the response is deploying the vaccine that exists while building the apparatus to test the ones that do not. Ring vaccination is also the method that worked against smallpox and in the 2015 Guinea Ebola trial, which means the framework is sound even where the product is uncertain.

The scale: more than 9,700 cases and over 4,600 deaths. The outbreak has reached four countries — the DRC, Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria — the widest geographic spread of any Ebola outbreak in Africa. Kenya has announced a second death.

What to watch: enrolment numbers from Bunia, which decide whether the trial can produce an answer at all; when the Bundibugyo-specific candidates enter the framework; and whether Nigeria's case count moves past the index case.

Confidence: Single-sourced. All of it comes from one Al Jazeera report, which is itself relaying Tedros's post on X; no WHO situation report, trial registration or protocol was reached, and the 9,700 and 4,600 figures are the agency's running totals rather than a reconciled count. Given this brief got the country count wrong once already, the four-country figure here is WHO's own framing as reported today. Sources: Al Jazeera · 21 October (contains the corrected error)

The Secretary of State has publicly asked Cubans to work with the CIA

Foreign policy New subject Single-sourced

Secretary of State Marco Rubio wrote on X: "To those who seek to create positive change in Cuba – now is the time," and encouraged Cubans to "seize this opportunity to create the change you desire through a discreet partnership with the CIA."

The content of the appeal is not new — the Central Intelligence Agency has run Cuba operations for sixty-odd years, and a sitting Secretary of State favouring regime change in Havana is the least surprising fact in American foreign policy. What is new is the channel. Intelligence recruitment is conducted privately because the people being recruited can be prosecuted, and because an agency that advertises undermines the deniability that protects its sources. An open call from the cabinet officer who runs diplomacy, rather than the one who runs intelligence, collapses the distinction the two departments are organised around.

Cuba's response was to treat it as a loss of face rather than a threat. Deputy Foreign Minister Carlos Fernández de Cossío called it "public recruitment for the CIA" and added: "There must be a great deal of desperation or misery within the agency for it to be forced to sell its services like a tube of toothpaste." That is a government choosing ridicule over alarm, which is itself a signal about how much pressure it thinks it is under.

There is also a question of who bears the risk. Under Cuban law, cooperating with a foreign intelligence service is prosecutable, and the people most likely to read an invitation on X are the ones already visible to the state. An open call transfers the hazard of the first approach from the officer making it to the civilian answering it, and does so in a medium the Cuban security services can read as easily as anyone else.

Divergence. The two readings are about what the post is for. Read one: it is operational. The administration believes conditions in Cuba are unstable enough that volunteers exist, and is lowering the cost of first contact for people who would never approach an embassy. Read two: it is signalling. The post is aimed at Havana and at Florida, not at recruits — a way to tell the Cuban government it is being worked on and to tell a domestic constituency the administration is doing something.

Why the difference matters. Under read one, the next thing to appear is enforcement inside Cuba: arrests, a counter-intelligence campaign, expulsions. Under read two, the next thing is nothing, and the post is complete in itself. The cause of the divergence is that this brief cannot see the thing that would settle it — whether any American agency has capability in place to receive the contacts the post invites. How to read the story: treat it as a statement of intent whose seriousness is unknown, and watch Cuban domestic security, not American rhetoric, for the answer.

What to watch: whether the CIA or the White House says anything, which would distinguish a coordinated policy from a Secretary posting; Cuban arrests or state-security announcements in the next fortnight; and whether the Cuba designation in the outbound investment rule above turns out to be part of the same posture.

Confidence: Single-sourced. One Al Jazeera report; Rubio's post was not read directly on X and the State Department website was not reached, so the quotation is as the outlet rendered it. No American official comment beyond the post, and no CIA response, was found. Sources: Al Jazeera · 20 October on Florida and Cuba policy

Geopolitics The Pentagon has designated Confucius Institutes a national security threat, and what that does is the part not available.

The Department of Defense has formally designated the Confucius Institutes — the Chinese government-funded language and culture centres hosted on university campuses — as a national security threat. The programme has been shrinking in the United States for years under funding restrictions attached to defence authorisation acts, and most of the large campus institutes closed some time ago, so the designation lands on a much smaller footprint than the one that provoked the original fight.

What the designation does is the whole story, and it is the part not available. A threat designation can be purely declaratory, or it can trigger contracting restrictions, security-clearance consequences for affiliated researchers, or reporting obligations on host institutions. Which of those apply decides whether this is an epitaph for a programme already gone or a new restriction on the universities that still host one.

Below the line because the designation document was not reached — only the fact of it — and a story whose significance turns entirely on a legal consequence this brief cannot name is a headline, not a lead.

Confidence: Single-sourced. Sources: NPR
