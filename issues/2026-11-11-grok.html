Core inflation rose 0.3 per cent for a third month, the line Waller said would justify a half-point hike

Macro & markets Continues: Waller, 10 Nov Primary source

The October consumer price index, published at 8:30 a.m. ET yesterday after this brief's Tuesday edition had closed, rose 0.4 per cent on a seasonally adjusted basis, after 0.6 per cent in September. Core prices, excluding food and energy, rose 0.3 per cent for the third month running. Hours earlier Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said that a core reading like the previous two months should put a 50 basis-point increase on the table at the 8–9 December meeting.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics release shows where it came from. Shelter rose 0.3 per cent and supplied more than 30 per cent of the monthly increase. Food rose 0.5 per cent after a flat September. Energy rose only 0.7 per cent after 5.2, and gasoline fell 0.7 per cent before seasonal adjustment. Recreation rose 0.8 per cent, hospital services 0.8, motor-vehicle insurance 0.7; used cars fell 1.2. BLS again published no twelve-month rate, because October 2025 prices were never collected during the shutdown.

The bond market moved on it. Treasury's own par yield curve shows the two-year at 4.73 per cent on Tuesday, from 4.56 on Monday, the ten-year at 4.98 from 4.88, and the thirty-year at 5.35. CNBC, citing CME FedWatch, says futures now fully price a quarter-point hike in December and give about 12 per cent to a half point. MarketWatch reports Goldman Sachs has added a further hike in March. Higher yields feed straight into debt service: Treasury's Debt to the Penny stood at $40.560 trillion on 9 November.

What to watch: this was the last CPI before the meeting — November's is scheduled for 10 December, the day after the decision. No Treasury curve prints today: SIFMA recommended a full bond-market close for Veterans Day.

Divergence — was the ten-year "back at 5%", and is core inflation 3.75 per cent?

What is disputed. Two facts disputes. CNBC's Investing Club wrote that the ten-year yield "surged back to 5%" after the print; Treasury's par curve for Tuesday says 4.98. MarketWatch wrote that October's core CPI hit 3.75 per cent; BLS published no year-on-year core rate at all for October.

Why they differ. The mundane explanation covers both. A vendor screen records intraday highs; Treasury's par curve is an end-of-day fitted series, and this brief has been burned before by printing a vendor's 5 per cent that the series of record never showed. The 3.75 figure matches what you get by compounding three 0.3 per cent months into an annual rate — roughly 3.7 per cent — which is a three-month annualised pace, not the year-on-year number readers will assume. That is my reconstruction; MarketWatch's visible text does not say how it was computed.

What it means for you. Use 4.98 per cent as the ten-year level and treat "through 5%" as an intraday event at most. For inflation, the official statement is three straight 0.3 per cent core months; any annual core figure for October is somebody's estimate. It is settled for the ten-year when Thursday's curve prints, and for core only when BLS resumes twelve-month rates.

Divergence — did this print make a half-point hike likely, or only possible?

What is disputed. Significance, not the number. American Banker says the report puts the Fed on track to hike and that Waller "could support a bigger increase". CBS quotes an analyst saying it "most likely seals the deal" for December. Futures, per CNBC, give a half point only about 12 per cent.

Why they differ. They are answering different questions. The press is asking whether the Fed hikes at all — on which everyone now agrees. The market is pricing the size, and Waller's condition was a core print "around" 0.3, which a rounded 0.3 meets without exceeding. One governor's conditional is also not a committee: two members dissented for a hike in October, per American Banker, and that is the size of the hawkish bloc on the record.

What it means for you. Expect a December hike. Treat 50 basis points as live but not the base case until more voters than Waller say it out loud. The tell will be speeches between now and the pre-meeting blackout; the answer is the 9 December statement.

Dig deeper — why the composition matters more than the headline

The September print was an energy story: energy up 5.2 per cent, gasoline up 9.1 per cent seasonally adjusted. October is the opposite. Gasoline fell before seasonal adjustment and energy contributed little, yet the headline still rose 0.4 per cent. That is what a broadening looks like: food up 0.5, with beef up 0.9 and snacks up 1.0; services such as hospitals and car insurance rising faster than the overall index; shelter steady at 0.3. Vanguard, quoted by CBS, calls it a "longer-lasting" pass-through of the energy shock into core and has raised its year-end core forecast to 3.2 per cent.

This links to the Fed staff note Waller cited on Tuesday, which found that oil shocks now feed into core prices more persistently than before 2020. Yesterday's numbers are the first test of that claim since the note appeared, and they fit it: the energy line cooled sharply while core did not.

The seasonal-adjustment detail matters for the pump story below. Unadjusted gasoline prices fell in October, but less than they usually do in October, so the adjusted index rose 1.0 per cent. November will look different: AAA's average has fallen about 41 cents in a month. That will pull the November headline down. It will not reach core, and it arrives the day after the Fed decides.

On the fiscal side, the two-year moving 17 basis points in a day is the number to hold. Treasury refinances a large share of the debt at the short end, so a repricing of the hiking path lands in interest costs within months, not years.

Home-equity borrowing jumped by the most since 2005, and 5 per cent of household debt is now delinquent

US financials New subject Primary source

The New York Fed's quarterly household debt report, released Tuesday, puts total household debt at $19.1 trillion at the end of September, up $334 billion or 1.8 per cent in the third quarter. The line that stands out is home equity: HELOC balances rose $23 billion to $481 billion, which the bank says is the largest quarterly increase since 2005. HELOC limits rose another $31 billion, extending an expansion that began in 2022.

Mortgage balances rose $257 billion to $13.4 trillion, on $595 billion of new originations. American Banker reports that about half the mortgage increase reflects balances re-reported after a gap in the previous quarter, so the true mortgage growth is smaller than the headline. Card balances rose $22 billion to $1.29 trillion, a record in the series; auto loans rose $21 billion to $1.73 trillion; student loans rose $5 billion to $1.66 trillion. Aggregate card limits rose $115 billion.

Credit quality moved the other way. The share of all household debt in some stage of delinquency rose 0.3 percentage points to 5.0 per cent. New early delinquencies kept rising for auto loans and mortgages and eased slightly for cards. The flow into serious delinquency worsened for mortgages, though the bank calls it low by historical standards.

Why it matters for this beat: households are borrowing against their houses rather than refinancing them, at a moment when the rate path just moved up. HELOCs are typically variable-rate lines, so a December hike would reach those borrowers quickly — the same households, the same lenders, a quarter later.

What to watch: fourth-quarter bank earnings for HELOC growth and card charge-offs, and whether the auto and mortgage delinquency rise survives a hike.

Divergence — a "Goldilocks" quarter or a worsening one?

What is disputed. Mostly significance, with one facts point. American Banker's headline is that card balances rose but growth slowed, and it quotes an analyst calling the numbers "Goldilocks numbers". CNBC's headline is record card debt. The New York Fed's own release leads on moderate growth but says aggregate delinquency worsened. The facts point: American Banker describes the card figure of about 12.9 per cent as the share of borrowers 90 days late; the New York Fed's serious-delinquency series are measured as shares of balances.

Why they differ. Vantage. A banking trade paper writing for card issuers looks at the flow into delinquency on cards, which improved. A general business outlet leads with the record level, which is mechanical in a growing economy. The New York Fed's research staff report every line, and the line that moved most was housing debt, which neither headline led on.

What it means for you. Both headlines are accurate about cards, and cards are not the story. The evidence supports reading this as a housing-equity quarter with deteriorating credit at the margin: delinquency up 0.3 points, mortgage and auto early delinquencies rising, HELOC balances growing by the most since 2005. Take the 12.9 per cent as a balance-based card figure until the bank's own chart says otherwise. The fourth-quarter report in February will show whether a rate hike turned the HELOC boom into the next delinquency line.

HUD has finalised the rule ending housing aid for mixed-status families; about 20,000 families, two-thirds of their eligible members children

US politics & policy New subject Primary source

The Department of Housing and Urban Development has filed its final "Verification of Eligible Status" rule for publication in tomorrow's Federal Register (document 2026-23003), and it takes effect on 14 December. Secretary Scott Turner announced it on Tuesday evening. It covers public housing, Housing Choice Vouchers, project-based Section 8 and below-market Section 236 units.

The rule does two things. Every household member, of any age, must now prove US citizenship or eligible immigration status; today a member can simply decline to claim status, and a citizen can sign a declaration. And "prorated" assistance — where a family keeps a subsidy cut to cover only its eligible members — becomes temporary, pending verification of everyone. In practice that ends assistance for households that include an ineligible member. Families can get extensions of up to 60 days to produce documents, and housing agencies may defer termination for up to 18 months.

HUD's own regulatory impact analysis, quoted in the preamble, sizes it. About 20,000 mixed-status families received assistance as of December 2024. Children under 18 are roughly 65 per cent of the eligible members of those families. About 16,000 of the families have children, and HUD expects them to leave together rather than split up. HUD assumes at most about 990 households will contest and be formally evicted. HUD also concedes that, because replacement households need larger subsidies, fewer households will be served unless Congress appropriates more.

The preamble says about 1.05 per cent of existing tenants are estimated to lack ready access to documents. They are mostly citizens, and they face the new paperwork too.

What to watch: litigation before 14 December; how many agencies use the 18-month deferral; and whether the incoming Democratic House attaches an appropriations rider. Housing advocates such as the National Housing Law Project have said they will fight it.

Divergence — 200,000 tenants, 80,000 people or 20,000 families?

What is disputed. Partly facts, mostly framing. HUD's press release says a HUD–DHS audit found "nearly 200,000 tenants" with incomplete or unknown eligibility verification. HUD's own impact analysis says the rule's direct target is about 20,000 mixed-status families. Commenters quoted in the preamble, and advocates since, put the people in those families at nearly 80,000.

Why they differ. Each figure counts something different, and each source picks the one that serves its case. The 200,000 is tenants whose files are incomplete, which includes citizens with missing paperwork; it is not a count of ineligible people. The 20,000 is households with at least one member who is ineligible or has not claimed status. The 80,000 counts everyone in those households, most of whom are eligible — commenters reading HUD's analysis count about 55,100 eligible people — and HUD says two-thirds of the eligible members are children. The press release uses the biggest number; advocates use the people count; the analysis uses households.

What it means for you. The rule removes assistance from about 20,000 households. Most people in them are eligible. HUD's own document says the benefit is reprioritising aid, not saving money, and that fewer households will be served without new appropriations. Read the 200,000 as a measure of paperwork gaps, not of ineligibility. The first real count will come from agencies' termination notices after 14 December.

Dig deeper — what the preamble concedes, and where the litigation will aim

The statutory hook is Section 214 of the Housing and Community Development Act of 1980, which bars HUD from assisting people other than citizens and specified categories of noncitizens. Under current regulations a family with an ineligible member can keep a subsidy reduced in proportion, indefinitely. HUD now argues that proration was meant to be a temporary bridge and that letting it run indefinitely misreads the statute. That is the legal question a court will be asked: whether prorated assistance, which HUD itself says the statute preserves in some cases, can be made temporary by regulation.

The preamble is unusually candid on cost. Because eligible members of mixed families have higher average incomes and pay more of their own rent, replacing them with fully eligible families needs a larger subsidy per person. HUD says plainly that, absent more money, fewer households would be assisted, and that the rule's benefit is reprioritisation under Section 214 rather than savings. Commenters drew on the analysis to say the number of eligible people assisted in those households falls from about 55,100 to 24,100, and that HUD would need $311–385 million more to serve the same number of households. Those are commenters' readings of HUD's analysis, quoted in HUD's own preamble; HUD does not dispute the direction.

On homelessness, HUD calls the net effect "ambiguous": displaced mixed-status families may become homeless, offset by fully eligible households, some formerly homeless, who get the freed units. HUD puts upfront implementation costs at $17–33 million.

Two points shape the litigation. First, the rule applies to current tenants, not only applicants, which raises reliance and notice arguments. Second, the 18-month deferral is at HUD's discretion and, by the rule's own terms, available only where the statute permits. Expect challenges filed in districts with the largest concentrations — commenters named California and Massachusetts — before 14 December.

The FCC's repeal of the 39 per cent TV ownership cap publishes tomorrow and takes effect 11 January

US politics & policy New subject Primary source

The Federal Communications Commission's order abolishing the national television ownership cap has been filed for publication in tomorrow's Federal Register (document 2026-23040). The repeal of 47 CFR 73.3555(e) takes effect on 11 January 2027. The commission voted on 6 August and released the text on 1 October; publication is the step that starts the effective-date clock and, per Communications Daily, opens the door to the legal challenges everyone expects.

The cap bars any one company from owning stations that reach more than 39 per cent of US television households — up to 78 per cent in practice, because a UHF station counts for only half its market's households. In its place the commission will review, case by case, any deal that would have broken the cap, against its goals of localism, competition and viewpoint diversity. The order says some such deals may be approved and some denied, and calls the change a shift from a blanket ban to individual review.

Its legal theory is that Congress, in the 2004 appropriations act, only told the commission to "modify its rules" to set 39 per cent, and that the power to modify includes the power to change them again. The same 2004 law removed the cap from the four-yearly ownership review.

The politics moved the same day. At a Senate Commerce hearing on Tuesday, Democrats opposed Danielle Thumann Severs, the President's nominee for a vacant seat and a senior counsel to Chairman Brendan Carr. Roll Call and Ars Technica report Democrats fear a three-Republican quorum would let the President remove the sole Democrat, Anna Gomez, and they promised to investigate Carr from January.

What to watch: petitions for review after publication, which circuit they land in, and the first station deal filed above 39 per cent.

Divergence — a rule the FCC may repeal, or a limit Congress set?

What is disputed. A legal-significance dispute about the same history. Radio & Television Business Report headlines the move as erasing a "Congressionally-Mandated" cap. The FCC's own order says the 2004 law only instructed it to modify its rules and that the commission "has always retained authority" to change the cap.

Why they differ. Both describe the same 2004 statute. It did set the 39 per cent figure, by directing the commission to write it into its rules, and it shielded the figure from periodic review. Whether that makes 39 per cent a statutory floor or merely a starting point is exactly what will be litigated. The FCC leans on a D.C. Circuit line describing the earlier 35 per cent cap as "the starting point"; the trade press, writing for broadcasters who will buy and sell under the new regime, reaches for the shorthand its readers use.

What it means for you. The FCC's reading of "modify its rules" is plausible on the text, and it asserted the same authority in 2016. But Congress removing the cap from routine review is the strongest point against it: it suggests Congress meant the number to stay put. Treat the repeal as effective from 11 January but legally exposed. A court ruling, or a rider from the incoming Congress restoring the cap, would settle it.

Gasoline is down 18 cents in a week on peace talk, but tankers are still being hit and Gulf freight has barely moved

Energy & oil Continues: Iran–oil–pump chain, 10 Nov Independent corroboration

The pump. AAA's national average for regular is $3.943 this morning, down from $3.979 yesterday, $4.124 a week ago and $4.359 a month ago; a year ago it was $3.072. Diesel is $6.131. The EIA's Monday survey agrees on direction: regular $3.995, down 15.8 cents on the week, and diesel $6.127, down 16.5 cents.

The barrel. Brent fell to its lowest since August on Tuesday, briefly below $90 according to OilPrice.com, before bouncing to about $93.25 this morning on Trading Economics' contract-for-difference series. That is 12.6 per cent down on the month. The American Petroleum Institute estimated a 9.0-million-barrel US crude build last week and a further release from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, leaving it at 279.5 million barrels. The government's own count, normally out today, moves to noon tomorrow because of the holiday.

The chokepoint. President Trump said on Monday he saw "positive momentum" toward ending the war, and Secretary of State Rubio, in Paris for the G7, said strikes were not needed while the economic campaign worked. Iran's security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, called reports of talks in Qatar "a pure lie" and said Hormuz stays shut until Washington meets Iran's seven conditions, which include lifting sanctions and the US naval blockade. On Tuesday a vessel operated by ADNOC Logistics was damaged in an attack in UAE waters, and UKMTO reported a tanker hit by a projectile 19 nautical miles north-east of Lima, Oman. Iraq says it has a US understanding to export through a lane near Oman and shipped about 3.5 million barrels a day in October.

The chain. The barrel is pricing a settlement; the strait is not yet open. A VLCC from the Gulf to China still costs $72–74 million, $36–37 a barrel, barely changed in two weeks, and roughly $20 a barrel more than loading just outside the strait.

What to watch: the EIA weekly at noon tomorrow; any US reply to the seven conditions; and where the first Russian diesel cargo bound for the US, now stopped off the coast, ends up; the President's energy chief says it will eventually be sold on the global market, per Reuters via gCaptain.

Divergence — is the war "effectively over" for oil?

What is disputed. An interpretation dispute between two markets. Crude futures have fallen to a three-month low on expectations of a deal. Freight markets for the same route have hardly moved, and attacks continue: OilPrice.com counts at least 16 tankers struck in the strait this month, only 11 of them reported by UKMTO; Trading Economics counted 37 vessels attacked in October.

Why they differ. Each prices a different horizon. Futures trade the expected supply over coming months, and so price the chance of a deal. A tanker charter prices the voyage now, through a strait that is still being hit, with crew, insurance and ship-time costs. Rezaei's denial is also aimed at that gap: he says Washington is talking up negotiations to bring oil prices down.

What it means for you. Pump prices should keep easing for a while, because retail follows the barrel with a lag. But the fall rests on talks that one side says are not happening. Freight is the better real-time sign: if Gulf charter rates fall toward other routes, the strait is genuinely reopening. If talks fail, the barrel has further to snap back than the freight does.

OpenAI built a 35-second kill switch for its research models, as New York opened registration for its AI safety law

AI lab safety New subject Independent corroboration

OpenAI said on Tuesday that it has deployed "Control Room", a central system that tracks every model workload across its research infrastructure and can stop one when safety or security concerns arise. Once a job is stopped, further output from the model is denied within at most 35 seconds; the company says the lag comes from a caching layer for authorisation, and that it is working to bring it to zero. The system fails closed: if Control Room itself goes down, model output stops. Every research workload doing agentic inference must be tied to an approved "safety case" before it gets a job ID. OpenAI says its public model-serving stack already had a well-developed ability to take deployments down.

The same day, New York's Department of Financial Services opened its RAISE Act portal. The law's obligations start on 1 January 2027. Any developer that has trained a model with more than 1026 operations must report critical safety incidents within 72 hours, or within 24 hours to authorities if there is an imminent risk of death or serious injury. "Large" developers, those with more than $500 million in annual revenue, must also register, publish a safety framework, and give the state a quarterly summary of catastrophic risk from internal use of their models. That clause reaches exactly the research-lab use Control Room governs.

The two are not formally linked. But together they mark a shift: lab safety moving from model release decisions to the labs' own internal use of agents, where The Register notes recent incidents of agents exceeding their brief. The Register also observes OpenAI has not said why it built the switch now.

What to watch: which labs register with New York before 1 January; whether others publish comparable stop mechanisms; and the first incident report filed under the law.

Divergence — has New York's AI safety law "taken effect"?

What is disputed. Timing. TechCrunch headlined Tuesday's news as OpenAI and Anthropic facing new oversight "as New York's AI safety law takes effect". The regulator's own page says registration and the reporting regime apply "beginning January 1, 2027".

Why they differ. What started on Tuesday is the portal: developers can now open accounts and are encouraged to do so early. The duties themselves — registration, incident reports, quarterly risk summaries — begin in seven weeks. The headline treats the administrative launch as the legal start.

What it means for you. No lab is yet obliged to report anything to New York. The first real test of the law is whether the large labs register by 1 January, and whether any company argues it falls outside the definitions. Until then, OpenAI's Control Room is a voluntary commitment described only by OpenAI.

A regional panel is deciding this week whether the US has lost its measles-elimination status, held since 2000

US health policy New subject Independent corroboration

The Pan American Health Organization's independent verification commission is meeting in Washington from 10 to 13 November and will decide whether endemic measles transmission has been re-established in the United States. The test is uninterrupted circulation of the same virus genotype and lineage for 12 months or more. For the US, PAHO set the analysis period to run from 20 January 2025, the onset of the reported outbreaks. The commission recommends; PAHO's director, Jarbas Barbosa, decides. A PAHO spokesperson told Reuters the determination is expected to be made public early next week.

The numbers going in are the worst in a generation. The CDC counted 5,182 cases this year as of 5 November, the highest annual tally since 1991, per the Associated Press; 96 per cent were unvaccinated or of unknown status. Pennsylvania alone has 1,505, followed by South Carolina and Utah. Mexico and Bolivia are also on PAHO's watch list. Canada lost its status a year ago, which cost the Americas region its measles-free label.

Noel Brewer, a University of North Carolina professor who chairs an independent US advisory panel on measles, presented the CDC's status report to the commission on Tuesday. He would not give details, but told Reuters that whatever the ruling, "we have a huge problem with measles."

This story was not on the watchlist; the negative check found it.

What to watch: the PAHO announcement early next week; whether Mexico's status falls with it; and any change in federal vaccine guidance in response.

Divergence — whose failure, and does the label matter?

Florida: regulators unanimously rejected Duke Energy's data-centre rate plan in the first test of the new cost law

Florida New subject (late: vote was 3 Nov) Independent corroboration

Late catch. This vote took place on Tuesday 3 November. No Grok edition reported it until today; it surfaced in Tuesday's Tampa Bay Times follow-up.

The Florida Public Service Commission voted 5–0 to deny Duke Energy Florida's proposed "large load" tariff for data centres, the first test of SB 484, the law Gov. Ron DeSantis signed this spring and which took effect on 1 July. The law requires regulators to "reasonably ensure" each large customer "bears its own full cost of service" and that the cost is not shifted to everyone else.

Commissioners found two problems. Duke's tariff excluded some large customers who meet the law's definition, and Duke failed to show its existing rates and contract terms would make data centres pay their full cost. Duke had largely proposed to treat data centres like other big industrial users, a class the Times notes is already subsidised by residents and small businesses, and said it would bring a data-centre-specific structure in 2027 alongside its next base-rate request.

The chair, Gabriella Passidomo Smith, said she understood Duke's argument that data centres could lower bills "in theory", but "that theoretical scenario does not address the statute we have before us." The commission's staff had recommended denial. All five commissioners are DeSantis appointees.

For Duke customers across much of Pasco and Pinellas, the vote means no data-centre cost structure is yet approved. Duke can refile; it would not say when. Tampa Electric's own proposal is still awaiting review.

What to watch: Duke's refiling; the Tampa Electric decision; and whether the cost figures, redacted at Duke's request, become public.

Divergence — how much would residents have paid?

What is disputed. Magnitude, and it cannot be checked. Consumer advocates said Duke's plan could saddle Floridians with billions of dollars of bill increases. Duke says it is focused on "ensuring that data centers pay their full cost of service" and argued data centres could lower bills.

Why they differ. The exact figure of potential costs was redacted from the public record at Duke's request, per the Tampa Bay Times. So neither side's number can be tested against the filing, and the commission ruled on the plan's structure, not on a dollar amount.

What it means for you. The ruling protects bills for now by refusing a structure, not by finding a number. The real figure will matter when Duke refiles and in its 2027 rate case; ask that the redaction be lifted then.

Sources: Tampa Bay Times · WUSF · Bay News 9 · Florida PSC news release
