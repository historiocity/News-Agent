Correction to yesterday’s lead: the President now says the US will not attack Iran before the 3 November midterms, while the blockade stays and a third carrier heads to the region

Geopolitics Correction New fact

Yesterday this edition led on Axios’s report that the Pentagon had told CENTCOM to finish preparing to resume major combat, possibly before the midterms. On Thursday the President wrote on Truth Social: “we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections,” adding that the two sides are having “productive discussions.” That timing frame no longer stands. The preparation itself was not denied, and Axios notes that his past statements on Iran have not always matched what he did: in February he said he had not decided less than 24 hours before the war began.

What stays: the naval blockade “will remain in full force and effect”. AP reported last week that about 9,000 sailors and Marines and a third aircraft carrier are heading to the region, which could put three US carriers in the Middle East by late October. Iran’s foreign minister Abbas Araghchi says Tehran is reviewing a US proposal and will answer “in the next few days” (Tasnim, via Deutsche Bank’s morning note). The IDF chief was briefed on Tuesday on US preparations for operations “within the next three weeks”, and said that could force Israel to postpone its 27 October election.

The region did not pause. Houthi strikes on Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport on Thursday killed three Saudi nationals, including a Saudia pilot. Hundreds of flights were cancelled, and the Saudi-led coalition promises a “firm response”. Reuters reports Iran has told Gulf states that any new oil-export route it has not authorised will be treated as hostile, and a chemical tanker, the Acers, was hit by projectiles 51 nautical miles off Qatar, inside the Gulf. Public support for the war was 31% in an August Reuters/Ipsos poll.

What to watch: Iran’s written reply; whether the carrier build-up continues on schedule after the pledge; and whether Riyadh’s “firm response” draws the US in.

Iran lead — right weight after yesterday’s frame?

OpenAI tells investors it is at about $50bn of annualised revenue, not the $68bn widely reported, and the AI trade has its worst day since mid-August; an Nvidia-backed $5bn data-centre IPO is pulled

AI & tech US financials New fact

CNBC confirmed an FT report: in an investor presentation, OpenAI put its annualised revenue at roughly $50bn at the end of September. Last month’s widely reported figure was $68bn. A person familiar with it says the bigger number was built by OpenAI’s own investors to include partners’ gross revenue, the way Anthropic counts cloud resale. OpenAI also cited 77% run-rate growth in the third quarter and 107% for enterprise. The Guardian describes the $50bn as this year’s revenue forecast rather than a run-rate; CNBC’s wording is the one used here.

The market reaction was sharp. On Thursday the Nasdaq fell 1.4%, its biggest one-day fall since mid-August. Nvidia fell 3%, Oracle nearly 6%, CoreWeave nearly 8%, and AMD, Broadcom, Intel and Super Micro 4–5%. Vital Knowledge’s Adam Crisafulli said the gross-versus-net point is not the main issue. In his view the main issues are that “standalone frontier labs are increasingly unattractive businesses” and that markets are pushing back on “the tidal wave of AI-linked debt and equity”. Futures were rebounding before Friday’s open, with Nasdaq-100 futures up 0.8%.

The financing side is wobbling too. Australia’s Firmus, an Nvidia-, Blackstone- and Coatue-backed AI data-centre operator that signed a Meta compute deal last month, withdrew a planned $5bn IPO at a valuation of about $30.6bn. It would have been Australia’s second-largest float, and the company cited “market volatility”. On the retail side, Robinhood’s margin book hit a record $21.6bn in the second quarter, up 127% on a year earlier. OpenAI is still in early talks to raise about $30bn. Its last round, in March, valued it at $852bn; the Guardian puts the new round’s valuation at about $1.4tn.

Why it matters: the AI build-out is now a credit story as much as an equity one (yesterday’s SpaceX/Apollo GPU-backed debt). Lab revenue is the cash flow that is meant to service it. A 26% gap between the reported and the presented figure is the kind of number that reprices that debt.

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Follow-up

Claims have been under 200,000 for a month, a stretch Navy Federal’s Heather Long says “hasn’t been seen since the 1960s”. Challenger counted announced job cuts down 20% on a year earlier in September. Employers are not firing; whether they are hiring is the question September’s +29k payrolls raised (Issue 29-grok).

Treasury counsellor David Zervos told CNBC that real yields are “really, really high by any historic standard” and have “room to come down” once the energy shock ends. He also called AI “SI”. Rates lead — right altitude?

One oil chain: Isaias shuts in 63% of Gulf of Mexico oil, supertanker rates hit records as Saudi Arabia formalises Hormuz shuttles, and pump gasoline turns up while diesel falls for a 17th day; Delta cuts its year by a quarter

Energy & chokepoints Macro & markets Follow-up

Supply. The offshore regulator reported on Thursday that 62.9% of Gulf of Mexico oil output (1.28m b/d) and 57.4% of gas (1.13 bcf/d) are shut in, up from about 25% on Wednesday. 121 of 371 manned platforms have been evacuated, and Shell has shut Mars, Olympus, Ursa, Vito and Appomattox. Undamaged facilities usually restart quickly after inspection. Brent rose more than 4% to above $104 on Thursday, then slipped to $102.94 early Friday after the no-strike pledge, with WTI at $90.51.

Freight. Gulf-to-East Asia VLCC rates reached an all-time high of nearly $1.4m a day on Wednesday, up about 540% since before the war, compared with roughly 40% for Brent. A US Gulf-to-Japan supertanker was offered at a possible record $82m, more than $40 a barrel. Yesterday’s US-to-China charter was $76m. Signal Ocean says more than 40% of the world’s roughly 850 VLCCs are in or near the Gulf, tied up by ship-to-ship shuttling. Bloomberg reports Saudi Aramco is negotiating to put loadings outside Hormuz into its long-term contracts for next year, with talks to conclude by year end. That would turn the wartime workaround into the default.

Pressure on Iran. Treasury designated 17 tankers and State five more (22 in all) under “Operation Economic Outcast”. Treasury says the action neutralises most of the remaining shadow fleet; trackers say many Iran-linked tankers remain unsanctioned. Kpler says Iran has loaded no crude for export since 25 August.

Pump and P&L. AAA this morning: regular $4.3718, up 1.1¢ overnight (still below last week’s $4.3961). Diesel is $6.2785, its 17th straight daily fall. The President’s diesel order is published in today’s Federal Register as EO 14435. Kalshi traders give 87% odds that gasoline is still above $4 on Election Day. Delta missed for the first time in two years and cut 2026 adjusted EPS guidance to $5.10–5.60 from $6.50–7.50, citing a $6bn rise in fuel costs this year, though it says demand is holding.

Oil chain — still one story?

Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say in an open letter that they were let go for “prioritising safety”; OpenAI cites a “significant breach of trust”, and Axios reports that lab executives are planning for public revolt after a first AI catastrophe

Lab safety AI & tech New fact

Mikita Balesni, Tomek Korbak and Jasmine Wang, safety and alignment staff fired last week, wrote on Thursday to OpenAI’s Safety and Security Committee, Safety Advisory Group and Mission Advisory Council. They say their dismissals have made colleagues “afraid to speak” and that well-defined procedures for working with outside evaluators are “itself an essential safety mechanism”. They deny leaking to The Information about less monitorable architectures in OpenAI’s newest models. Korbak says he had spent months warning “we’re losing the ability to monitor what AI agents think”.

OpenAI’s research leaders replied on Friday that “these decisions were not about raising safety concerns”. They say an investigation found “a significant breach of trust beyond what’s outlined in the letter”, and a spokesperson cited a “pattern of misconduct”. OpenAI did not say which policies were broken. It says it agrees on “preserving the monitorability of frontier models” and is finalising contracts with third-party safety assessors, with details in the coming weeks.

Axios scoop: executives at Anthropic, OpenAI and other labs are privately gaming out a political backlash after a catastrophic AI event, most likely a cyberattack that takes down financial services, connectivity, power or water. Many insiders told Axios they expect a major event within six to 12 months. OpenAI says its preparedness scenarios “are not treated as inevitable”. Separately, Common Sense Media tested ChatGPT for Teens and found the block on role-play works but most other safeguards do not; it recommends under-18s not use it.

Accountability: this is the third OpenAI safety story this brief has carried in ten days, after safety lead Robinson’s resignation (Issue 33-grok) and the Australia rogue-agent disclosure (Issues 32 and 34-grok). Chain-of-thought monitorability is now the specific technical point in dispute, not a general mood.

Lab-safety lead — right depth?

Washington and the labs: a Banks–Gillibrand bill would make the Pentagon vet its frontier-AI vendors, Anthropic opens a critical-infrastructure defence programme, both labs hire Trump officials, and the President calls anyone who says “AI” “THE ENEMY!”

AI & tech US policy New fact

The bill. Sens. Jim Banks (R-Ind.) and Kirsten Gillibrand (D-N.Y.) have introduced an 18-page bill directing the Defense Secretary to set reporting requirements and voluntary guidance for large AI contractors. The Pentagon would continuously vet them for security vulnerabilities and “algorithmic failures”, using DoD purchasing power to enforce safety standards ahead of industry-wide law. Gillibrand: the Pentagon is deploying “incredibly powerful AI technology without commonsense guardrails”. Context: DoD’s May frontier deals went to SpaceX, OpenAI, Google, Nvidia, Reflection, Microsoft, AWS and Oracle, but not Anthropic, which an appeals court upheld as a supply-chain risk in September.

Anthropic’s programme. Its new Critical Infrastructure Defense Program puts frontier models, on-site engineers and threat research with firms that secure grids and water systems. Founding partners are Accenture, Booz Allen, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Insane Cyber, Nozomi, Palo Alto Networks, PwC and Rockwell. It also launched a free OSS Scanner for open-source projects; its reports go to maintainers without human review. Anthropic did not say who pays for compute.

The revolving door. CNBC counts at least two former Trump officials at Anthropic: Sihao Huang, formerly OSTP’s AI lead, now head of frontier compute strategy, and Chris Liddell on the board. It counts at least three at OpenAI. A CNAS fellow: the labs “have learned from the Anthropic episode that it pays to be on the good side of the White House.”

The word. The President on Truth Social: the White House “considers anyone that uses the term, ‘Artificial Intelligence’… THE ENEMY!” This follows up Issues 28 and 29-grok on the September executive order (FR Doc 2026-20321), which carries no penalty and defines “Super Intelligence” using the existing legal definition of AI. AI.gov and OSTP still say “AI”.

AI-policy roundup — keep bundled?

Labor suspends Microsoft, Adobe and six IT outsourcers from the green-card labour-certification programme, on the same stage where Nadella received a National Medal of Science

US policy AI & tech New fact

Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling said the department will not accept or process new or pending PERM labour certifications (the employer step toward a green card) involving Cognizant, Infosys, Capgemini, Tata, Wipro and HCL. Microsoft and Adobe are suspended “due to multiple active federal investigations”. Sonderling: these companies have sought “almost 3 million foreign workers” since 2009, with more than 230,000 H-1B approvals and 100,000 labour certifications. Vice President Vance tied Microsoft’s suspension to its 6,000 layoffs in 2025, said the company “replaced” them with H-1B workers, and said the ban lasts until it shows it is “serious about putting American workers first”.

Microsoft’s reply: of about 6,000 H-1B applications last fiscal year, 80% extended or changed the status of existing employees, and the rest equal 1% of its US workforce, all people already in the country. Vance also announced investigations of nine universities, including Harvard, Yale and Stanford, over alleged J-1 visa fraud and wage undercutting. The announcement came at the White House science summit where Nadella, Huang, Su and Musk, all immigrants, received medals.

Why it matters: this is enforcement against named companies, not a rule change. It sits on top of September’s H-1B integrity order and Thursday’s proposed $70,000 OPT fee. For tech employers, the green-card queue now depends on a firm’s standing with the administration.

SpaceX buys nationwide 800 MHz spectrum to make Starlink Mobile a full carrier, and AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile slide; the President’s August disclosure shows SpaceX notes bought two days before his space-launch policy

AI & tech US financials New fact

SpaceX agreed to buy a portfolio of up to 14 MHz of paired 800 MHz spectrum from Grain Management, subject to FCC approval. It says the deal fills “one of the key remaining technical gaps” for Starlink Mobile to become “a major mobile carrier in the US”, combining satellite-to-phone with terrestrial service. Low-band spectrum gets through walls, which satellites alone cannot do. Analyst Tim Farrar: it is “still a very limited amount of spectrum” and urban coverage would need towers. SpaceX rose about 4% premarket Friday; the three carriers fell sharply. Last week they formed a satellite joint venture that leaves out Starlink.

The disclosure. The President reported 517 securities transactions in August totalling $74.3m–$273.3m (CNBC’s analysis of the ranges). On 18 August his accounts bought $1m–$5m of SpaceX 5.35% notes due 2031. Two days later he signed a national space transportation policy targeting more than 1,000 launches and re-entries a year by 2030. The largest single trade was a $5m–$25m Meta purchase on 21 August. He also bought AT&T and Chevron and sold some Nvidia, AMD and T-Mobile.

This follows yesterday’s brief item on the FCC’s 29 October vote on satellite-to-phone spectrum, which would also benefit SpaceX.

SpaceX / disclosure — worth a lead?

The NRC staff recommends licensing four AP1000 reactors for Fermi America’s “Advanced Energy and Intelligence Campus” next to the Pantex nuclear-weapons plant in Texas; comments are due 23 November

AI & tech Energy Primary document

In today’s Federal Register, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission publishes a draft environmental impact statement on Fermi America’s combined licence application for Project Matador Nuclear Units 1–4, four Westinghouse AP1000 pressurised-water reactors. The site is the Project Matador campus in Carson County, Texas, next to the Department of Energy’s Pantex Plant. Staff’s preliminary recommendation is that the licence be issued, contingent on the separate safety review. The first part of the application was filed on 17 June 2025, so the environmental review has reached a draft in about 16 months.

This is the AI power story at the paperwork level: a data-centre campus whose name includes “Intelligence”, seeking four large light-water reactors, not small modular ones. A draft EIS is not a licence. The safety review, a hearing and financing all still stand between this and construction. No construction schedule or cost is in the notice. Docket NRC-2026-0100; comments by 23 November.

Read with: Congress’s permitting bill making large data centres pay their full grid costs (Issue 34-grok), and Google–Constellation’s 890 MW PJM deal (Issue 33-grok). Self-supplied generation is how a campus avoids both the bill and the queue.

Florida & Tampa Bay: Isaias is a 110 mph hurricane forecast to reach 120 mph before a Panhandle-area landfall tonight, and the surge forecast now reaches Tampa Bay at 1–3 feet

Florida & Tampa Bay Correction

NHC Advisory 11 (4am CDT Friday): maximum winds 110 mph, 963 mb, about 200 miles SSE of the mouth of the Mississippi, moving NNE at 15 mph. Isaias is “likely to become a major hurricane this morning”. The forecast is 120 mph (105 kt) at 1pm CDT today, then some weakening from shear, for a high-end Category 2 landfall in the warning area tonight or early Saturday. The discussion warns that a six-hour delay in the shear “would bring a stronger hurricane than expected onshore”. NWS Mobile’s most likely landfall is near the Alabama–Florida line, with surge across Pensacola Bay.

Warnings: hurricane warning from Ocean Springs, MS to the Bay/Gulf County line. Storm surge warning from the mouth of the Mississippi to the Suwannee River. Surge watch from the Suwannee to Yankeetown. Peak surge forecasts: Dauphin Island to Indian Pass 5–7 ft, Indian Pass to the Steinhatchee 4–6 ft, and Yankeetown to Egmont Key 1–3 ft, which takes in Tampa Bay’s mouth. Pinellas and Hillsborough are not under a watch or warning. DeSantis has declared emergencies and expects about 5,000 Guard troops mobilised by today. Alabama has ordered non-residents out of Gulf Shores, Orange Beach and Dauphin Island.

FEMA: the Guardian calls this the first large surge-deployment test since the cuts. GAO counts about 4,300 departures in FY2025 (17% of staff) and 58 senior executives gone in a year, against about 13 a year before. The Region 4 administrator, covering Florida and Alabama, is among them. FEMA says incident teams are deploying to both states.

Correction: yesterday this edition said the forecast peak of “110 mph” held. Isaias is already at 110 mph and the peak forecast is now 120 mph.
