The evaluator Amodei promised is a consultancy on a $1bn contract — and the slowdown is now an antitrust defendant

AI & tech industry US politics & policy Independent corroboration

On Friday 18 September, Anthropic named Accenture as its first embedded evaluator. Each company says it expects to invest at least $1bn over five years. The work — evaluating and red-teaming models, alignment assessments, testing safeguards — runs through Faculty, the specialist AI unit Accenture bought in January. Evaluators are to watch models take shape in training, follow the decisions governing how they are built and deployed, and speak directly to staff. The arrangement is not exclusive; Anthropic says it is talking to METR and other nonprofits about piloting embedded evaluation on their own funding.

That fires the trigger this file named on 17 September. Scoring the pacing thread then, it asked three questions — is an evaluator named, is the access standing or project-scoped, is the first published report unflattering — and answered one of three. The first is now answered. The second is answered badly.

Because the same Friday, four paying subscribers to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and Gemini filed an antitrust class action in the Northern District of California against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceXAI, alleging they illegally agreed to slow AI development. The complaint’s evidence is the public record: Dario Amodei’s 12 September essay, the same-day agreement from Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis, and a July statement by senior lab employees conceding “intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow”. Counsel is Nick Rowley. No defendant had responded by Saturday.

What to watch: whether Anthropic publishes the access terms and Accenture’s publication rights, and whether any defendant answers the complaint by asserting there was no agreement at all.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether a paid commercial evaluator is independent. Anthropic’s position is that outside evaluators “do not reduce our accountability, but help to make it more verifiable”, and it concedes the novelty directly: no standards yet exist for evaluators’ access or communications. Critics read the whole arrangement as self-policing designed to pre-empt regulation. This is a dispute about significance, not facts — nobody disputes the money, the duration or the access.

Cause. The mundane explanation is capacity, and it is probably the true one. The 12 September essay promised evaluators with permanent, employee-level access — office access, company equipment, tool permissions comparable to internal risk teams. Almost nobody can staff that. The nonprofits that would satisfy the independence objection are small; a firm that can put a standing team inside a frontier lab for five years is, by construction, a large consultancy. Reach for that before reaching for evasion. But note what follows from it: the constraint is structural, so the same substitution will happen at every lab that tries this.

What it means for you. Independence is not a vibe, it is three contract terms, and you can check them: (1) who can terminate the engagement and on what notice; (2) whether the evaluator may publish an unfavourable finding without the lab’s editorial control — the essay promised exactly this, and neither account read for this story confirms it survived into the contract; (3) whether the evaluator sells anything else to the lab or its competitors. A billion dollars over five years is around $200m a year, which is a material revenue line for a business unit and creates precisely the incentive an auditor is supposed not to have. Until those terms are published, treat this as access secured and independence unproven. The observation that settles it is the first unflattering published report — that is still the thread’s third question, and it is still unanswered.

Dig deeper — why the lawsuit is the more interesting of the two documents

The suit is weak on the law and strong as a description of the problem. A Sherman Act §1 claim needs an agreement, and what the plaintiffs have is an essay and three executives saying in public that they agree with it. Conscious parallelism — rivals independently reaching the same conclusion and saying so — is not a conspiracy, and public advocacy for a policy is close to the core of what the First Amendment protects. The July employee statement is better evidence, because it is an acknowledgement of the competitive pressure the parties are accused of relieving, but it is a statement asking government to act, which is the Noerr-Pennington safe harbour almost by definition. On today’s public record this looks unlikely to survive a motion to dismiss.

And yet it identifies the trap precisely. Anthropic’s own co-founder Jack Clark described the pacing problem on 15 September as “a collective action problem” — one firm acting alone does not relieve the pressure on the rest. That is a correct diagnosis and it has an uncomfortable corollary: the only remedy that works is the one antitrust law exists to forbid. Competitors cannot lawfully agree among themselves to restrict output or degrade a product, however good their reason. This is exactly why Amodei’s step 1 — the unilateral evaluator commitment — was the only part with a signature on it, and steps 2 and 3, coordination among firms and among governments, had no date, no signatory and no enforcement. The essay was structured to avoid this problem. The lawsuit says the structure did not work.

Score the thread honestly. It is now 1.5 of 3. An evaluator is named: yes. Access standing rather than project-scoped: yes on its face — five years and employee-level access is standing by any reading — but the independence terms that make standing access worth anything are unpublished, so this is half a point, not a point. First published report unflattering: nothing published. Compare OpenAI’s 16 September framework, which named no evaluator, created no outside access and set no publication frequency; Anthropic has now done materially more than that, and the correct way to say so is that it has done more of a thing whose value still depends on terms nobody has seen.

Watch for one more thing: Accenture stock was reported up around 6% premarket today on the announcement. An evaluation contract that moves the evaluator’s share price is a commercial relationship first. That is not an accusation — it is the reason auditor independence rules exist in every other industry that has them.

Disclosure, and it is not a formality on this story. This brief is written with a model made by Anthropic. Anthropic is the subject of the first half of this story and a defendant in the second. The standing rule in this file is that the reader’s own interests get the same scepticism as anyone’s; the same applies here, and the reader should discount accordingly and check the sources below.

Correction: the ten-year did go through 5%. This brief’s correction was itself wrong

US debt & fiscal Macro & markets Primary source

Correction — second on the same number in four days

On 18 September this brief ran a lead headed by the claim that on Treasury’s own par yield curve the ten-year had not printed above 4.94% in September, that the 5% crossing “did not happen in the series of record”, and that the 15 September lead built on it was withdrawn. That correction was wrong. Treasury’s series, read again this morning with the column headers enumerated explicitly, shows the ten-year at 5.00% on Tuesday 15 September, 5.01% on Wednesday 16 September, 4.94% on Thursday and 5.01% again on Friday 18 September. The 5% crossing happened. The 20 September issue then reported Friday’s close as 4.93%; the correct figure is 5.01%.

What went wrong is identifiable, which is the only reassuring thing here. The values printed on 18 and 20 September were read off the right page and out of the wrong columns. Treasury’s table now carries a 1.5-month tenor between the 1-month and 2-month columns, and the figures this brief published as the “10-year” are in every case the 7-year, and those published as the “2-year” are the 1-year. The 20-year and 30-year, which sit at the end of the row and can be counted backwards from the edge, were right both times. That is why the 18 September entry noticed the offset was not uniform and could not explain it.

So the original vendor-sourced figures — 5.00, 5.041, 5.016, 4.943 for 14 to 17 September — were out by +3, +4, +1 and 0 basis points against the official series. Not good enough to print to the basis point. But the claim they carried was true, and this brief withdrew a true claim.

What to watch: today’s close, due on the same page tomorrow morning. The September high on the series of record is 5.01%, set twice.

Dig deeper — what survives, what is withdrawn, and the rule that has to change

What survives. The analytical claim from 17 September — that the market priced the Fed’s first hike since 2023 as inflation control rather than as a fiscal or credit problem — survives on the corrected numbers and is a little cleaner on them. On Treasury’s series the ten-year fell 7bp from Wednesday’s 5.01 to Thursday’s 4.94, the thirty-year 6bp, the two-year 7bp (4.74 to 4.67). Then Friday gave the whole thing back: ten-year 5.01, two-year 4.76 — a 9bp jump at the short end, the largest single-day move at that tenor this month. A two-year rising nine basis points the session after the market decided the hike was disinflationary is not a comfortable combination, and it is the observation this brief lost by reading the wrong column.

What is still withdrawn. “Highest since July 2007”. That claim was never verified against a multi-decade series and still has not been. It may well be true — a 5.01% ten-year is plainly unusual — but this file does not have the history to say so and will not print it again without one. The 15 September figure of 5.041% also stays withdrawn: the official close was 5.00, and 5.041 has no counterpart at any tenor that day.

The rule that has to change, and it is a different rule from the one written down on 18 September. That day’s lesson — go to the issuer, not to a summary of a blocked vendor page — was correct and stands. It was also insufficient, because it addressed which page and said nothing about how to read it. The new rule is narrow and checkable: when pulling a value out of a wide numeric table, enumerate the column headers in the same request and count the position, never the label. Treasury’s table has twenty-three columns, several of them frequently blank, and a tenor was added to the middle of it. Counting backwards from the right-hand edge is what saved the 20-year and 30-year on both bad reads, and it is the sanity check to apply every time.

The thread’s breaking observation is unchanged and still unbroken: specifically American underperformance — a tailed thirty-year, or US yields rising while Bunds and JGBs fall. That test still cannot be run, because a dated Bund level and a dated ten-year JGB level have both now eluded this brief across five runs. Handelsblatt’s front page carries DAX, Euro Stoxx, Brent and the euro this morning and no Bund yield. The 4 November refunding is where the test would show up.

The SEC wants to abolish the shareholder proposal. The clock started this morning

US financials US politics & policy New subject Primary source

The Federal Register published two SEC proposals this morning, starting a comment period that closes 20 November 2026. The first, File No. S7-2026-32 (Release 34-106383), would rescind Rule 14a-8 in its entirety. That is the rule under which a shareholder can require a company to put a proposal in its proxy statement and hold a vote on it. Under the proposal, “the Federal proxy rules would no longer require companies to include in their proxy materials shareholder proposals on the basis that they satisfy procedural and substantive requirements established under Federal law.” A companion amendment to Rule 14a-4(c) would widen management’s discretionary voting authority over proposals kept out of the materials, with a shareholder opt-out per holding.

The stated ground is not policy but power. Chairman Paul Atkins: “Absent authorization from Congress—which it has not granted for shareholder proposals—the Commission has no authority to determine which matters are a proper subject for a shareholder vote.” The release argues §14(a) of the Exchange Act does not reach the scope of matters put to a vote, and that the rule has been “supplanting, and hindering the development of, state law”.

The second proposal, S7-2026-33, is housekeeping with one live item: it would end the Notice of Exempt Solicitation under Rule 14a-6(g), drop the requirement to deliver an annual report to holders, and cut the broker search period from 20 business days to 5.

What to watch: whether Delaware legislates, and whether index managers file.

Dig deeper — what actually replaces it, and the numbers the release does and does not give

Rescission is not abolition, and the difference matters. Nothing in the proposal bars a shareholder proposal. It removes the federal entitlement to have one carried at the company’s expense in the company’s own proxy. What is left is state corporate law and the company’s bylaws — which is to say, in most cases, whatever the board has written down. A shareholder who wants a vote without the board’s cooperation would have to run an independent solicitation and pay for it, which costs six figures at a large issuer and is why Rule 14a-8 existed. Atkins’s answer is to “look to the state’s legislature” and “the company’s governing documents”. He does not say what a shareholder in a state whose legislature has not acted should do in the meantime, and Delaware — where most large US issuers are chartered — has no such statute today.

The one empirical argument in the release cuts both ways. It compares 34 to 66 proposals included per year in 1943–46 with 437 to 599 a year in 2020–25, and notes that in 2025 about 7% of submitted proposals and 11% of those actually voted on won majority support. Read one way that is a rule being used far beyond its original scale for causes most owners reject. Read the other way, roughly one proposal in nine that reaches a vote commands a majority of the shares — which is a real rate of owners telling boards something the board did not want asked, and the reason management dislikes the mechanism. The release does not give the figure that would settle it: how many proposals were withdrawn after the company negotiated, which is where most of this rule’s effect has historically shown up and which never reaches a vote at all.

Why this belongs on a banks-and-markets beat rather than a governance one. Rule 14a-8 is the cheapest route by which an outside owner forces a large financial institution to hold a recorded vote — on executive pay, on lobbying disclosure, on risk oversight, on concentration. Remove it and the remaining routes are a proxy fight, a lawsuit, or private engagement, all of which favour the largest holders and disadvantage everyone else. The institutions with the most to gain are the biggest issuers; the institutions with the most to lose are asset managers who use the mechanism cheaply on behalf of index funds. Watch whether the large index managers file comments, because if they do not, the practical answer is that they prefer private engagement anyway.

What this brief cannot tell you: the Commission vote. Neither the press release nor the Federal Register text records a tally or a dissent, and Commissioner Uyeda published a supporting statement. Caroline Crenshaw, who dissented from earlier changes to this rule, left the Commission on 3 January 2026. The current composition and the vote count were not established here.

Announced 16 September; published today, which is what starts the 60-day clock. This brief should have had it five days ago.

A nationwide immigration emergency quietly entered its twenty-first month today

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Primary source

Among sixty-one notices in this morning’s Federal Register sits doc 2026-19253, “Finding of Mass Influx of Aliens”. Signed by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin on 18 September, effective on publication, it runs a further 180 days — to roughly mid-March 2027. It finds that “an actual or imminent mass influx of aliens is arriving at the southern border” and, on that basis, requests “the assistance of State and local governments in all 50 States”.

The finding is the statutory precondition for a specific power. Under 8 U.S.C. §1103(a) and 28 CFR §65.83, once a mass influx is found, the Secretary may authorise a state or local police officer — with that officer’s superiors’ consent — to perform the duties of an immigration officer. That is the route around the 287(g) process, which requires a negotiated written agreement with each individual agency.

The document cites its own lineage: an initial finding of 23 January 2025 (90 FR 8399), then extensions on 21 March 2025 (90 FR 13622), 17 September 2025 (90 FR 45396) and 21 March 2026 (91 FR 14703). Today’s is the fifth instrument in the series, and it means the finding has run without a break since January 2025 — twenty months, renewed every six.

What to watch: any published count of officers actually authorised under §1103(a) rather than under 287(g).

Dig deeper — why a rolling emergency is different from a long one

The legal shape is the story, not the policy. Congress wrote §1103(a) as an emergency valve: in a genuine surge, the Secretary should not have to negotiate agency by agency before local police can help. Every feature of the power follows from that premise — it is broad, it is fast, it requires no agreement, and it is bounded only by the Secretary’s own finding that the emergency exists. Renewed on schedule five times across twenty months, it stops being an emergency valve and becomes a standing alternative to the statutory process Congress designed for the ordinary case. Nothing about that is unlawful on its face. It is the same structural move this file has now recorded five times in three weeks — §338 unlitigated for 96 years, the Alien Terrorist Removal Court unused for 30, a bank supervision threshold carried inside a housing bill, an excepted-service schedule created by executive order — a dormant or narrow authority switched on and then simply left on.

Be precise about what the finding does and does not do. It does not itself deputise anyone. It unlocks the Secretary’s discretion to do so. The operative question — how many officers, in which agencies, in which states, are actually carrying §1103(a) authority today — is not answered by this document and this brief could not find it answered anywhere. That is the number that converts a paper authority into a fact on the ground, and its absence is why the renewal passes without comment. By contrast DHS publishes a 287(g) participation list, which is why 287(g) generates local news and this does not.

Note the internal tension, because it is on the face of the document. The finding rests on a mass influx “arriving at the southern border”, and the relief requested is assistance in all fifty states. Those are different geographies, and the document does not bridge them. The administration has spent the year publicising historically low southern-border encounters; a finding of imminent mass influx sits oddly beside that, and the same tension was noted when the March 2026 extension issued. A litigant challenging a specific deputisation would start here — not with the policy, but with whether the predicate finding is supported.

How this was found, since that is the transferable part: by reading the whole day’s notice list rather than the entry the watchlist pointed at. It sat between two FERC water-quality notices and an HHS request for information on wireless radiation, among sixty-one items dominated by information-collection renewals. Nothing flagged it. No reachable news outlet appears to have covered today’s renewal at all.

Washington and Beijing have agreed to telephone each other about AI accidents

AI & tech industry World economy Single-sourced

Sunday’s talks at JPMorgan’s headquarters ran about eight hours and produced the first concrete thing this thread has seen since it opened on 5 September. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Vice Premier He Lifeng agreed to pursue a US–China AI Dialogue, with a US proposal for a notification mechanism covering AI incidents rising “up to a national security level”. Officials expect to reconvene in Shenzhen in about two months. Bessent called the talks “very successful”. Accounts also report progress on tariff carve-outs.

The summit itself is Thursday 24 September, with Xi arriving Wednesday.

Weigh this against the record. A Reuters report on 5 September said AI safety talks were being arranged; a White House official said no such meeting was planned; both capitals publicly rejected the pacing argument on 14 September, Beijing calling it a “Cold War playbook”. The meeting happened anyway, and produced a named mechanism and a follow-up venue. When statements and meetings disagree, weight the meeting.

What to watch: whether Thursday’s readout gives the mechanism a threshold, a channel and a named authority on each side, or leaves it at “dialogue”.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. What was actually agreed. Bloomberg reports the two sides agreed to launch an AI dialogue; CNN reports Bessent proposed an incident-notification mechanism; the Washington Times headlines a communication line as opened. Those are three different degrees of commitment, and the gap between “proposed” and “agreed to launch” is the whole question.

Cause. Not spin, but sequence: this is a briefing after a preparatory meeting three days before a summit, and the deliverable is being held for Thursday. A briefer will describe an American proposal that the Chinese side did not reject as agreement to pursue; a reporter working from a US source will hear “we proposed”. No Chinese-side readout was reached for this story, which is the missing control — and its absence is itself informative, because a jointly announced mechanism would normally be announced jointly.

What it means for you. Read the ceiling, not the headline. An incident-notification channel is the AI equivalent of a military hotline: it does not constrain what either side builds, it only reduces the chance that an accident is read as an attack. That is worth having and it is much less than the pacing proposal both governments rejected a week ago. The test is Thursday’s readout, and there are three things to look for: a defined trigger threshold, a named authority at each end, and whether Beijing announces the same thing in its own words. Two months to Shenzhen is a long time for a mechanism whose value is speed; if the next step is a meeting rather than a working channel, this is a summit deliverable rather than a safety instrument.

CNN, MS NOW and Politico sued this morning. The networks have stopped sharing the pool

US politics & policy Independent corroboration

The three outlets the President declared banned on Friday 18 September filed suit in federal court in Washington, DC this morning against the President and several senior officials. The claims are First Amendment viewpoint discrimination and retaliation, and Fifth Amendment due process — credentials revoked, the complaint says, “without notice or process”. They seek an immediate order restoring access. From the complaint: the President banned the three outlets “in disregard of the most basic guarantees of the First Amendment”.

Saturday’s issue reported that reporters were still inside on Friday afternoon. That changed over the weekend: journalists from each outlet — MS NOW’s Akayla Gardner, CNN’s Betsy Klein, Politico’s Cheyenne Haslett — were turned away by the Secret Service, which took their passes.

The consequential part is not the lawsuit. The White House television pool runs on a five-network rotation. With CNN excluded, the networks have suspended pool coverage of the President. That is a collective action by outlets who are not parties, and it is the first time in this episode that the cost has fallen on the White House rather than on the banned outlets.

What to watch: whether a TRO issues this week, and how long the pool suspension holds.

Dig deeper — why the precedent is strong and the remedy may still be slow

The law here is unusually settled for a First Amendment question. Courts have twice held during this administration that suspending a reporter’s White House pass violated the Constitution, and the line of authority runs back to Sherrill v. Knight (D.C. Cir. 1977): the White House need not open its grounds to everyone, but having created a press facility it cannot deny access to an individual journalist arbitrarily, and must give notice, a chance to respond, and a written decision. The stated rationale here — that it is “wrong” to allow people to write “purposely negative stories” — is close to a stipulation that the decision was content-based. Jameel Jaffer of the Knight First Amendment Institute called it “pretty clearly unconstitutional” before the suit was filed.

The complication is that a strong case does not guarantee a fast remedy. The Associated Press won on similar ground after being excluded from the Oval Office, Air Force One and certain events, and its case is still running; the practical effect of a favourable ruling has repeatedly been narrowed on appeal or absorbed by changing the format of the event rather than the rule. An organisation-level ban on three outlets is a larger act than excluding one reporter, which cuts in the plaintiffs’ favour on the merits and against them on speed, because the government has more to appeal.

Which is why the pool suspension is the thing to watch. The rotation exists because a single camera and a small group of reporters cover events the full press corps cannot attend, and the resulting material is shared with everyone including the outlets now barred. Suspending it removes the video and the print report from the President’s own daily coverage. It is a private remedy, available immediately, requiring no judge, and it can be withdrawn the moment it becomes inconvenient — which is the real question. Collective refusals by commercial competitors are historically fragile; the first network to resume gets the access, and each of them knows it.

What this brief has not established: the case number and the assigned judge, the full list of defendants, and whether a TRO application was filed with the complaint or is to follow. The complaint itself was not read.

Two more tankers hit this morning, and seventeen ships crossed Hormuz all weekend

Geopolitics Energy & grid Single-sourced

Two tankers were struck in the Strait of Hormuz today — an inbound tanker, two crew with minor injuries, and an outbound LPG carrier, crew safe. Neither vessel was named and neither projectile was identified; UKMTO did not attribute either. That makes at least five attacks or security incidents involving tankers since 16 September, one of which breached a hull and started a fire. The Joint Maritime Information Center rates the threat SEVERE and reports that IRGC Navy attacks, attempted attacks and harassment have continued.

The number that matters is the traffic. Seventeen commodity vessels transited over the whole weekend, against roughly 125 a day before the war. This brief’s prior figures — four transits on Tuesday 16 September, seven on the 15th, a ten-day average of 18 — are daily counts, and cannot be added to a weekend total; but seventeen across Saturday and Sunday is consistent with them and no better.

Still absent, and now unfired for fourteen days: the published coordinates of Iran’s restricted zone, and any published tariff for the authorised-passage regime the IRGC announced on 18 September.

What to watch: whether owners comply with Iranian authorisation or keep staying away. That distinction — regime versus threat — is the whole thread.

Dig deeper — what an unattributed strike means when one party has claimed others

Do not resolve attribution, and notice why that is not fence-sitting. Iran claimed the Trend on 18 September explicitly, as a penalty for “illegal passage”. It has not claimed today’s two, or the two of 16–17 September. There are two readings and this brief cannot choose between them on today’s evidence. Either the IRGC is claiming the strikes that serve its declared permission regime and leaving the others deniable, which is what a party building a legal practice would do; or there is more than one actor in the channel. The first is more likely on the pattern recorded since 6 July — 22 of 35 projectile strikes on the southern Omani route, and most incidents hitting engine rooms — because disabling a ship in a narrow channel is cheap, deniable and far better at deterring owners than sinking one. But “more likely” is not attribution.

Hold the measurement discipline that this thread keeps needing. Transit counts come in three incompatible units: gCaptain’s figures are daily counts from Reuters preliminary data; Lloyd’s List Intelligence publishes weekly averages; and CENTCOM’s claims are in barrels assisted, not hulls. Barrels and hulls are not contradictory — a market can move oil on few large ships — and this is exactly where the Wright and CENTCOM volume claims have survived scrutiny they should not have. No official transit count has been published since 28 February, which remains the single number that would settle the argument.

The consequence is not at the strait, it is at a truck stop. The chain this brief has been running as one story holds: Aramco is routing roughly 60 million barrels through Hormuz across September and October because the East–West pipeline is still damaged; that lengthens the haul at the moment vessels became unobtainable; physical Brent has decoupled from front-month futures by $20–30; and US diesel took a twelfth consecutive record this morning. Data centres are on the ballot in eighteen Ohio towns in six weeks

AI & tech industry Energy & grid New subject Single-sourced

On 3 November, voters in eighteen Ohio municipalities and townships decide local measures on data centres. Nine of them — Conneaut, Defiance, Granville, Oregon, Pataskala, Piqua, Sunbury, Trenton and Upper Sandusky — are citizen petitions mirroring a proposed statewide ban on data centres drawing more than 25 megawatts. A referendum on a specific project is on the ballot in Ashville; three petitions in Sidney are headed for a special election after the Ohio Supreme Court held the city clerk had illegally blocked them.

This is no longer only local. On 14 July 2026, New York Governor Kathy Hochul issued Executive Order 62, the first statewide moratorium on large data centres: the Department of Environmental Conservation pauses review of incomplete permit applications for facilities that can consume 50MW or more, while the Department of Public Service prepares a generic environmental impact statement.

NPR, which published a piece on this today, reports the backlash is reshaping politics community by community, and separately that non-disclosure agreements around data-centre deals are drawing legislative attention.

What to watch: how many of the eighteen pass, and whether any of the nine identical petitions fails.

Dig deeper — the megawatt threshold is the whole design, and it points back at FERC

Notice what these measures regulate. Not “AI”, not “technology companies”, not emissions — electrical demand at a single site. Ohio’s petitions use 25MW, New York’s order uses 50MW, and the federal Ratepayer Protection Act (H.R. 9340, out of House Energy and Commerce 52–0 in July, no floor vote) uses 100MW. Three levels of government, three different thresholds, one variable. That is a strong signal about what the fight is actually about, and it is not land use or noise: it is who pays for the generation, transmission and distribution upgrades needed to serve a very large new load. A megawatt threshold is a jurisdictional hook that survives a preemption argument better than a rule about what the building does inside, because connecting load to a distribution system is about as squarely local as regulation gets.

This is the retail end of a story this brief has been covering from the wholesale end. FERC issued show-cause orders on 10 September to all six jurisdictional market operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE, NYISO — to revise how they interconnect large co-located loads or justify their current rules, in docket RM26-4, and conspicuously left cost allocation open. Cost allocation is what reaches a household bill, through a state rate case. What is on the Ohio ballot is the same question arriving from below, before the federal proceeding has answered it, and arriving in a form that binds a specific parcel rather than a tariff.

Two cautions about how to read the vote. First, a moratorium is a pause, not a decision; New York’s order pauses incomplete applications pending a study, which is a real constraint on new entrants and no constraint at all on projects already permitted. Second, be careful with the polling. A figure circulating in coverage of this story holds that roughly seven in ten Americans oppose an AI data centre being built near them; this brief could not reach the underlying poll and is reporting it as attributed rather than as a measurement. Stated opposition to a nearby facility is among the least predictive survey questions there is, and the eighteen ballot lines on 3 November will be a far better instrument than any poll, because they are votes.

The disclosure applies here too: this brief is written with a model whose training and serving depend on exactly the facilities being voted on.
