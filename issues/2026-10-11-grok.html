A missile on Riyadh’s airport kills twelve, an American among them, and the President says the US “may” join Saudi strikes on the Houthis; the Red Sea, the route around Hormuz, is now the front

Geopolitics US policy Energy New fact

The attack. A missile hit the complex housing terminals 3 and 4 at King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, the third strike on Riyadh’s airport this week. Saudi aviation authorities put the toll at 12 dead and at least 309 wounded: four Saudis, two Bangladeshis and one each from the United States, Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Palestine and Sudan (Al Jazeera). It is the deadliest attack on the kingdom in more than a decade. The Saudi-led coalition calls it a Houthi “war crime”; the Houthis have not claimed it, but on Sunday renewed their warning to airlines and travellers to stay out of Saudi airports and airspace. A projectile also fell near Dammam’s airport. Riyadh schools go remote for a week.

The US decision. “We may. We’re going to look at it,” the President said of joining Saudi strikes in Yemen. “We move very quickly.” AP reports US help already includes intelligence sharing, target identification and planning for the coalition’s offensive, and that Saudi Arabia is short of interceptors and has asked France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt for air-defence help; a regional official noted US stocks have also run down during the Iran war. The US, UK, Germany, Australia and Canada told citizens to avoid the airport. At least 291 flights were cancelled in 24 hours.

Why it matters beyond Saudi Arabia. The Houthi offensive followed the collapse of the UN-brokered ceasefire in Yemen and a Saudi-led push to reverse Houthi gains along the Red Sea. Those gains gave the group better surveillance over the corridor that is the main alternative to the Strait of Hormuz (AP). The Houthis say they have struck Aramco facilities, including the Rabigh refinery on the Red Sea coast (Al Jazeera, unverified claim). Semafor notes the timing: Riyadh hosts the World Petroleum Congress this week, with Exxon, Shell, TotalEnergies and BP expected, and some US officials planning to attend are reconsidering.

The diplomatic thread. The Kremlin says Putin relayed to the President, “in agreement with” Iran’s Pezeshkian, Tehran’s vision for ending the war; no specifics were given and the White House did not confirm (CNBC citing Interfax).

What to watch: whether the US joins strikes on Yemen. That would be the first new American combat front since the no-strike-before-midterms pledge on Iran, which this brief reported on Friday and which covers Iran itself, not its proxies.

The Justice Department’s Antitrust Division opens an investigation into ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox for jointly suspending pool coverage of the President, treating a press protest as a possible Sherman Act boycott

US policy Media & law New fact

What happened. The department has asked the five networks in the White House television pool to hand over communications about their decision last month to pause presidential coverage for several days. They did so after the administration revoked the credentials of reporters from CNN, MS Now and Politico on 19 September (BBC, Guardian). A spokesperson: “The Antitrust Division is simply investigating to determine whether these news organizations have violated the antitrust laws.” The theory is that a group boycott among competitors can violate the Sherman Act.

The legal setting. CNN, MS Now and Politico sued over the ban, and a judge’s order restoring their access is in place until Tuesday (Guardian). The White House took control of pool membership from the Correspondents’ Association in February, and the AP’s access case is still being litigated. Pool coverage has resumed. The Committee to Protect Journalists says antitrust should not be used “to intimidate news outlets defending press access.”

Why it is substance, not noise. This uses a competition statute, not a defamation suit or a credentials fight, against the shared newsgathering arrangement that supplies footage of the President to outlets across the country. The pool divides costs and footage among rivals, which is exactly the kind of coordination antitrust law examines. A civil investigative demand would carry compulsory process. No outlet checked has published the department’s letter.

What to watch: Tuesday’s court date on the credentials order, and whether the networks receive formal civil investigative demands.

Microsoft’s Nadella says every frontier model should be treated as already compromised and built with an “emergency brake”; OpenAI, after firing safety staff over contact with evaluators, says outside-assessor contracts come “in the coming weeks”

AI & tech Lab safety New fact

Nadella. In a long post on X on Saturday, Microsoft’s chief executive said AI cannot be treated “as a set of nested black boxes” whose output we simply accept or reject. “We must assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start. Think of it like an emergency brake. An authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task” (The Verge, TechCrunch). His list: separate the model from the harness that runs it, externalise controls, keep “tamper-proof human readable evidence” of every meaningful action, use model diversity, test continuously, allow independent audit, and disclose incidents. “Treating frontier closed and open weight models like insider risks is a way to build such a system” (CNBC).

Why it lands now. Anthropic’s disclosure that its test agents touched government sites and sent Philadelphia police a fake tip (lead 3, Saturday) is the case study. Nadella runs one of the largest platforms that sells frontier models to businesses, so when he calls for containment “standards where existing ones are insufficient”, he is proposing terms for the labs whose models it carries. He uses “Super Intelligence”, the administration’s renamed term from EO 14434.

The evaluator gap. CNBC reports the independent evaluators the labs now lean on, METR, Apollo Research and Transluce among them, are mostly small nonprofits, with open questions on funding, access and reporting lines. The President’s voluntary accord in late September urged labs to “partner with an independent external auditor or evaluator”. Update on Friday’s Grok lead: OpenAI fired three staff last week. Two of them, Mikita Balesni and Tomek Korbak, say it was over how they communicated with outside evaluators; Balesni wrote on Thursday that former colleagues “are afraid to speak”. OpenAI disputes that and said on Friday it is “actively finalizing contracts with third-party safety assessors and will announce details in the coming weeks.”

What to watch: the OpenAI assessor contracts, specifically who pays, what access they get and whether results are published; and whether Microsoft writes Nadella’s containment terms into Azure AI contracts.

Macro US debt & fiscal Markets

The curve. Treasury’s par-yield series for Friday 9 October closed the 10-year at 5.24%, the 20-year at 5.65% and the 30-year at 5.60%. The 2-year is 4.80% and 3-month bills 4.25%. So the curve rises steeply from bills to 20 years, then dips at the very long end. Thursday showed the same shape (5.64% against 5.60%).

The forecasters. The Wall Street Journal’s quarterly survey finds economists no longer see the climb to quarter-century highs as a blip. That is opinion, but its numbers line up with the nearly $2tn fiscal-2026 deficit this brief reported on Saturday.

The Supreme Court looks ready to side with Intel in a 401(k) case that sets how hard it is to sue over private-market funds, as employers wait on it and on Labor’s alternatives rule before changing plan menus

US financials Courts

The case. Anderson v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee, argued on Tuesday 6 October, asks whether a claim that a retirement plan underperformed must point to a “meaningful benchmark”. The case is not about whether hedge funds and private equity can be in a 401(k). The lower courts said underperformance alone is not enough (CNBC).

The bench. Justices across the court reached for the same metaphor. Thomas said “you can’t compare apples and oranges”; Kagan said “the thing that you need … is another apple”; Gorsuch asked the employees’ lawyer to accept that “a meaningful benchmark of some kind is required”. The Solicitor General’s office urged the court to set “some parameters”: “Prudence is about process and not about performance.” Columbia’s Ronald Mann, writing on SCOTUSblog, read the argument as leaning toward Intel.

Why it matters. A ruling for Intel would make it harder to sue employers who add private-market sleeves to 401(k)s. That is the litigation risk keeping sponsors on the sidelines while asset managers launch products. ERISA lawyer Elizabeth Hopkins says companies are waiting for the decision and for the Labor Department’s final rule on alternatives. WSJ separately reports that indexed annuities, which insurers can periodically reprice, have become a funding engine for private credit (teaser only). Retirement money is moving into private markets from more than one direction.

What to watch: the decision, likely by June, and the final DOL rule.

Sources: CNBC — argument, Mann (read) · WSJ — annuities (teaser)

Data centres are on the ballot: at least 20 candidates in 36 governor’s races promise moratoriums, more than 30 local measures sit in eight states, and Ohio’s Senate race is being fought over power bills

AI & tech AI power & grid US policy

Wired’s count is the most concrete measure yet of AI infrastructure becoming a vote. Citing MultiState research, it says at least 20 candidates in the 36 gubernatorial races have promised some kind of data-centre moratorium. Voters in eight states face more than 30 local ballot measures, and Ohio has more than half of them, from local moratoriums to rezoning referendums.

The races. An NRSC memo in August warned that Sherrod Brown’s attacks on Sen. Jon Husted as “the face of data centers in Ohio” could tip the race, calling it “a sleeper issue”. Senate Democrats blocked Husted’s bill modelled on the White House ratepayer-protection pledge; Schumer called it “toothless”. In Texas, Gov. Abbott has moved from courting data centres to a de facto moratorium on permits in less than a year. New York’s Hochul signed the only true hyperscale moratorium in the country in July. In Wisconsin both governor candidates back restrictions, yet one has branded the other “Data Center David”.

Why it matters for the build-out. This brief has followed the physical limits: grid interconnection, turbines and power bills. The political limit now arrives at the state level, where permitting and utility rates are decided. A wave of moratorium governors would bind regardless of federal AI policy. Many Republicans are breaking with the White House on this, Wired notes.

What to watch: the Ohio ballot measures and Senate result on 3 November, and whether Texas formalises its permit pause.
