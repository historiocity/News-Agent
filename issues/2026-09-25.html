The ten-year is at 5.18%, the highest of this move, and the thirty-year mortgage has a seven in front of it for the first time in twenty months

US debt & fiscal Macro & markets Primary source

Treasury’s own par yield curve closed Thursday with the ten-year at 5.18%. That is a new high for this move: the September high had been 5.01%, set on the 16th and again on the 18th. The curve has moved 22 basis points in two sessions — 4.96% on Tuesday, 5.11% Wednesday, 5.18% Thursday.

The same morning, Freddie Mac’s weekly survey put the thirty-year fixed mortgage at 7.03%, up from 6.95%. It has not printed a seven since the week of 16 January 2025, when it was 7.04% — twenty months. The fifteen-year went to 6.42% from 6.26%, a sixteen-point jump in a week.

Two details matter more than the headline numbers. First, the survey closes on Thursday and reflects a week in which the ten-year averaged roughly 5%, not 5.18% — so 7.03% is already stale to the downside, and next Thursday’s print is the one that carries this week’s move.

Second, the twenty-year sits at 5.53% and the thirty-year at 5.47%. Twenties yielding more than thirties is not a normal shape, it has persisted for over a week, and it widened to six basis points yesterday. The long end is not repricing uniformly; it is repricing worst where the issuance is heaviest.

What to watch: next Thursday’s Freddie Mac print, which absorbs this week; whether the twenty-thirty inversion survives the 4 November refunding; and the two-year, at 4.87%, which is rising too but eleven basis points slower than the ten over the same two days.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not the level — everyone has the same yields. The dispute is over what a rising long end signifies, and the two live readings are almost opposite. Handelsblatt’s front page this morning describes a US bond selloff spreading globally, with Treasury yields at yearly highs on Fed tightening concerns: a stress story. Freddie Mac, publishing a 7.03% mortgage rate the same day, writes that the housing market “remains supported by a solid labor market and an economy that is growing at a healthy rate”: a strength story. This is a dispute about significance, not facts. Both are describing a 5.18% ten-year.

Cause. Vantage, mostly, and a little interest. Handelsblatt is writing for European investors who hold Treasuries as an asset and experience a yield rise as a loss and as a signal about American fiscal credibility. Freddie Mac is a government-sponsored enterprise whose job is the mortgage market and whose weekly commentary is, by long habit, reassuring; a chief economist for a housing agency does not lead with distress. Neither is wrong on its own terms. But note the timing asymmetry too: Freddie Mac’s comment was written against a week averaging 5%, before Thursday’s 5.18%.

What it means for you. The evidence currently favours the strength reading over the stress reading, and there is a specific reason: the two-year is rising as well, 4.71% to 4.87% over the same three sessions. A pure fiscal-credibility event shows up as the long end rising while the front end stays put or falls — the curve steepening because investors demand more to hold duration, not because they expect higher policy rates. That is not what this is; the whole curve is moving, which reads as expectations about the Fed rather than doubt about the Treasury. The observation that would flip it is in the twenty-thirty inversion: if twenties keep out-yielding thirties, that is a supply-and-issuance signal rather than a policy one, and it is the single thing in this table that does not fit the benign story. Watch the 4 November refunding, which is where a genuine buyer strike would show.

Dig deeper — the column trap recurred this morning, and the rule written on 21 September caught it

This brief has now got the ten-year wrong twice and nearly got it wrong a third time this morning. The history is worth one paragraph because the failure mode is mechanical and reusable, not a matter of judgement. On 14–17 September the figures came from a search-engine summary of a blocked vendor page and were out by up to four basis points. On 18 September a correction claimed Treasury’s own series never crossed 5%; that correction was itself wrong, and was withdrawn on 21 September when the table was re-read with the column headers enumerated. The values printed as the “ten-year” on 18 and 20 September were in fact the seven-year.

It happened again today. The first read of Treasury’s September page this morning returned 4.93, 4.89, 4.89, 5.05 and 5.10 for the 18th, 21st, 22nd, 23rd and 24th. Those are the seven-year column. The second read, which asked for the headers numbered and every cell in position order, returned a twenty-six-column table — the ten-year is column 24 — and the correct values: 5.01, 4.96, 4.96, 5.11, 5.18. Had the first read been published, this issue would have told you the ten-year had just crossed 5% for the first time, which is both wrong and something this brief has already said once.

Two things follow. The first is that the rule written on 21 September — enumerate the column headers in the same request and count the position, never the label — is not a precaution, it is the only thing standing between this brief and a third wrong print of the same number. The second is that the table itself is unstable: on 21 September it had twenty-three columns, this morning twenty-six. A position memorised on one day is wrong on another. The enumeration has to happen every time.

What is still withdrawn, and stays withdrawn: “highest since July 2007”. A 5.18% ten-year is plainly unusual, and this brief verified directly that Treasury’s series was above 5% routinely in October and November 2023, peaking at 5.19% on 17 and 18 October 2023 and last printing a five-handle on 13 November 2023. The twenty-eight months between then and now were not walked one by one, so the phrase “highest since” is still not available. What can be said is narrower and true: 5.18% is within a basis point of the October 2023 peak, and above every reading this brief has recorded in September.

The Senate rejected the Iran war-powers resolution 49–50 — but it was July’s resolution, and one more vote would not have passed it

US politics & policy Primary source

At 1:45pm Thursday the Senate rejected H. Con. Res. 89 by 49 to 50, on roll call 244. The resolution would have directed the President to remove US forces from hostilities with Iran absent a declaration of war or a specific authorisation.

Four Republicans voted yes: Collins, Murkowski, Paul and Tillis. One Democrat, Fetterman, voted no. Alsobrooks did not vote. The arithmetic is checkable from the totals alone — fifty of the fifty-three Republicans are in the nay column with Fetterman, which leaves exactly four.

Two things the coverage is compressing. First, the margin is not one vote. At 50–50 the Vice President breaks the tie, so passage needed 51. Alsobrooks’ absence cost nothing; the resolution was two votes short, not one.

Second, this was the older resolution. H. Con. Res. 89 is Jayapal’s, House-passed 23 July, 214–208, four Republicans. The House passed a second withdrawal resolution on 15 September — Moulton’s H. Con. Res. 93, 220–204, seven Republicans. The Senate took up the weaker count and has not touched September’s.

And as this brief said on 16 September: a concurrent resolution is not presented to the President, cannot be signed or vetoed, and would not have the force of law even if it had passed. This is a count, not a constraint.

What to watch: whether Moulton’s H. Con. Res. 93 gets a privileged Senate vote before the recess; a joint resolution, which is the instrument that would actually bind; and the appropriations route, which is the only mechanism in this area that has ever worked.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether yesterday was progress or regression, and the sources split on which count to compare it to. NPR frames it as Senate Republicans blocking the effort in one of their last votes before November. NBC frames it as the Senate narrowly failing to stop further strikes, and leans on the four Republican defections. Neither says what the comparison class is, and the answer changes the story: against the Senate’s own March vote, which went 53–47 on straight party lines, four Republican defections is real movement. Against the House’s 15 September vote, where seven Republicans crossed, the Senate is behind.

Cause. Partly a reporting convention and partly the calendar. “Narrowly rejected” is the natural way to write 49–50, and the tie-breaking rule that makes 51 the real threshold is one procedural step past what a same-day story has room for. The calendar matters more: this is the last Senate week before members go home to campaign, which is why both accounts reach for the midterm frame rather than the legislative one. And there is a genuine substantive reason the Senate is behind the House — senators who face voters in 2028 rather than in six weeks have less reason to move.

What it means for you. Read this as a whip count that improved by two and is still two short, and discount any account that presents it as nearly having stopped the war — it was never the instrument that could. The number that actually matters going forward is not 49 but four: the Republican defection trend is four in July, seven in the House in September, four in the Senate yesterday, against zero in the Senate in March. The observation that would change the picture is a Republican senator saying the legal position has changed rather than that the war is unwise — which is what Zach Nunn did in the House on 15 September, and what no senator has yet done on the record. Watch for that sentence, not for the tally.

Dig deeper — why the Senate voted on the July resolution, and what happened to September’s

Two House-passed concurrent resolutions directing withdrawal from Iran are now sitting in the Senate. H. Con. Res. 89, Jayapal’s, cleared the House on roll call 282 at 10:24am on 23 July by 214–208: Republicans 4–207, Democrats 210–0, the chamber’s one independent against, nine not voting. H. Con. Res. 93, Moulton’s, cleared on roll call 307 at 10:32pm on 15 September by 220–204, with seven Republicans and thirteen veteran cosponsors. The second is the stronger count on every measure.

The Senate voted on the first. The mechanical reason is that a war-powers concurrent resolution becomes privileged on a schedule tied to when it was received, and 89 had been sitting since July with its procedural clock long expired into availability; 93 arrived ten days ago. The practical effect is that the Senate has now put itself on the record against the weaker of the two texts, and a second vote on the stronger one would be a second bite that leadership has no reason to schedule. If 93 never gets a floor vote, the July count is the Senate’s answer to both.

Worth separating clearly: the reason none of this binds anybody is INS v. Chadha (1983), which struck down the legislative veto and with it the self-executing concurrent-resolution mechanism written into the War Powers Resolution of 1973. Congress responded the same year by adding expedited procedures for a joint resolution — which goes to the President, can be vetoed, and needs two-thirds of both chambers to survive that veto. Nobody has taken that route on Iran. Tim Kaine has run the Senate side repeatedly through 2026, in March, April and May, and his measures have been rejected each time; his statement yesterday was that the President is in violation of the Constitution, the War Powers Act and a concurrent resolution already passed.

One limit on this item. The Senate roll call, the House roll calls and the Clerk’s question lines have all been read directly. The text of H. Con. Res. 89 has not: congress.gov has returned 403 to this brief since 14 September. So the description of what the resolution directs comes from the question line and from Bloomberg Government’s bill analysis. If it contains a reporting requirement or a certification clause, that would not be visible here.

Transportation deleted the race and sex presumptions from federal contracting this morning, and suspended every contract goal while firms re-apply

US politics & policy Underreported New subject Primary source

A Transportation Department final rule published this morning and effective today rewrites the Disadvantaged Business Enterprise programme — the rule that governs who gets a share of federally funded highway, transit and airport contracting.

Gone is the presumption that members of named groups — Black, Hispanic, Native, Asian-Pacific and Subcontinent Asian Americans, and women — are socially and economically disadvantaged. Every firm must now make an individualised showing, through a personal narrative documenting specific instances of economic hardship, social barriers and denied opportunities.

The operationally significant part is not the standard but the interval. Contract goals are suspended while certifying agencies re-evaluate their existing rosters, and those re-evaluations must be complete by 24 December 2026. For the next three months there is a federal contracting programme with no numerical targets attached to it.

The legal scaffolding is cited explicitly: the 2024 Mid-America Milling district court ruling that the presumptions were likely unconstitutional, Students for Fair Admissions, Executive Orders 14151 and 14173, and a Justice Department conclusion that the presumptions violate equal protection.

This is the final rule, not the first move. Transportation issued it as an interim final rule without prior notice and comment, claiming good cause, then took comment afterwards. It received 637 responses, and today’s document is the answer to them.

What to watch: how many currently certified firms fail to re-certify by 24 December; whether any state transportation department litigates the suspension of goals; and whether the same individualised-narrative structure appears in SBA’s 8(a) programme, which rests on the identical presumption.

Dig deeper — what a personal narrative actually requires, and why the good-cause procedure matters as much as the standard

The DBE programme is not small. It is the mechanism attached to federal-aid highway money, transit formula grants and airport improvement grants — which is to say most publicly funded construction in the United States has a DBE clause in it. State and local certifying agencies, organised as Unified Certification Programs, maintain the rosters. A firm on the roster can bid into the set-aside; a firm off it cannot. Roughly speaking, the roster is the programme.

What changes for a firm is the burden of production. Under the presumption, an owner established group membership and personal net worth and was done. Under an individualised determination the owner must write a narrative and evidence it: specific instances of economic hardship, social barriers and denied opportunity, in their own business history. That is a materially different exercise. It is the kind of showing a firm with counsel can make and a two-person firm often cannot, and the predictable effect of the change is compositional rather than simply numerical — the roster will thin at the small end first.

The procedural history deserves its own attention. Issuing an interim final rule without notice and comment is an exceptional step, justified by “good cause” under the Administrative Procedure Act; the Department’s theory was that a constitutionally infirm presumption could not be left running while comment was taken. That theory has a cost. Six hundred and thirty-seven comments arrived after the rule was already operating, which means the record was built around a fait accompli, and the answer to those comments is the document published today. Anyone challenging this rule will start there rather than with the equal protection question.

What is not visible from here. This item rests on the Federal Register full text, which is authoritative on what the rule says. It does not establish how many firms are currently certified, what share of contract dollars flowed through the programme last year, or what the 637 comments argued. Those numbers are what would let you size the effect rather than describe it, and none of them are in the document.

Nolo is a hurricane and Hawaii County is under a tropical storm warning, with a Category 3 possible tonight

Science & public health Warning in effect Independent corroboration

Nolo reached hurricane strength overnight, with maximum sustained winds of 75mph, centred about 255 miles south of South Point and 435 miles south-southeast of Honolulu. Yesterday it was a strengthening tropical storm under a watch.

The alerts have escalated accordingly. Hawaii County is now under a tropical storm warning as well as a hurricane watch, and Maui County is under a tropical storm watch. A warning means conditions are expected, not merely possible.

Forecasters expect continued strengthening to a Category 3 tonight, around the time of closest approach to the Big Island — a day earlier than yesterday’s Saturday-night framing. Rainfall guidance is unchanged and remains the main threat: 15 to 20 inches, with local maxima to 35, described as capable of catastrophic and life-threatening flooding and mudslides.

One piece of the forecast has moved in Hawaii’s favour. Nolo is expected to turn sharply left to the west near the island on Saturday, and the latest track puts that turn further south than earlier runs. A strong Category 3 passing offshore is a very different event from one making landfall, and the difference is currently a matter of tens of miles.

What to watch: whether the hurricane watch converts to a hurricane warning today; the closest-approach distance in tonight’s advisories; and the rainfall totals on the Hilo and Ka‘u slopes, which is where the 35-inch maxima would land.

The White House budget office wants eighty-six essential medicines made in America within eighteen months, and is asking who can do it

Science & public health Underreported New subject Primary source

The Office of Management and Budget published a request for information this morning on domestic and near-shore manufacturing of the medicines on the ASPR 86 list — the federal inventory of drugs deemed essential. The stated goal is domestic production within eighteen months. Comments close 26 October.

Read the scope before the ambition. It excludes Schedule II controlled substances and branded drugs: this is about generics, where the shortages and single-source foreign supply chains actually are. Two antibiotics not on the list, amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin, are pulled in on vulnerability grounds.

What it asks for is a capacity census with a price attached: idle capacity and how fast it could run, new sites and whether they are brownfield or greenfield, FDA registration status, the country of origin of upstream inputs — the question that actually maps the dependency — and then what debt, equity or offtake contracts a respondent would need, and what price per unit makes it viable.

The tell is in the purpose clause. Responses will inform “near-term federal acquisition efforts”, potentially including limiting competition for a federal contract to a particular source. That is procurement language for picking a winner, and it is unusual to see it stated this plainly in an RFI.

Note who is asking: OMB, over the signature of a deputy associate director for economic policy — not HHS, not ASPR, not the FDA.

What to watch: whether a Defense Production Act determination follows the comment close; which companies respond publicly; and whether the eighteen-month horizon survives contact with FDA facility approval timelines, which are the binding constraint on any new sterile-injectable line.

Confidence: Primary source — the RFI text. The ASPR 86 list itself was not retrieved, so this item does not tell you which medicines are on it. Sources: Federal Register, OMB request for information 2026-19646, 25 September

Sole-source procurement of generic medicines is a supply-chain story, an industrial-policy story and a drug-pricing story at once.

New York sued Polymarket and Polymarket sued back the same afternoon. The question is whether a state can regulate a prediction market at all

US financials New subject Independent corroboration

New York Attorney General Letitia James sued Polymarket on Thursday, alleging it operates as an unlicensed gambling platform: no Gaming Commission licence, marketing sports wagers since July 2025, and accepting users at eighteen where the state’s threshold is twenty-one. Polymarket countersued in Manhattan federal court within hours.

The counterclaim is the substance. Polymarket argues the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has exclusive authority over prediction markets, which would put state gambling enforcement outside its jurisdiction entirely. This is a preemption fight dressed as a consumer-protection case.

It is also not an isolated one. Arizona, Massachusetts and Nevada have brought their own actions against prediction platforms, and New York has previously moved against Kalshi, Coinbase and Gemini. The pattern is a growing set of states asserting authority that a federal regulator says it already holds.

Why this matters beyond the parties: a CFTC-regulated event contract and a state-licensed sports bet can be economically identical, and the two regimes carry completely different rules on age, advertising, funding sources and consumer recourse. Whichever way the preemption question resolves, it settles that arbitrage for every venue at once.

What to watch: whether the CFTC intervenes or files a statement of interest, which would convert this from a company-versus-state case into a federal-versus-state one; and whether the Second Circuit gets it before another circuit does.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — but neither complaint has been read here, only reporting on them. Sources: Al Jazeera economy desk

Judged on a local bar rather than a national one. Today it clears both.

Hillsborough, Pasco and Pinellas all declared states of emergency over dengue yesterday afternoon — two of them with single-digit case counts

Florida & Tampa Bay Emergency declared Independent corroboration

All three Tampa Bay counties issued local states of emergency on Thursday afternoon. Pinellas did it by Resolution 26-76. The declarations run seven days unless rescinded earlier, and their practical effect is to let county administrators and emergency management directors waive routine procedure — procurement, contracting, notice — to move on mosquito control.

The case distribution is the part worth sitting with. 143 confirmed cases across the three counties: 134 in Hillsborough, 7 in Pinellas, 2 in Pasco. Hillsborough added 36 in the latest state report. Pasco and Pinellas declared emergencies at two cases and seven.

That is not an overreaction, and the reason is the vector. Aedes aegypti is a container breeder with a flight range measured in a few hundred metres; local transmission spreads by mosquito populations establishing in new neighbourhoods, not by infected people travelling. A county that waits for its own case count to justify the spend has already lost the window in which spraying and source reduction are cheap. Two cases in Pasco is the number at which acting is still possible.

Behind all of it is the death this brief reported on 20 September: an 80-year-old woman bitten near her South Tampa home, the region’s first dengue death in roughly a century.

What to watch: whether the seven-day declarations are extended on 1 October, which is the tell for whether officials think the curve is bending; the state arbovirus report for week 38; and whether Hernando or Manatee follow, which would mean the perimeter did not hold.

Dig deeper — what a seven-day local emergency actually buys, and the week-37 gap this brief kept reporting

A Florida local state of emergency under chapter 252 is an administrative instrument, not a public-health order. It does not close anything, require anything of residents, or trigger state money on its own. What it does is suspend the procedural furniture: competitive bidding on contracts, the usual approval chain for overtime, and in some counties the notice period for entering private property for abatement. For mosquito control specifically that translates into being able to hire contract spray crews and buy larvicide this week rather than at the next commission meeting.

The seven-day clock is the interesting design feature. It forces a decision on 1 October: extend, and you are saying the outbreak is still growing; let it lapse, and you are saying the surge capacity did its job. Watch that date rather than the case counts, because the counts lag infection by one to two weeks and the administrative decision does not.

On the surveillance question this brief has been tracking for eleven runs: the Florida Department of Health arbovirus report is the authoritative weekly count, and its absence was the story for most of September — week 36 finally published on 18 September and showed the outbreak’s biggest week to that point. The counts quoted above come from the counties and from the latest state report as relayed by local newsrooms, not from the PDF itself, which cannot be parsed in this environment. The 134 figure for Hillsborough and the 36-case weekly increment are therefore second-hand, and the tri-county total of 143 should be read as a count of confirmed cases at the moment of declaration rather than a surveillance number.

One thing the coverage does not yet establish: whether these are all locally acquired. Travel-associated dengue is common in Florida and is counted separately in the state report; locally acquired cases are the ones that indicate an established mosquito reservoir. If a meaningful share of the 134 is travel-associated, the picture is less severe than the headline count suggests. None of the reachable sources split it.

Geopolitics The UAE suspended every Iranian airline flight today. The lever was a US warning that servicing them costs an airport its dollar access.

The UAE’s General Civil Aviation Authority suspended all flights operated by Iranian carriers to and from the country on 25 September. Dubai has been the principal hub for Iranian aviation, with multiple daily services; an Iran Airtour Tehran–Dubai flight was cancelled on the day the suspension took effect.

The mechanism is the interesting part. Treasury sanctioned 27 airlines and dozens of supporting companies earlier this month, paired with a warning that any airport servicing Iranian carriers risked losing dollar access. That is not a sanction on the UAE; it is a sanction on Iranian airlines plus a credible threat to a third party’s banking.

The pattern is regional: Oman, Iraq, Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkmenistan have all restricted access, and the UAE had already suspended trade and financial exchange with Iran last month. It is the clearest example yet of dollar-access leverage producing a third-country action.

Below the line because the underlying policy — the 27-airline designation — is two weeks old and already covered, and today’s item is a foreseeable consequence of it rather than a new decision by the United States. It leads if a country pushes back publicly, or if the dollar-access threat is used on a sector other than aviation.

Energy & grid EPA finalised a rule saying air permits cannot be used to revisit construction permits — which closes the route citizens have used to challenge them.

A final EPA rule published this morning, effective 26 October, narrows what counts as an “applicable requirement” in a Title V air operating permit. Two changes carry weight.

First, a New Source Review construction permit issued under an approved state programme now conclusively establishes the NSR-related requirements, and EPA will not re-examine that decision through Title V. Second, the Clean Air Act’s general duty clause on accidental releases is declared not an applicable requirement, so it does not enter the permit.

The practical effect is procedural and substantial. Title V petitions have been the standard route for citizens and environmental groups to raise an NSR problem — a plant that should have got a construction permit and did not. Those objections now go through title I appeals, with shorter windows and different standing rules. This was a proper notice-and-comment rulemaking, proposed in January 2024 — unlike the transportation rule two stories up.

Below the line because it is a rule about which forum hears an objection rather than about emissions, and the effect is measured in permits that are never challenged, which will not show up in any dataset for years. It leads if a specific major source — a data-centre gas plant, say — is permitted under it in a way that could not have survived the old route.

Confidence: Primary source. Sources: Federal Register, EPA final rule 2026-19671

US financials OFAC pulled every sanctions penalty into one new rulebook today, without notice and comment, and wrote a criminal-intent standard into it.

Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control published a rule this morning, effective immediately, consolidating penalty provisions scattered across its individual sanctions programmes into a single new part of the regulations. OFAC describes it as reproducing existing procedures without substantive change.

The amounts confirm that: the IEEPA civil penalty remains the greater of $377,700 or twice the transaction value, criminal penalties up to $1m and twenty years for wilful violations, the same under the United Nations Participation Act, and base penalties halved for voluntary self-disclosure.

Two features are not housekeeping. It was issued without notice and comment, on the foreign-affairs exemption — routine for OFAC, worth noticing when the subject is the penalty schedule itself. And it writes in explicit language on the mental state required for criminal liability, citing Executive Order 14294: wilfulness is required. That mens rea codification is the part with teeth, because it is the standard a prosecutor has to meet.

Below the line because OFAC says it changes nothing substantive and the penalty figures bear that out, so the consequence is administrative clarity rather than a shift in exposure. It leads if the wilfulness language is invoked to drop or narrow a live enforcement action.

Confidence: Primary source. Sources: Federal Register, OFAC rule 2026-19678

World economy The trade commission will run full five-year reviews on tyre duties from four Asian suppliers — the slow version of the process, which usually means the duties survive.

The International Trade Commission gave notice this morning that it will conduct full five-year reviews of the antidumping and countervailing duty orders on passenger vehicle and light truck tyres from South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam.

The choice of instrument is the content. A sunset review can be expedited or full. Expedited means the Commission saw inadequate response from interested parties and will decide on the paper record; full means it received adequate responses from both domestic producers and respondents and will run hearings, questionnaires and a staff report. A full review takes most of a year and is what happens when the domestic industry shows up in force.

Revocation would reopen a supply line that currently carries duty; continuation locks in the wall for another five years, on top of whatever section 232 and 301 measures apply. For a household it is a line item in the price of four tyres.

Below the line because a notice of institution is the first step of a twelve-month process with no determination attached, and this brief has not read the responses that made it full rather than expedited. It leads on the final determination, or earlier if the staff report puts a number on the import share at stake.

Confidence: Primary source for the notice; the 2021 order dates are from background knowledge and were not verified against the Commission’s records this morning. Sources: Federal Register notices, 25 September (2026-19628)

Science & public health NIH has proposed telling clinical trial participants what the trial found — which is not currently anyone’s obligation.

NIH published a draft policy this morning on sharing summary-level study results with the people who took part in the research. It is out for comment.

The gap it addresses is real and unglamorous. A participant in an NIH-funded trial has no general right to be told what the study concluded. Results go to ClinicalTrials.gov and into the literature, both of which are public and neither of which is a communication to the person who was in the trial. People routinely give blood, time and risk and never learn the answer.

The hard part is in the phrase “summary level”. Aggregate findings are not individual findings, and a participant told the drug arm did better will want to know which arm they were in. Draft NIH policies bind grantees through the terms of award, which in practice means most US clinical research.

Below the line because it is a draft in a comment period with no adoption date, and this brief has not read the document — only the notice announcing it. It leads on the final policy, with the implementation date and whether it applies to trials already under way.

Science & public health FDA issued draft guidance on what a robotic surgery system has to show before it goes to market.

FDA published draft guidance this morning on premarket submissions for robotically-assisted surgical devices, open for comment.

This is the neighbouring question to the AI medical-device thread this brief has been running, and the distinction matters. A robotically-assisted surgical device is a tool a surgeon operates; the regulatory questions are human-factors ones — training, the interface, what happens when a component fails mid-procedure, how the system hands control back. An autonomous or AI-driven device raises a different question about who is deciding. Guidance that sets the evidentiary bar for the first category is the scaffolding the second will be built on.

Draft guidance is not binding and represents current thinking, but device sponsors treat it as the specification because departing from it means arguing your way through review.

Below the line because it is a draft with no effective date and the guidance text was not read — only the notice of availability. It leads if the final version addresses adaptive or AI-driven control, which would make it the first FDA document to set an evidentiary bar for surgical autonomy.

Confidence: Single-sourced — the notice title only. Sources: Federal Register notices, 25 September (2026-19704) · relates to 22 September
