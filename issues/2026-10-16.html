The SEC has licensed betting on earnings numbers, and the contracts settle even if the numbers are restated

US financials Macro & markets New thread Underreported Primary source

The Securities and Exchange Commission has approved, on an accelerated basis, a Cboe proposal to list binary options on the individual financial metrics companies report in their earnings filings — not on their share prices. Release 34–106659, file SR–CBOE–2026–061, signed 13 October and published this morning at 91 FR 65854.

The mechanics are blunt. A binary pays a fixed amount if the option finishes in or at the money and nothing if it does not, so the payout is all-or-nothing. Here the settlement test is whether a named metric in a Commission filing meets or exceeds a strike level. The exercise settlement amount is $1 and the contract multiplier is one — the Exchange's own words call the product retail. The position limit, added only in the amendment the Commission approved alongside the rule, is 25,000 contracts.

Twenty-two issuers are listed, each with its own named metrics. Apple on iPhone and Services net sales and on Greater China. Meta on Family Daily Active People. Tesla on Model 3/Y production and on Supercharger connectors. Palantir on closed deals of at least $1m. Robinhood on funded customers and average revenue per user. Marathon Digital on blocks won and energised hashrate. JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citigroup on net interest income and provision for credit losses. Also SpaceX, listed only since June.

The provision that should give a reader pause is the one on error. If an issuer restates a metric after expiry, the settlement does not change. The value as reported on the expiration date is final and the money has moved. A contract on reported accounting therefore pays out on the report, not on the truth.

What to watch: the options disclosure document, still pending Commission review, which is what a retail buyer will actually be handed; the first listed series and which metric it is written on; and whether another exchange copies the filing.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A dispute about law, not about facts, and it is about which regulator owns this. Commenters argued these are not securities options at all: unlike every binary now listed, they do not settle on the price or value of a security but on an accounting line. One commenter attacked the Exchange's central authority, the 2007 CD Options approval order, as predating Dodd-Frank and therefore no longer load-bearing. The implication is that a contract on a reported number is an event contract, which would put it under the Commodity Futures Trading Commission.

The cause. Vantage, and it is not disreputable on either side. The Exchange is a securities exchange and wants its products inside its own rulebook, where it already has clearing, position limits and a disclosure regime. The objectors trade or study event contracts and have watched prediction markets grow under a different statute with different rules. Neither is reporting from a different set of facts; they are reading the same product against two statutes.

What it means for you. The Commission resolved it in its own favour and the reasoning is thin in one place worth knowing: the Act does not define "option" at all, so the Commission reached for the Commodity Exchange Act's definition to argue these are options on securities, and said earnings metrics reference a core component of the shareholder's pecuniary interest. Read that as a jurisdictional claim staked rather than a question settled. What would settle it is a CFTC response or a petition for review; the thing to watch is whether the CFTC — which this brief has reported operating with a single voting member — says anything at all.

American shares will trade 23 hours a day from 6 December, and this morning the exchanges filed the rules for the other 14

US financials Macro & markets New thread Underreported Primary source

Two filings in this morning's Federal Register, from two different exchanges, carry the same date in them: 23-hour, five-day trading is scheduled to commence industry-wide on 6 December 2026. That is seven weeks away. The date has sat in this brief's dates-ahead list for weeks as "the LULD overnight change becomes operative"; these are the first documents to say what it means.

The Long-Term Stock Exchange is amending its clearly-erroneous-executions rule "in light of the Commission's approval of Overnight Protected Bands" under the limit-up limit-down plan — the price collar that stops a trade printing far from the market. It did not exist overnight because the market did not. The plan's operating committee filed Amendment 27 to create it, the Commission approved it, and LTSE's change becomes operative when 23/5 trading starts.

Nasdaq's filing describes the shape of the day. Trading hours now run 4:00am to 8:00pm Eastern. From December there is also a Night Session, 9:00pm to 4:00am, and the rules say the systems behind the day and night sessions are technically and functionally separate, with quoting held briefly in abeyance at the handover so the public tape is not sent overlapping data. Its substance is narrow: how a newly listed exchange-traded product opens, and how a halted one reopens, when the halt can now fall at two in the morning.

That is the content of this story. Nobody is debating whether overnight trading is a good idea; the decision is made and the date is fixed. What is being written now, one filing at a time and with no coverage, is what happens when something goes wrong at 2am: which price is erroneous, which band applies, who opens the book, and whether the tape knows which session it is in.

What to watch: filings from the remaining exchanges before 6 December, because each needs its own halt, auction and erroneous-trade rules; whether any of them is published for comment rather than taking effect immediately; and the first overnight halt.

Ukraine's drones lost their satellite link, and Musk says Washington set the limit

Geopolitics AI & tech industry New thread Independent corroboration

Overnight on 13–14 October a speed cap took effect on the SpaceX satellite terminals Ukraine's military uses for battlefield communications and drone control. Ukrainian officials say they had no warning. The service now stops working above a ground speed, and the two available figures for that speed do not match: a Ukrainian official told Reuters the military-grade link fails above 100mph, while Ukraine's 7th Rapid Reaction Corps reported drones dropping out for ten to twenty seconds at speeds as low as 68 to 75mph. Fast-flying drones simply lost their link.

SpaceX has published no explanation. Elon Musk said on 14 October that the speed limits on Ukraine's Starshield terminals are set by the US military, Trump included, and not by his company. Reuters reported on 15 October, citing two US officials and a Western official, that the instruction originated in the White House, and that Defense Department leadership was told the service for the Ukrainian military had to be slowed. None of them would say who in the White House decided, or who carried it out.

The timing matters. It lands in the week the President again publicly demanded Ukraine stop striking Russian refineries, the next story in this issue. Starlink does not operate inside Russia, so throttling it cannot stop a strike directly — but it degrades precisely the long-range drone operations that carry those strikes out.

Note what this is: a privately owned constellation, serving a foreign military, altered without public notice on an instruction nobody will put a name to. The company says the government decided; unnamed government officials say the same. No one has produced the order.

What to watch: any named official or written direction — this stays an unattributed account until one appears; whether the cap is lifted or widened; and whether the figure settles at 100mph or at the lower number the Ukrainian unit reported, which are different capabilities.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Two disputes, one factual and one about weight. The factual one is the threshold: 100mph from a Ukrainian official speaking to Reuters, against 68–75mph from the unit flying the drones. The weight dispute is whether this is a commercial engineering change or an act of US policy — and that turns on a document nobody has.

The cause. On the threshold, the mundane explanation is almost certainly right and is not a contradiction: a cap set at 100mph will begin degrading a link well below it, because a terminal on an airframe is handing off between satellites and loses lock before it hits the hard limit. One number is the specification, the other is the experience. On attribution, both the company and the anonymous officials are pointing away from themselves, which is an unusual alignment and a reason to take it seriously rather than less so — neither party benefits from naming Washington, and Musk in particular has been blamed for exactly this before.

What it means for you. Treat the throttling as real and deliberate, treat its origin in the US government as well supported but formally unestablished, and treat 68–75mph as the operational number because it is the one measured in use. What would settle the origin is a written directive, a named official, or a congressional letter forcing an answer — and that is the observable to watch, not further rounds of unnamed sourcing.

Single-sourced for the White House origin: that rests on Reuters' unnamed officials, reached here through Handelsblatt's live file, and no primary document was read. Two things moved on this brief's longest-running thread, and they belong in one story because they are one causal chain.

This is not new and that is the point. Axios reported the same request made privately on a 20 September call; he made it publicly in Ireland, and again beside Zelensky at the UN General Assembly, where he said the strikes had dealt Russia a serious blow and moved diesel prices. Four reiterations in four weeks is a position, not a remark.

Meanwhile the price. AAA's national average this morning: diesel $6.3261, up 0.88 cents overnight and a fourth consecutive daily rise; regular petrol $4.3271, down 0.73 cents on the day and 4.5 cents on the week. The two are moving apart, and the diesel premium is now $1.999 — effectively two dollars, and the widest of the entire move. Diesel is up 73.4% on the year against petrol's 41.5%.

What to watch: whether the premium holds above $2.00, which is the number that passes into freight rates and then into shelf prices; the next EIA weekly print on Tuesday; and whether any of the public pressure on Kyiv becomes a condition rather than a complaint.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. The two instruments this brief uses for diesel now point in opposite directions, and both are right. AAA's daily card average has risen four days running. EIA's weekly survey has fallen two weeks running, from $6.199 to $6.179. A reader looking at one and then the other would think someone had made an error.

The cause. Not interest or access — measurement. A daily card-reader average measures today; a weekly survey measures a seven-day period ending Monday, and its latest reading covers the week to 12 October, before the four rises happened. The weekly series cannot re-accelerate on a Thursday and the daily series cannot describe a trend. This brief called a trend turn off the daily series on 21 September and had it undone the next morning; the rule written then is the rule that resolves this now.

What it means for you. Believe both, for different questions. For the level you pay, the daily number: diesel is near $6.33 and rising. For direction over the move, the weekly: the decline from the September record is real but has almost stopped, two cents in the latest week against fourteen in the one before. The observation that settles it is Tuesday's EIA print, which is the first weekly reading that will contain this week's rises — and this brief owes the reader a correction from 15 October for naming a release date without checking its calendar, so: EIA's retail price page publishes Tuesdays, and the next reading is due 20 October.

Treasury designated an Iraqi bank and a Cuban one, and quietly widened two licences for Lukoil

Geopolitics US financials Continuation Primary source

The Office of Foreign Assets Control published an action dated 15 October under the banner "Operation Economic Outcast", aimed at what Treasury calls the Iranian regime's abuse of religious charity networks. Sixteen individuals and eleven entities were added to the Specially Designated Nationals list — ten under the Iran authority, one under Cuba.

Four of the eleven are banks or exchange houses, and that is the part with reach. Chabahar International Bank Overseas and Jazireh International Bank, both Iranian. Two exchange companies in Qom and Tehran, one of them registered as recently as November 2024. And, critically, the International Development Bank for Investment and Finance in Baghdad — an Iraqi commercial bank, with a SWIFT code, designated along with a wind-down licence (Iran-related General License FF) to unwind existing business. Separately, Banco Internacional de Comercio, a Cuban state-owned bank in Havana, was designated under the Cuba authority.

Designating an Iraqi bank is the consequential act. Iraq's banking system runs on dollars supplied through an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, a direct lever on Iraqi finance Washington has nowhere else in the region. A wind-down licence rather than an immediate block says Treasury expects correspondent relationships unwound, not frozen.

Issued the same day, and much less remarked: Russia-related General License 128D, authorising transactions involving Lukoil retail service stations located outside Russia, and General License 130B, authorising transactions involving certain Lukoil entities in Bulgaria. Both are successive amendments — the letters say so. The United States is continuing to carve out the European downstream of a sanctioned Russian oil company while, as the diesel story above records, publicly complaining about the price of fuel.

What to watch: whether any Iraqi or Gulf correspondent bank cuts the Baghdad bank off before the licence expires; the licences' own expiry dates, which were not read here; and whether a 128E or 130C follows, which is how this thread has moved all month.

EPA exempted far more refineries than it forecast, and proposes to hand the bill to the next two years

Energy & grid US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

In this morning's proposed rules, the Environmental Protection Agency admits a forecasting miss and proposes two ways to absorb it. When it set renewable fuel volumes on 1 April 2026, it projected that 2025 small refinery exemptions would remove 990 million renewable identification numbers of obligation. The exemptions it then granted removed 1.76 billion. The gap is 770 million RINs.

The petition tally is in the document: for 2025 the agency granted full exemptions to 18 petitions, partial exemptions to 11, denied three and found two ineligible. Thirty-two petitions, twenty-nine of them granted in whole or part.

This matters because of how the programme works. An exemption does not merely excuse one refinery — it frees up carryover credits that would otherwise have been retired, and those credits then satisfy somebody else's obligation in a later year. EPA's own stated purpose for reallocating is to stop the surplus from "removing the market incentives for actual production and use of renewable fuels" in 2026 and 2027. Left alone, the exemptions would quietly reduce how much biofuel actually has to be blended.

The two co-proposed options are to split the 770 million between the 2026 and 2027 reallocation volumes, or to put all of it into 2027. EPA also says the proposal may extend the 2026 compliance reporting deadline beyond 31 March 2027. Comments close 4 December 2026; a virtual hearing is set for 4 November, with registration by 28 October.

What to watch: which option EPA takes, because front-loading into 2026 raises obligations on parties already deep into the compliance year; whether the compliance deadline slips; and the RIN price, which is the market's own reading of all this and which this brief has not been tracking.

The underlying Set 2 Rule of 1 April 2026 and the exemption decisions themselves were not read. Sources: EPA, Reallocation of Additional 2025 Small Refinery Exemptions, docket EPA-HQ-OAR-2024-0505

351 of Florida's 368 local dengue cases are in three Tampa Bay counties, and not one of them began in October

Florida & Tampa Bay Science & public health Continuation Primary source

This brief has owed the reader a proper reading of the Florida outbreak for nine days, because the weekly state surveillance report is a PDF and until last week this session could not read one. It can now. From the Department of Health's Week 40 arbovirus report, covering 4–10 October:

368 locally acquired dengue cases in 2026, from eight counties, with 72 reported in this week alone. The county breakdown, all of it from this single report: Hillsborough 310, Pinellas 37, Pasco 4, Miami-Dade 9, Orange 5, plus Broward, Palm Beach and Polk. Hillsborough, Pinellas, Pasco, Orange and Miami-Dade are under a mosquito-borne illness alert. Separately there were 288 travel-associated cases, 254 of them serotyped, 221 acquired in the Americas.

Now the number that changes the story. The report gives onset months for all 368: July 21, August 159, September 188. That sums to the total, so no case in this report began in October. The 72 "reported this week" are earlier illnesses arriving late in the surveillance system — reporting catch-up, not an October acceleration. Anyone reading the weekly figure as this week's infections is reading it wrong.

Three counties hold 95% of the state's local transmission. That total is a legitimate sum because it comes from one report describing one moment; on 3 October this brief added three counties' figures published on three different days, which was wrong. This is the corrected method, not a corrected number.

One more instrument: 39 sentinel chickens tested positive for West Nile antibodies this week across fifteen counties including Pasco and Pinellas, 316 for the year from 27. A caged chicken that seroconverts was bitten by an infected mosquito at that location — it reads the mosquito population directly, and it leads human cases.

What to watch: the Week 41 report, which is the first that can carry October onsets and therefore the first real test of whether transmission is still running; and the local emergencies, below.

Standing gap, stated plainly. The Hillsborough and Pinellas local states of emergency were last extended, by orders this brief could find, to 15 October — yesterday. Florida law caps such a declaration at seven days unless renewed. No further order was reachable this morning: the county newsroom URL pattern that carried the 8 October extension returns 404 for every date from 13 to 16 October, and no reporting found says either county extended again. That is a statement about what could be read, not about what is in force. The emergency may well have been renewed; this brief could not confirm it either way and will not infer a lapse from a missing document.

Energy & grid The second fuel-policy instrument in this morning's issue: Energy has opened the door for fuels with no published emissions rate.

The Department of Energy's Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation published a notice of availability for its section 45Z Emissions Value Request Process. Section 45Z is the Clean Fuel Production Credit, created by the Inflation Reduction Act and amended by the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act. The credit's size depends on the fuel's lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions rate. A producer either uses the published emissions rate table or, if its fuel's type, feedstock or pathway is not on that table, petitions Treasury for a provisional emissions rate. This process is how DOE supplies the emissions value that petition needs.

Read it alongside the EPA proposal above. In one morning, the federal government moved on both sides of the biofuel ledger: the blending obligation that creates demand, and the tax credit that pays for supply — the second one specifically to accommodate fuels nobody has yet classified.

Below the line because it is a notice that a process exists, with no rate, no deadline and no identified petitioner.

The International Trade Administration published three final determinations on high purity dissolving pulp: sales at less than fair value from Norway, sales at less than fair value from Brazil, and an affirmative countervailing duty determination on Brazil. All three are affirmative.

Dissolving pulp is the feedstock for viscose and rayon, for cellulose acetate, and for the cellulosic chemistry behind films and filters. It is a genuine input industry, which is what makes an antidumping order on it a cost pass-through to US textile and speciality chemical producers rather than a win for them. The duty rates were not read here, and they are the whole economic content of the determination.

Separately, the International Trade Commission scheduled the final phase of countervailing and antidumping investigations on a rubber accelerator chemical from China.

US politics & policy The rewrite of historic preservation review is still sitting at OMB, which is why there is no story yet.

NPR reported this morning that US historic preservation law faces its most serious threat in sixty years. The underlying action: on 24 July 2026 the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation voted 15–5 with two abstentions to advance a rewrite of the Section 106 regulations at 36 CFR Part 800 — the first amendment in over two decades.

What practitioners describe: consultation steps replaced by an agency-prepared Section 106 report; Section 106 characterised as purely procedural; agencies free to alter any regulatory deadline; "avoid, minimize, or mitigate" adverse effects softened to "address" them; the area of potential effects narrowed to direct material alterations; and "historic property" redefined to require a physical location.

But it is a draft at the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, not a published rule. This brief checked: there is no notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register, no docket and no comment period. Current regulations remain in force.

Below the line because there is no document to read — and a rule at OIRA is exactly the state in which this brief's own rules say to wait.
