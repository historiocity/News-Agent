Democrats have taken both chambers

US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

Democrats have flipped control of the House and the Senate. As of about 2 a.m. eastern they had picked up seven Senate seats — in battlegrounds including North Carolina and Maine, and in the red states of Iowa, Ohio, Texas, Kansas and South Carolina — along with several governorships, and won multiple House seats that had been redrawn to favour Republicans.

Counting continues and this brief is written before dawn, so no seat total is given here as final. The two chambers changing hands is reported by the outlet read for this issue as called; individual races are not, and the one this brief cares most about is in the next story.

The campaign's own framing makes the reading hard to avoid. At the rallies he held through swing and red states in the final weeks, the President told supporters: "Pretend I'm on the ballot." Late on Tuesday he posted: "I wasn't on the Ballot and if I was, we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE! Thank you for your attention to this matter." Just after midnight he told Fox News: "I don't view it as a hit on me. I think we've done a, we've had the greatest two years in the history of the presidency in terms of the economy, in terms of all the things that we've done."

Two numbers from before the vote are the ones this brief has been circling for six weeks without connecting them. Roughly two-thirds of Americans told AP-NORC in September that they held the President responsible for higher-than-usual prices, and his approval has been running below 40 per cent. This brief has published the diesel price every morning since September and established on Saturday that it is set by an international refining margin. It did not once write that the price was also a political fact.

Hakeem Jeffries, now poised to become the first Black Speaker of the House, opened his remarks with "America is back" and promised "transformational change" focused on affordability — housing, education, healthcare, retirement. Speaker Mike Johnson, in Shreveport, said he "left it all on the field" and that "to keep the House and sustain our majority we'd have to defy more than a century of midterm history". Representative Robert Garcia, incoming chairman of House Oversight, issued a two-word statement on the new majority: "Lawyer up."

What to watch: the final seat counts, which will take days; whether the Senate margin is large enough to matter for nominations; and the January handover, when a veto becomes the administration's main instrument.

Confidence: Independent corroboration, on an incomplete count. Control of both chambers, the seven Senate pickups and all quotations are NPR's reporting as of about 2 a.m. eastern; this brief did not reach the Associated Press, any state election authority, or a results feed of its own, and reports no seat totals because none it could verify were final. The AP-NORC figure is as cited by NPR. Sources: NPR · NPR on Jeffries · 2 November, which set the terms for this story

In Alaska the margin is 2,071 votes and the spoiler got more than that

US politics Courts Continuation Independent corroboration

With 313 of 401 precincts reporting, Senator Dan Sullivan has 48.5 per cent of first-choice votes and Mary Peltola has 47.5 per cent — a gap of 2,071 votes out of 205,474 cast. The independent candidate with the nearly identical name, Dan J. Sullivan, has about 1.7 per cent.

That is roughly 3,500 votes, which is more than the gap between the two leading candidates. It is the precise factual situation the Justice Department's lawsuit was built around, and the suit no longer exists: Judge Sharon Gleason threw it out on Sunday and the Supreme Court declined to intervene in the California companion case on Monday evening.

Two things make the next fortnight unusually consequential. Alaska counts by ranked choice, so the result is not expected until mid-November, after the second-choice tabulation — which is also when Dan J. Sullivan's voters' second preferences get distributed, and they decide the seat. And the precincts not yet reporting are largely rural, which is where Peltola's support is concentrated.

Before any result was known, Republican officials said they would challenge the legality of a Peltola victory, citing the New York Times reporting that Democratic campaigners recruited the independent Sullivan. That reporting remains, as this brief said on 30 October, single-sourced upstream and unconfirmed by the outlet relaying it.

What this brief got wrong along the way, since it has been wrong about this man's name twice. He has appeared here as "Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg" and as "Daniel Sullivan Jr."; today's sources render him Dan J. Sullivan. The brief does not know which is correct and should have established it on day one of a story that is entirely about a name.

Separately and today: the grand jury subpoenas served on Los Angeles registrar staff are returnable this morning. Nothing in the four court rulings touched them, and the Department's letters warning county employees about "aiding and abetting" have not been withdrawn.

What to watch: whether the subpoenas are honoured, withdrawn or quashed today; the ranked-choice tabulation in mid-November; and whether a challenge is actually filed if Peltola wins.

The FDIC wants to delete the rule that makes big-bank depositors payable fast

Macro & markets Regulatory New subject Primary document Underreported

When a bank fails on a Friday, the reason insured depositors can usually be paid by Monday is that the FDIC made large banks build the plumbing in advance: systems that can identify, at close of business on any day, exactly which depositor holds what and how much of it is insured. A proposed rule published this morning would remove the recordkeeping regulation for timely deposit insurance determination altogether.

The proposal has three parts. It would rescind the recordkeeping rule; revise the large-bank deposit insurance determination rule so that institutions currently covered by the rescinded one fall under a modified version of it, "with certain modifications", and with its requirements clarified "to better align the burdens of the rule with the benefits"; and index the regulatory thresholds to inflation, so fewer institutions are captured over time without anyone deciding so.

"Align the burdens with the benefits" is the operative phrase and it is doing a lot of work. The burden of the recordkeeping rule is continuous, expensive and borne by banks that will mostly never fail. The benefit is discontinuous, arrives only in a failure, and is borne by depositors and by the deposit insurance fund. A cost-benefit comparison between a certain cost and a contingent one will favour removing the cost, every time, right up until the contingency happens.

The timing invites a comparison this brief will state and not resolve. The 2023 regional bank failures were the live test of exactly this machinery, and a great deal of the subsequent supervisory argument was about how quickly institutions could produce reliable depositor data under pressure. Whether this proposal reflects a judgement that the lesson was learned and the rule is now redundant, or a judgement that the cost was never worth it, is in the preamble, which this brief did not read.

Comments close 4 January 2027, RIN 3064-AG35 — a two-month window, which is long by the standards of what this brief has logged in the past fortnight.

What to watch: which institutions fall out of scope under the inflation indexing, which is the quiet structural change; whether the FDIC's own analysis estimates a resolution-speed cost; and whether any of the three FDIC board seats dissents.

Confidence: Primary document, summary, dates and docket only. Published today and read at source; the preamble, the regulatory analysis and the proposed text were not read, so this brief cannot say what the "certain modifications" are, which is most of the rule. The description of what deposit-determination recordkeeping is for, and the 2023 comparison, are general knowledge and not from the document. Sources: FR 2026-22495 · GovInfo PDF

The refunding is today, and the curve has not moved

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Continuation Primary source

Tuesday's Treasury print, read while the polls were still open: two-year 4.61 per cent, up 2 basis points; ten-year 4.95, up 4; twenty-year 5.35, up 4; thirty-year 5.32, up 4. The twenty-minus-ten gap is +40 basis points, unchanged from Monday.

This brief has spent a month watching that gap and was wrong about it yesterday, in print. What can be said now, with the full October series pulled: it opened the month at +40, reached +43 on 7 October, fell to +34 on 21 October, and has been at +40 for two sessions. That is the whole claim. It is a round trip that has returned to where it started, and nothing in 23 business days establishes whether that is a feature of the curve or a month of noise.

Today is the quarterly refunding announcement — the event every long-end story in this brief since early October has pointed at, and the first one to land under a Congress that changed hands overnight. The announcement sets the size of the coming quarter's note and bond auctions, and the long end is where any increase lands hardest.

What a changed Congress does to that is not knowable this morning and this brief will not pretend otherwise. The refunding schedule is set by the Treasury, not by Congress; what a Democratic House and Senate change is the fiscal path two years out, which is the horizon the twenty- and thirty-year parts of the curve price. Whether anything in today's announcement reflects last night is a question for whoever reads it at 8:30.

At the pump, another record: diesel $6.2748, down 1.27 cents; regular $4.1239, down 2.54. The spread is $2.1509, the highest this brief has recorded, on a day when both fuels fell by more than a cent.

What to watch: the refunding sizes at 8:30 this morning; whether the gap breaks +43, the only level this brief can honestly call a high for the period it has measured; and tomorrow's EIA release, for the crack spread that explained the fuel story on Saturday.

Confidence: Primary. Treasury and AAA read at source this morning; the hump series was recomputed yesterday for every business day since 1 October and is not characterised beyond that window. No commentary, dealer or Treasury official was consulted, and this brief has no view on the refunding sizes. Sources: Treasury Daily Par Yield Curve · AAA · 3 November, which corrected this thread

Method What a changed Congress does to the fifty-odd things this brief is tracking.

This brief carries a list of comment deadlines and effective dates running into 2027: the ACA verification proposal, the PBM disclosure rule, the accreditation rewrite, the section 504 change, the organ procurement rule, the methane reconsideration, two section 232 findings with 180-day clocks, and dozens more. Almost none of it is legislation, and almost none of it changes because the House and Senate changed hands.

Three mechanisms do change, and they are worth naming now rather than discovering later. The Congressional Review Act lets a new Congress disapprove recent rules by simple majority in both chambers — but a resolution still needs the President's signature or a two-thirds override, so a Democratic Congress cannot by itself undo this administration's rules. Oversight changes immediately and completely: subpoena power over agencies moves in January, and the incoming Oversight chairman's stated list includes DHS and ICE. Appropriations is the real lever, because almost every rule in that list depends on an agency being funded to implement it.

Below the line because it is this brief explaining its own filing system, and because the useful version of this story is written in January when a committee actually does something, not the morning after a result.

Confidence: this brief's own tracking list; the CRA mechanics are general knowledge and were not verified against the statute this morning.

Regulatory The NCUA trio this brief has owed for a month arrived, and it is housekeeping.

Since early October the owed list here has carried "the NCUA trio" — three credit union rules identified and never read. All three published this morning, and having now read them, the honest report is that there is not much there.

Statutory Lien removes a provision on federal credit unions' statutory lien authority, finalised as proposed, on the reasoning that case law covers it and removal "does not diminish an FCU's obligation to determine… if there are other laws that apply". Member Business Loans; Commercial Lending is an interim final rule eliminating "an outdated, transitional provision that is no longer necessary", effective today. The third concerns the treatment of emergency amendments relating to the Paycheck Protection Program Liquidity Facility — a 2020 programme.

This is the negative result and it is recorded rather than quietly dropped, which is the standing rule here for a cluster that turns out routine. A month of carrying three documents on an owed list produced three deletions of dead text. The cost of that is small; the cost of never checking would have been carrying them for another month.

Below the line because housekeeping is housekeeping. The trio comes off the owed list.

US politics The demographic breakdown exists and this brief has not opened it.

A survey of tens of thousands of voters, conducted by SSRS for a consortium of news organisations including the Associated Press, has produced the standard crosstabs on how demographic groups voted for House candidates. The published margin of error on the full sample is plus or minus 0.6 percentage points.

That is an unusually large sample and a correspondingly tight overall margin, and it is the document that will be cited for the next two years about who moved and why. It is also the document most likely to be cited wrongly, because the margin of error on the full sample says nothing about the margin on a subgroup, and subgroups are the only reason anyone reads it.

Below the line because this brief read the methodology note and not the charts — it is today's negative check. Reporting a crosstab without the subgroup sample sizes is the error this brief would most likely make if it hurried, and it is not going to make it at 4 a.m. on an incomplete count.

Confidence: methodology read, findings not. Sources: NPR

Macro & markets Treasury has scheduled a hearing on what Trump Accounts may invest in.

Treasury has published a notice of hearing on its proposed guidance covering eligible investments for Trump Accounts — the tax-advantaged accounts for children created in this administration's tax legislation. This brief has carried the comment deadline on its dates-ahead list since October.

A hearing notice on an investment-eligibility rule is narrow and it is where the money is decided. What counts as an eligible investment determines which asset managers can sell into the accounts, and for a programme intended to be universal that is a large and permanent flow. The fights in rules of this kind are usually about index breadth, fee caps and whether anything other than broad-market funds qualifies.

Below the line because a notice of hearing is a calendar entry, and this brief read the title and not the underlying guidance. The substance is in the proposed rule, which remains unread here and is on the owed list.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: FR 2026-22506

Energy & grid The fuel spread set another record while both fuels fell more than a cent.

Diesel $6.2748, down 1.27 cents. Regular $4.1239, down 2.54. The spread is $2.1509 — the widest this brief has recorded, on a morning when every grade got cheaper.

That combination is the signature of the finding established on Saturday: the diesel price is anchored to an international refining margin, so when the whole barrel cheapens the petrol side moves first and further. Regular petrol has now fallen on eight consecutive days, from $4.2957 on 24 October to $4.1239 today — 17.2 cents, or 4 per cent, in eleven days. Diesel over the same stretch is down 6.3 cents, or 1 per cent.

The consumer story in that is the one this brief kept not writing until last night. Petrol is what most people buy and it is falling quickly; diesel is what moves everything else and it is barely moving. A household sees the first; the price of what the household buys reflects the second.

Below the line because it is one more reading in a series whose explanation is settled. Tomorrow's EIA release carries the crack spread and the export figure, which are the numbers that would show the margin closing.

Confidence: Primary, read at source; the 24 October comparison is from this brief's own published record. Sources: AAA · 31 October
