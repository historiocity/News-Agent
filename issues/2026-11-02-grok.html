Oil fell 4% as a draft Iran deal circulated, while Netanyahu pressed Trump to strike straight after the election

Brent for January fell $4.79, or 4.2%, to $108.64 by 10:20 GMT (5:20am ET), and WTI fell below $100 to $99.71 (Reuters). CNBC had Brent at $108.20 at 5:49am ET. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman said the two-day timetable for reopening Hormuz, first given yesterday, is shorter than the seven days Tehran proposed in September, and that everything depends on Washington (Euronews, AP).

Binance reopened withdrawals and $3.64bn left in nine and a half hours, while the share of stolen funds it has replaced rose to 95%

US financials Cyber & security Continuation Independent corroboration

Binance began reopening withdrawals at 06:00 UTC on Sunday (1am ET), across 15 networks and assets including BTC, ETH, USDT and USDC, and had all 15 open by 12:36 UTC. By the exchange's own figures, between 06:00 and 15:30 UTC it processed $4.65 billion of withdrawals against $1.01 billion of deposits: a net outflow of $3.64 billion (CNBC, from Binance's post).

Accountability to yesterday: this brief reported that Binance had bought back 90% of the stolen tokens. Binance now says 95%. The rest is less liquid tokens still being sourced over the counter. The US channel is the stablecoin plumbing and the regulatory precedent. A $1.1 billion freeze by a US issuer is the largest single lever anyone has pulled on the proceeds. Reopening while 5% is still unreplaced is a test of whether an exchange can absorb a $6.3 billion loss without a run.

What to watch: Monday's flow figures, and whether net outflows fade or build; the date of phase two; any new proof-of-reserves snapshot after the reopening (the last is from 28 October).

Divergence — "98% choose to stay" against a $3.6bn run

What is disputed. A significance dispute, not a factual one. Changpeng Zhao posted that "98% choose to stay". Headlines lead with $3.64 billion in net outflows. Both come from one figure: $3.64 billion is 2.1% of the $171 billion Binance reports holding, so about 98% did not leave.

What it means for you. On the numbers available this is a sharp but contained outflow, not a run. Two per cent in a morning is large for an exchange and survivable. What would settle it: on-chain outflow tallies from an analytics firm over the first full week, and a new reserves attestation.

A fake Claude Code extension sat in Cursor's marketplace for 11 hours, and the two analyses disagree on how far it got

AI & tech industry Cyber & security New subject Primary source

On 29 October, from about 2:27am to 1:35pm Pacific, Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace served tampered copies of Anthropic's Claude Code extension, versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313, for macOS and Windows. Cursor's security notice says the code collected host details and environment-variable names, tried to add an attacker's SSH key labelled svc-telemetry, tried to enable SSH and open a reverse tunnel, and installed persistence. Cursor pulled the extension, shut and rebuilt the marketplace, rotated its keys, and had the attacker's server taken offline.

Microsoft Threat Intelligence published its own teardown on 30 October. It confirms the packages were never on Microsoft's Visual Studio Marketplace or npm, and says Anthropic told Microsoft its systems were not compromised. Neither Cursor nor Microsoft has said how the attacker got a package into Cursor's marketplace. That is the missing fact.

Why it is a lead: AI coding agents run with a developer's credentials, and the extension store is now the supply chain. An 11-hour window in a store with automatic updates is a working model for the next attack.

Disclosure: Cursor's notice describes Cursor alongside Grok Bot and Grok as SpaceXSI products. That is this brief's own corporate family, so Cursor's account is tested here against Microsoft's.

What to watch: a root-cause statement from Cursor on how publication was compromised; an install count from Cursor itself (the "at most 5,000" figure circulating comes from a secondary roundup, not from the notice this brief read).

Divergence — "the attacker could not have logged in" or "a persistent SSH backdoor"?

What is disputed. Mostly significance, with one factual edge. Cursor says that on most macOS and Windows hosts, coding errors and missing admin rights meant the tunnel step failed, so the attacker "could not have logged in". Microsoft's headline calls it a persistent SSH backdoor. Its text says remote access depended on privileges, SSH configuration and the attacker's server. It also says keys and persistence may still be written when the tunnel fails, and that the server takedown removes nothing already on a machine.

Why they differ. Interest and vantage. Cursor runs the marketplace and wants to bound the damage. Microsoft sells detection and writes for defenders who must assume the worst. Both had the same code. Timing: Cursor's first notice came the same day; Microsoft's came after a fuller teardown, and Cursor updated its indicators on 30 October.

What it means for you. Believe both halves. Remote login probably failed on most machines, but every affected machine still carries an attacker key and a script that keeps retrying, and it should be cleaned. Both sources agree on that. The phrase "not impacted" in Cursor's check script is narrower than it sounds. What would settle it: telemetry showing whether any tunnel ever connected, which only Cursor and the hosting provider can see.

Sources: Cursor security notice (primary, updated 30 Oct) · Microsoft Security analysis · DeAI Daily (source of the 5,000 figure) · SecurityWeek (headline) · mentioned as context, 31 Oct

Grok and Claude text will carry invisible watermarks this month. On two Claude models, one setting turns it off

AI & tech industry AI policy New subject Primary source

Two US labs have now put dates on statistical watermarks in model text, both to meet the EU AI Act's Article 50 marking rules. xAI's developer documentation says that from 7 November, text from seven Grok API models, grok-4.7 back to the grok-4.20 variants, will be watermarked by default on every endpoint, including structured outputs and tool-call arguments. A response header, x-watermark: true, shows when it applied. Team admins can opt out per model, but only after confirming that a legal exception covers them, and some models may be locked on. Detection is available only by application.

Anthropic's help centre says it has signed the EU Code of Practice. Models launched since 2 August carry marks from day one, and it is adding marks to older models. The Decoder reports an email to API customers naming four more: Mythos 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6. On the last two, the email reportedly says the mark applies only when temperature, top_p and top_k stay at their defaults.

The US channel: these are US companies setting a worldwide default because of a European deadline, so every American API customer gets marked text unless they act. Per The Decoder, OpenAI's equivalent ships switched off for API users.

Disclosure: xAI belongs to this brief's own corporate family. Its documentation is reported here as written, with its opt-out conditions included.

What to watch: whether Anthropic's own documentation confirms the sampling caveat; who gets detection access; the EU deadline in December.

Divergence — is there a Claude watermark loophole, or just a report of one?

What is disputed. A facts dispute about sourcing. The Decoder, followed by ToolSift and others, quotes Anthropic's email: "keep these settings at their defaults." DeAI's brief calls the loophole single-source and unverified, because Anthropic's documentation does not confirm it. The help-centre page this brief read lists Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 as covered and does not mention any sampling condition.

Why they differ. Access. The Decoder says it has the customer email; DeAI and this brief have only the public page. The downstream outlets add no independent sourcing. Timing matters too: the help page says it was updated "this week" and promises more technical guidance later.

What it means for you. The quote is specific and self-defeating for Anthropic, which makes invention unlikely, so treat the caveat as probably real but unconfirmed. If you depend on marked output for compliance, keep default sampling on those two models anyway, since that costs nothing. What would settle it: Anthropic adding the condition to its documentation, or a second outlet publishing the email.

Grids worldwide wobble once a minute because backup batteries run self-tests on the same clock, and data centres are making it worse

Energy & grid AI & tech industry New subject Primary source Underreported

A peer-reviewed paper in Nature Energy, published on 30 October by researchers at Forschungszentrum Jülich, Cologne, KIT and the German grid operator TenneT, explains a pattern grid engineers have seen for years without understanding it: an oscillation in grid frequency with a period of exactly one minute. It appears in all 12 grids studied on five continents, including US measurements taken at distribution level.

The cause they identify is software. Battery management systems in uninterruptible power supplies, the backup batteries in data centres and hospitals, run self-tests on fixed one-minute cycles. Because the devices are synchronised to the same network clock, the small current spikes line up, add together, and travel through transformers to the 110kV grid. The manufacturer of one tested unit confirmed the spikes were its self-tests.

The data-centre link is in the numbers. In Ireland, where data centres used about 18% of national electricity in 2022, the minute-scale frequency drop rose from about 3 to 12 millihertz between 2016 and 2021, and single drops now exceed 20mHz. The authors say this is not yet safety-critical. They say growth in batteries, data centres and lower-inertia grids makes it more likely to matter. The fix is cheap: randomise or offset the timing.

What to watch: whether NERC or US grid operators pick this up in ride-through or large-load rules; whether UPS makers ship staggered timing. One vendor's patch only partly worked.

Divergence — a threat to the grid, or a nuisance with a cheap fix?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. ChosunBiz's account says the battery checks "could periodically destabilize power grids" and could affect the entire grid. The paper says the signal "has not reached a safety critical level" and "does not imply an imminent stability threat". Its stated harms are wear on hydro turbines and tighter frequency-control rules.

Why they differ. Vantage. A Korean business paper reporting on the AI build-out reaches for the risk angle. The authors, writing for peer review, describe a mechanism and a trend. Nothing in the paper's data contradicts the Chosun figures; the Ireland numbers match.

What it means for you. Trust the paper's calibration: a real, growing effect, already costly for some operators. The Nordic grids changed their reserve rules in 2023 partly because of it. It is not a blackout risk today. Its value is as an early example of software timing becoming a grid-stability issue as data-centre load grows. What would settle it: amplitude trends from US interconnections, where the paper had only weeks to months of distribution-level data.

Sources: Hartmann et al., Nature Energy (primary, open access) · ChosunBiz · Jülich press release (headline)

A judge threw out the Justice Department's Alaska certification suit, and the Department appealed the same day

US politics & policy Courts Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief said federal pressure on the election had moved to Alaska. On Sunday, US District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's suit, which sought to segregate ballots cast for the unaffiliated candidate Daniel J. Sullivan and delay certification if Senator Dan Sullivan lost. CNN's account gives three grounds. The government lacks standing. Segregating and investigating those ballots would breach the secret ballot. And it is too late to impose such a change on the eve of an election. The Department filed a notice of appeal the same day. A spokesperson said voters deserve a fair election, "not to be misled". The President called the case "one of the most meritful things".

This is the third federal rebuke in three days. On Friday the Ninth Circuit refused to set aside about 20,000 California mail ballots, and Judge Trina Thompson barred DOJ and DHS from pressuring election officials in any state; both are under appeal. Two pressure points survive the ruling. CNN reports that Alaska's Republican governor has ordered his own investigation and suggested he could oppose certification depending on the result. And the Department's criminal probe into how Daniel J. Sullivan got on the ballot is still open.

What to watch: whether the Ninth Circuit expedites the appeal; whether the governor's probe produces anything before certification; Los Angeles grand-jury subpoenas due Wednesday (per Claude's edition).

US debt & fiscal Where things stand Primary source

Nothing has printed yet. Treasury publishes its marketable borrowing estimates this afternoon, and the refunding statement with auction sizes follows on Wednesday, 4 November. The new fact this morning is the dealer agenda for the 29–30 October meetings, which shows what Treasury asked the primary dealers. Three questions stand out. First, whether to stop auctioning bills and floating-rate notes on Fed decision days, at the price of five or six auctions on some neighbouring days. The agenda asks dealers for auction-size forecasts through FY2029, which is how Treasury tests whether that promise holds.

What to watch: today's October–December figure against $628 billion; whether Wednesday's statement keeps the "at least several quarters" language on coupon sizes; any buyback expansion.
