A presidential committee will hold a four-hour hearing at the White House on whether to remove a Federal Reserve governor

Macro & markets US politics & policy New thread Primary source

A presidential memorandum of 7 October, published this morning at 91 FR 65435, establishes a committee of inquiry into allegations that Federal Reserve Governor Lisa D. Cook made false statements in connection with one or more mortgage instruments. Its task is to report whether there is “cause” for her removal under 12 U.S.C. 242, as construed in Trump v. Cook, 146 S. Ct. 2234 (2026). The committee is three people: the Assistant to the President for Economic Policy, the EEOC Chairman and the Director of the Office of Government Ethics.

The procedure is the story. Cook may file a written statement three days beforehand. The hearing is at the White House on 5 November 2026, no more than four hours, closed but transcribed. The committee must give her its evidence in advance; she must appear, may present argument, evidence and witnesses, may have counsel, and must produce documents it requests. She may file again by 10 November, after which the committee sends the President findings of fact, conclusions of law and a recommendation.

Read that against June. In Trump v. Cook, decided 29 June 5–4, the Court declined to let the removal proceed, holding that for-cause removal of a Fed governor is judicially reviewable and must be preceded by notice and an opportunity to respond, which Cook had not received. The same day, in Trump v. Slaughter, it held 6–3 that the President may remove other independent agency heads at will, overruling Humphrey’s Executor. The Federal Reserve was the single exception.

So the memorandum is not defiance of the Court but the opposite: the construction, item by item, of the process the Court said was missing. The stated basis is the take-care clause, Article II, section 3, which the memorandum says includes removing subordinates who cannot be trusted to tell the truth.

What to watch: whether Cook participates, since declining leaves the record one-sided and appearing legitimises the forum; whether the transcript and findings are published; and the date — two days after the midterm elections.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Nobody has written about this document yet, so the disagreement to adjudicate is the one between the memorandum and the judgment it cites. The Court required notice and an opportunity to respond and said the question is judicially reviewable. The memorandum supplies the first with unusual care. What it cannot supply is the second, and it does not try: a committee of three presidential appointees, two of whom serve at the President’s pleasure, recommending to the President whether the President has cause to remove someone. That is the live question and it is a legal one, not a political characterisation — whether process furnished by the removing party satisfies a requirement the Court imposed precisely because the removing party was the interested one.

Cause. The mundane reading is the correct one and it is not discreditable: the government lost a stay in June on a procedural ground, and the ordinary response to losing on process is to supply process. Doing so carefully is what counsel advises. Note what the memorandum avoids — it does not assert that the Court was wrong, does not invoke Slaughter to argue the Fed exception should fall, and does not attempt removal first and litigate after. It builds a record. That is the behaviour of a government that intends to win on the merits rather than to provoke. The timing is harder to read charitably or uncharitably: 5 November is two days after an election, which is either deliberate or the first available date after a month’s notice.

What it means for you. This is the most consequential thing in today’s issue and the slowest-moving. The Federal Reserve’s independence is not an abstraction to anyone holding a mortgage, a bond fund or a job — it is the reason American long-term interest rates have historically carried a lower political risk premium than those of countries where the central bank can be reorganised by the executive. The sixth story in this issue shows the 20-year Treasury at 5.60% with a hump in the curve that has lasted a week; that is the market pricing duration risk, and the single cleanest observable here is whether the long end moves on 5 November. If a governor can be removed through a four-hour hearing at the White House, the removal power is real whatever the statute says, and every future governor knows it. If the committee finds no cause, the process will have demonstrated the opposite. Either way the answer arrives in writing, in findings of fact and conclusions of law, and this brief will read them.

Confidence: Primary source, full text read. The account of Trump v. Cook and Trump v. Slaughter is from secondary legal summaries, not the slip opinions, and the June decision was a stay ruling rather than a merits judgment. The allegations are described as the memorandum describes them and are unproven. Sources: Memorandum of October 7, 2026, Establishment of a Committee of Inquiry To Investigate Allegations of False Statements by Lisa DeNell Cook, 91 FR 65435 · Faegre Drinker and Jones Day on the June decisions

Twelve senators, half of them Republicans, asked the President to rescind the Russian diesel licence — and the administration warned Kyiv it could cut off intelligence

Energy & grid Geopolitics US politics & policy Independent corroboration

On 13 October twelve senators wrote asking the President to withdraw from the Russian diesel arrangement, rescind General License 135, and use the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act. Six are Republicans: Collins, Cornyn, McConnell, Murkowski, Tillis and Armstrong; the letter was led by Senator Blumenthal. Their argument: the licence lifts sanctions on Russian diesel exports, funds the war, and would have a negligible effect on fuel prices.

On 10 October this brief read the licence and Public Law 117-109 against each other and reported that the statute bans importation of all Russian chapter 27 products, and that its only exit is a certification nobody has submitted. The Hill now reports the deal may conflict with a 2022 law barring such a purchase without notice to Congress.

The second half of the day is worse. Zelenskyy told NPR the administration warned it could cut off intelligence sharing with Ukraine unless Ukrainian forces stop long-range drone strikes on Russian refineries — messages sent, he said, to the military rather than to him, and not yet acted on. Asked whether the US had stopped sharing, the President said: “We are helping them and we are sharing intel, we want the war to end.” The White House did not address whether a threat was made. CNN reported the same on 11 October.

There is precedent: sharing was suspended in March 2025 after the Oval Office meeting and restored once Ukraine accepted a ceasefire proposal. The instrument is known to work and known to have been used.

And the price has turned. AAA diesel is $6.2997, up 2.10 cents — the largest single-day rise of the move, after 1.22 cents yesterday. Petrol fell 0.80 cents to $4.3333, a third consecutive fall. The two have moved in opposite directions for three days, which is not a crude story: crude moves both together. It is a distillate story.

What to watch: today’s EIA weekly petroleum status report, which carries distillate stocks and will say whether this is tightness or noise; 18 October, the Graham Act implementation deadline; and whether any intelligence actually stops, which Ukraine would not announce and Russia would demonstrate.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Two disputes, one resolvable. The first is whether the administration threatened to cut intelligence sharing: Zelenskyy says yes and names the channel (messages to the military, not to him); CNN reported it independently two days earlier; the President answered a different question — whether sharing has stopped — and the White House declined to address whether a threat was made. A denial of the present tense is not a denial of the warning, and nobody has issued one. The second dispute is the senators’ claim that the licence would have a negligible price effect. That is checkable and this brief checked it on 10 October: the announced 4.8 million tonnes is about ten days of American distillate consumption, spread over months, promised to world markets rather than to the United States.

Cause. The mundane explanation for the intelligence threat is the stated one: the President believes Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries are raising the price Americans pay for diesel, and intelligence is the only lever he holds that bites immediately. The senators’ interest is more varied than party — six Republicans signing a letter against their own President’s deal six days before a statutory deadline is a message about the Graham Act as much as about Russia, and McConnell and Murkowski have institutional reasons to defend a sanctions statute Congress passed last month. Note the timing on all sides: the letter lands five days before the implementation deadline, which is when leverage exists and after which it does not.

What it means for you. Watch the chart, not the diplomacy. Diesel and petrol have separated for three days and the gap is the widest in a week — which means whatever is moving the diesel price is not the crude market, and therefore not the Russian announcement either, since Russian barrels would arrive as product and loosen exactly this. The candidates are the Gulf shut-ins now unwinding, refinery maintenance season, and distillate inventories going into winter. Today’s EIA report answers that question this morning and costs nothing to read. On the policy side the thing to hold is narrow: the senators have asked for rescission, the statute they cite has a deadline on 18 October, and a national-interest waiver is available to the President on certification to Congress. If that waiver is filed, it will be the clearest statement yet of what this administration intends, and it will be a public document.

Confidence: Independent corroboration; the senators’ letter was not read in the original and the signatory list is as reported, with one outlet mislabelling party affiliations. The intelligence warning rests on Zelenskyy’s account plus prior CNN reporting; no document exists. Prices are primary, from AAA. Four immigration rules published on one morning, and the first took effect the day it appeared

US politics & policy New thread Underreported Primary source

Today’s Federal Register carries four DHS immigration items, three of them final rules effective immediately. Four in one morning is the repetition tell: a programme, not a filing.

Removal of Automatic Extensions for Certain Employment Authorization Documents (RIN 1615-AD05, 8 CFR 274a, effective 14 October) is the largest. Until last year a timely renewal filing automatically extended an expiring work permit for up to 540 days. An interim final rule of 30 October 2025 ended that, issued without prior notice and comment under the good-cause exception and effective immediately; today’s rule adopts it unchanged after 653 comments. Affected categories include L-2 spouses, adjustment-of-status applicants and pending asylum applicants. DHS’s own estimate: 20,000 to 33,000 work permits lapsing in eighteen months from May 2027, $312.1m in labour turnover costs, $138.1m in lost productivity, 1,700 to 2,800 employers affected. The stated justification is vetting, citing a June 2025 attack in Boulder by a man whose permit had been automatically extended.

Removing Deference to Prior USCIS Determinations of Eligibility (91 FR 65119, comments close 13 November) would delete 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), under which USCIS generally defers to its own prior approval on the same facts when adjudicating an extension. Remove it and every extension is adjudicated from scratch, however many times the petition has been approved.

Imposition and Collection of Civil Penalties for Certain Immigration-Related Violations (91 FR 65390, DHS and Justice jointly, effective today) finalises a June 2025 interim rule on penalties for failing to depart or improperly entering, with one change: late appeals may be accepted where documented mail delays were beyond the person’s control. And Removing 30-Day Refugee Termination Notice ends a notice period before refugee status is terminated.

Three of the four finalise rules first issued as interim final rules without notice and comment. That is the signature worth recording: the comment period happens after the rule is already operating, and the final rule adopts it unchanged.

What to watch: the 13 November comment file on deference, the only one of the four still open; whether any employer association litigates the automatic-extension rule now that it is final and reviewable; and whether the lapse estimate proves low, since it rests on filing volumes the rule itself changes.

The automatic-extension rule was read in full (the document runs to about 461,000 characters; the first 100,000 were read, covering the rule and its economic analysis, not all comment responses). The other three are from the Federal Register’s own abstracts and metadata, not read in full. A judge found the press ban both retaliatory and viewpoint discrimination, and made the order indefinite

US politics & policy Independent corroboration

US District Judge Timothy Kelly granted CNN, MS NOW and Politico a preliminary injunction on Tuesday 13 October, barring the administration from enforcing its revocation of their reporters’ hard passes while the case proceeds. It replaces the temporary order of 24 September with one that runs indefinitely.

The legal content is new. September’s order rested solely on due process. Tuesday’s finds the ban likely violated both the Fifth Amendment due process clause and the First Amendment — and on the First Amendment, that it was both retaliatory and viewpoint discrimination. That is the first time the court has reached the speech question. Kelly added that nobody should read the decision as suggesting the President’s concerns about accuracy and fairness in reporting are unfounded. The President had predicted he would lose and has said he will appeal.

Now hold it against Saturday. On 10 October the Justice Department’s Antitrust Division opened an investigation into whether five networks violated the Sherman Act by jointly suspending coverage of the President — a boycott that began as a response to the credential revocations. A court has now found those revocations likely unconstitutional retaliation. The antitrust theory therefore targets a collective response to conduct a federal judge has said was probably retaliation for speech.

That does not make the antitrust investigation unlawful. Group boycott doctrine does not have a “we were provoked” defence written into it, and the two proceedings are formally unrelated. But it is the fact that was missing on Saturday, and it changes how the sequence reads.

What to watch: whether the administration appeals to the DC Circuit, and whether it seeks a stay; whether the plaintiffs amend to add the antitrust probe as further retaliation, which would put both before Judge Kelly; and whether the Antitrust Division issues civil investigative demands now that the predicate conduct has been judicially characterised.

Confidence: Independent corroboration; the order was not read here and the quoted characterisations come from reporting rather than the opinion. Sources: MS NOW · The Hill · CNN on the September order · continues 11 October

Commerce has told customs officers how to measure “US content” in a Canadian or Mexican car

World economy Macro & markets New thread Underreported Primary source

The Bureau of Industry and Security has published guidance to US Customs and Border Protection on assessing “US content” in products of Canada and Mexico that qualify for preferential treatment under the USMCA, for the purpose of implementing tariffs under clause 2(D) of Proclamation 11032 of 1 June 2026. It is at 91 FR 65162 and is effective today.

The mechanism matters more than the document’s length. Under the proclamation, a USMCA-qualifying good from Canada or Mexico is tariffed on the portion that is not American — so the duty owed on a vehicle, an engine or a wiring harness turns entirely on how much of its value a customs officer counts as US content. The rate is set by proclamation; the bill is set by this guidance.

That is where the money is in integrated North American manufacturing. A vehicle assembled in Ontario or Coahuila contains American steel, American engines, American software and American design, and whether those count, at what valuation, and with what documentation decides whether an importer pays on 30% of the value or on 70%. Nothing in the proclamation answers those questions; guidance to the officers at the border does.

This brief did not read the guidance itself, only the notice announcing it, so the content-counting rules are not described here. That is a gap worth naming rather than papering over: the operative text is where the whole effect of this lives.

What to watch: whether the automakers or the Canadian and Mexican governments respond, since all three have spent a year arguing about exactly this measurement; whether CBP issues implementing instructions of its own; and whether any importer challenges a content determination, which is how the guidance would first be tested.

Confidence: Primary source for the notice, its citation and effective date, read from the Federal Register API. The guidance document itself was not read, so no content-counting methodology, threshold or documentation requirement is reported. The account of why it matters is this brief’s own. Sources: BIS, Guidance to U.S. Customs and Border Protection Regarding the Assessment of “U.S. Content”, 91 FR 65162

Against Friday 9 October that is the 10-year down 3 basis points, the 20-year down 5 and the 30-year down 4.

The shape held. The curve still peaks at twenty years and falls back at thirty — a 4-basis-point hump, against 5 on Friday. On 11 October this brief said a structural bid at the 30-year does not usually hold a hump of that size for weeks, and named its persistence as the thing to watch. It has persisted through a holiday weekend and a small rally, which is a week.

A five-basis-point easing at the long end is not a story on its own; it is inside the daily noise of a market that moves on auctions and inflation prints. What makes it worth a paragraph is the first story in this issue. The long end is where political risk to the central bank would show up, because it is the tenor that prices a decade and a half of monetary credibility rather than the next two meetings. It did not move on the Cook memorandum — but the memorandum published this morning, after Tuesday’s close.

The reconciliation is worth stating since this is the series this brief corrected itself on in September: 26 column headers, 26 cells, the 10-year at column 24, every historical row identical to the reads of 11 and 13 October, and the curve monotonic from one month to twenty years.

What to watch: today’s and tomorrow’s prints, which are the first after the Cook memorandum; the 4 November quarterly refunding, where issuance across the curve is set; and whether the 20-year hump survives a week in which the Federal Reserve’s composition is formally in question.

Science & public health The DEA moves to schedule two kratom alkaloids above a threshold, on its second attempt this year with a different number.

The DEA has issued a notice of intent to temporarily place 7-hydroxymitragynine and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl — two alkaloids found in kratom — in Schedule I, when either alone or both together exceed a specified threshold, at 91 FR 65370. The scheduling reaches isomers, esters, ethers and salts.

The procedural history is the interesting part. HHS provided a different, lower threshold in July 2025, and DEA published a notice of intent on that basis on 7 July 2026; it has also already published a temporary order on related ground. This is a second notice at a revised threshold, which means the dividing line between a lawful botanical product and a Schedule I controlled substance has moved once and may move again.

That threshold is the whole regulation. Kratom leaf contains these compounds at low natural concentrations; concentrated extracts contain far more. A threshold set low enough captures the plant; set high enough, only the concentrates. The DEA is regulating a number, not a substance.

Below the line because it is a notice of intent rather than an order, the threshold figure was not obtained here, and the abstract rather than the notice was read. It leads when the temporary order issues with the number in it.

Energy & grid EPA pushed the greenhouse gas reporting deadline out by more than four months, as an interim final rule.

EPA has taken interim final action extending the deadline under the Greenhouse Gas Reporting Rule for reporting year 2025 data from 30 October 2026 to 10 March 2027 — a slip of more than four months. It is at 91 FR 65112, effective today, and the agency is taking comments on all aspects until 30 November. No other change is made to the rule.

AI & tech industry A Massachusetts company is asking the ITC to block imports of the power systems that feed AI chips.

The International Trade Commission has instituted Investigation No. 337-TA-1526, on a complaint filed 9 September by Vicor Corporation of Andover, Massachusetts, alleging that imported vertical power delivery systems, their components, and computing systems containing them infringe claims of US Patent No. 10,903,734. Notice at 91 FR 65254.

Vertical power delivery is the technique of bringing power to a processor from directly underneath the package rather than from the side. It exists because modern AI accelerators draw so much current that lateral delivery wastes too much of it as heat in the board. It is not a peripheral component; it is one of the things that makes a kilowatt-class chip physically possible.

The forum is what makes this worth recording. A section 337 case is not a damages suit — the remedy is an exclusion order barring the accused products at the border, and “computing systems containing the same” in the caption means the complaint reaches assembled servers, not just the power modules. Section 337 cases also run fast by patent standards, typically to a determination inside eighteen months.

Below the line because an instituted investigation is the beginning: no respondents are identified here, no determination exists, and the complaint itself was not read. It leads if an exclusion order issues, or if the respondents turn out to be the major accelerator vendors.

Energy & grid The reactor design built at Vogtle has been recertified, with the lessons of actually building it folded in.

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission is renewing the AP1000 design certification and incorporating Revision 20 of the AP1000 design control document, at 91 FR 65091, effective 28 December 2026, with comments on the companion proposed rule closing 13 November. Docket NRC-2026-2377. The stated purpose is to update the design to match changes made during the licensing, construction and operation of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 — what the NRC calls a “current, construction-validated design”.

That phrase carries the significance. A certified design is a pre-approved reactor that a utility may reference rather than relitigate, which is the whole point of Part 52 licensing. The AP1000’s problem was never the certificate; it was that Vogtle 3 and 4 came in years late and billions over budget, partly because the certified design kept changing during construction. Revision 20 is the first version that reflects two units actually finished and running.

Whether that makes the next AP1000 cheaper is the open question, and nothing in a certification renewal answers it. It removes one excuse.

Below the line because a design certification renewal authorises nothing by itself — no reactor is ordered, sited or financed by it — and the rule text was not read. It leads at a combined licence application referencing Revision 20.

Science & public health EPA proposes to let trichloroethylene keep being processed until the end of 2027.

EPA is proposing to extend certain compliance dates for trichloroethylene under the Toxic Substances Control Act to 31 December 2027. The extension would cover chemical processors facing the prohibition deadline for processing TCE, industrial and commercial use of TCE as a processing aid and the associated wastewater disposal, and refrigerant manufacturers phasing down TCE feedstock in the production of HFC-134a. Docket EPA-HQ-OPPT-2026-7327, at 91 FR 65148; comments close 13 November. The stated basis is TSCA section 6(d), allowing “necessary and reasonable transition periods”.

TCE is a degreasing solvent that EPA determined presents an unreasonable risk of injury to health, including as a human carcinogen, and the rule banning most uses was among the first major section 6 actions the agency completed. Extending a compliance date is not reopening that finding — the risk determination stands, and the ban still arrives. What changes is how long the exposure continues.

The HFC-134a feedstock piece is the least intuitive and the most structural: TCE is an input to the refrigerant in most car air conditioners, so the phase-down is tangled in a second regulated chemistry with its own timetable.

Below the line because it is a proposal with an open comment period and no substantive change to the underlying rule, and the preamble was not read. It leads if the extension is finalised, or if it is extended again.

A note on being four days early, stated carefully. On 10 October this brief reported that OFAC’s General License 135 sits against an unrepealed statutory import ban, that the statute’s only exit is a certification nobody has submitted, and that neither CFR part the licence names implements the executive order the statute cross-references. On 13 October twelve senators asked the President to rescind the licence, and The Hill reported the deal may conflict with a 2022 law requiring notice to Congress. Two things follow and only one of them is flattering. The flattering one: reading two primary documents against each other produced, in a morning, an argument that took the Senate three days. The other: the brief has no way of knowing whether its reading is right, and the fact that senators now make a similar argument is corroboration of salience, not of law. The statutory question will be settled by a court or by a certification, and neither has happened.
