The oil chain: Washington tells the Gulf to brace for a post-election Iran campaign, Vance writes to Tehran, and Brent gives back a dollar

Energy & gridGeopoliticsMacro & marketsContinuationIndependent corroboration

The warning. CNN, citing a source, reports that the US has told some Gulf governments a campaign against Iran could be “short but intense”. AP reports allies were told to prepare for strikes after the 3 November election. Neither gives a date. CNN adds that US forces could move within four days of an order. Separately, Qatari mediators gave Tehran a new draft to end the war, and Iranian officials called it “unacceptable” (Middle East Eye; Geo).

The letter. Vice President Vance wrote to Mohsen Rezaei, Iran’s security chief. A US official told Axios that any talks to avoid strikes “require discussions on the nuclear issue”. Asked about it on Wednesday, the President said the letter was about getting medical supplies to Iran (Axios; NDTV). Divergence A covers the gap.

The barrel. Brent settled at $115.32 on Wednesday, up 1.3%, and WTI at $102.78 (The National). By early Thursday in Asia, Brent was at $114.10 and WTI at $101.45 (Reuters, 06:51 Saudi time). Overnight, the Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed another Houthi ballistic missile aimed at the Eastern Province. Reuters reports an EIA crude draw of 1.9m barrels. A Reuters poll had expected a 15,000-barrel build. This paper did not read the EIA table itself today.

At the pump. AAA, Thursday: regular $4.2258 (−2.1¢ on the day) and diesel $6.3347 (−1.5¢). Both fell, but diesel fell less, so the premium widened to $2.109. A year ago diesel was $3.6826. A warning that strikes may come after Tuesday adds a risk premium, while a letter about talks takes one away. The pump is where the net of the two lands.

Divergence A — what is the Vance letter?

Substance (facts and characterisation). Axios’s official, and the WSJ’s first report, describe outreach on restarting negotiations, with the nuclear file as a precondition and the President “enthusiastic”. The President describes it as help with medical supplies.

Cause. Vantage and interest, the mundane explanation first. The officials briefing reporters want it read as diplomacy. A president five days from an election, with his own strike option on the table, has a reason to play down talk of a deal. Both can be true if the letter mentions both.

What it means. Weight the officials’ account: it is specific (the recipient, the nuclear condition, who proposed it). The President’s line is a characterisation, not a denial that the letter exists. It would be settled by publication of the text or by an Iranian reply that says what it was answering.

Divergence B — Ras Tanura, a day on

Substance (facts). Yesterday this paper said the evidence showed a fire near Ras Tanura, not a confirmed hit on the refinery. Energy News Beat now cites NASA FIRMS heat signatures at the export terminal on Tuesday. The Houthi claim still names the refinery. Saudi Arabia still confirms only interceptions.

Cause. Satellite fire detection locates heat, not damage. The combatants’ incentives are unchanged.

What it means. The evidence has moved slightly toward “terminal fire” and away from “refinery hit”. A terminal fire is a loading problem lasting days, not lost refining. Loading data from Ras Tanura will settle it, and so would the absence of any Aramco force-majeure notice.

Where the talks stand. Axios names the sticking point. Iran wants the naval blockade lifted, frozen funds released and oil sales allowed first. Washington wants nuclear talks to restart before any relief. Qatar’s seven-day draft did not bridge that: Middle East Eye and Geo both say Tehran refused flexibility on the nuclear file. On Tuesday Brent fell almost $2, to $113.88, on reports of Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani talks in Tehran. On Wednesday it rose $1.44 on the Houthi claim. On Thursday it slipped on the letter. Each day’s move was smaller than the one before, and the market now treats the 3 November date as a known risk.

Who prints what. Several Brent numbers are in circulation for Wednesday. WAM had $115.36 at 01:40 GMT, CNBC $114.68 later in the morning, and The National a $115.32 settlement. These are snapshots taken at different times, not a dispute, so this paper uses the settlement for Wednesday and Reuters’ timed quote for Thursday. UBS (via The National) expects Brent near $110 at year-end, and says pledged strategic reserve releases are “largely completed”. If so, there is little left to cushion a second shock. The SPR was last reported at 282.6m barrels, the lowest since 1982.

What to watch. Any Iranian reply to Vance. OPEC+’s Sunday meeting, where Reuters’ sources expect December targets held. Ras Tanura loadings. Whether the diesel premium keeps widening on days when both fuels fall, which would point at refining rather than crude.

The Fed holds 10–2, both dissenters want a hike, and the 10-year closes at 5.05%

Macro & marketsUS debt & fiscalContinuationPrimary source

The decision. The FOMC kept the target range at 3.75–4.00% by 10 votes to 2. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland’s Beth Hammack dissented. Both wanted a quarter-point increase. It is Cook’s first dissent since she joined in 2022 (CNBC), and it comes while the administration is again trying to remove her. The statement upgraded growth to “expanding at a strong pace” from “solid”, said inflation “remains elevated”, and added that the Committee “will deliver price stability”. The range has been unchanged since September’s hike.

The press conference. Chair Warsh said first-half GDP was revised up to 2.4% and that Q3 looks above 3%, with business investment rising at about a double-digit pace. He also changed the calendar. From 2027 there will still be eight meetings, but one will be for strategy only, with no rate vote or statement. In 2028 there will be two such meetings. So there will be fewer chances to move rates.

The curve, from Treasury’s series. 28 October closes: 2-year 4.67 (−3bp), 10-year 5.05 (+6bp), 20-year 5.41 (+7), 30-year 5.37 (+7). That is a 9bp steepening in a session in which a hike was openly discussed. The 20s–10s hump is +36bp. The front end eased while the long end sold off. Markets are charging term premium on the issuer rather than pricing a tighter Fed. Treasury publishes its borrowing estimates on 2 November and the refunding statement on 4 November.

Divergence — data-dependence or election timing?

Substance (significance). The statement and the Chair frame the hold as waiting for “more information”. Reuters (via Geo) writes that the Fed opted to delay any change until after next week’s election. CNBC notes speculation that officials will not want to move this close to a vote.

Cause. Vantage. The Fed is not going to name the calendar, and reporters can see that the next meeting falls after 3 November. Nobody on the record says politics drove the vote.

What it means. Both readings predict the same thing: no move before the election. They part in December. If inflation prints stay hot and the Committee still holds, the timing reading loses force and the hawks gain. Two dissents for a hike is the stronger signal than either framing. The next CPI and the December dot plot will settle it.

Alphabet burns $9.1bn in a quarter and loses a tenth of its value; Microsoft spends less than expected and rises

AI & techMacro & marketsNew subjectIndependent corroboration

What happened. Alphabet’s third-quarter free cash flow was −$9.07bn, its second negative quarter in a row (−$5.9bn in Q2, its first in 22 years as a public company). Capex was $51.36bn. Revenue of $127.6bn missed by about $0.6bn, and the advertising miss was $1.4bn. Google Cloud more than doubled, to $30.71bn, with a $561bn backlog. Long-term debt is $125.1bn, against $21.6bn a year ago. The shares fell about 10% after hours and were still down 10% before Thursday’s open. CNBC notes that a close down more than 9.77% would be the worst post-earnings reaction in Alphabet’s history (CNBC; Reuters).

Why it matters. 2026 capex guidance stays at $195–205bn. The CFO said 2027 will rise “significantly”, and reports put it above $300bn. The business chief blamed slower search-ad growth on AI Mode, which Google has not yet fully monetised. The AI product is eating the cash engine that funds the AI build-out, and the gap is now covered with debt. Microsoft is the contrast. Capex and finance leases were $45.2bn, up 30% but below the $50.67bn analysts expected; free cash flow fell 27% but stayed positive at $18.63bn; the shares rose about 4–5%.

What to watch. The size of Alphabet’s 2027 guide on its Q4 call. Whether other hyperscalers report cash burn. Whether Alphabet’s bond spreads move. Its debt is now an AI-credit story, which this paper has followed through CoreWeave and the neoclouds.

Divergence A — how big a miss on cash?

Substance (facts, about the consensus). CNBC says analysts expected about −$2.70bn of free cash flow. Reuters, citing LSEG, says the expectation was “close to breaking even”.

Cause. Different consensus polls (CNBC typically uses StreetAccount; Reuters uses LSEG) with different contributor sets. This is mundane, and the actual number is not in dispute.

What it means. Use −$9.07bn as the fact and call it a miss of $6–9bn depending on the poll. Either way it is a large miss, and the 10% fall is consistent with both.

Divergence B — why Microsoft spent less

Substance (significance). A capex shortfall can mean less demand or slower construction. The Company Chronicle, citing the FT, says Microsoft blamed data-centre construction delays, not a pullback. This paper has not read Microsoft’s call transcript or the FT piece.

Cause. Access. The FT reported from the call, and other summaries report only the number.

What it means. If it is delays, the spending has been deferred, not cancelled, and it lands in later quarters. That fits the grid and transformer constraints this paper has tracked, and lead 5. Treat “delays” as single-sourced until the transcript is read. Microsoft’s next-quarter capex figure will settle it.

Two labs, one week, two models rated for real cyber-offence, both released only to vetted defenders

AI & techLab safetyNew subjectPrimary source

What happened. On Wednesday Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Carbon, two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon. Its own post claims 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro (finding vulnerabilities), 54.8% on ExploitGym (turning them into exploits) and 71.0% on CWE-bench (patching). It ranks first on Artificial Analysis’s index at 59, ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra (The Verge). Access is limited to a subset of cyber defenders through Google’s Fairwind programme. Google says it is “actively engaged” in the US government’s voluntary process for testing models before release.

The other one. On 26 October OpenAI published the system-card addendum for GPT-6.1 Terra, a fast model for high-volume work. OpenAI rates it Critical for cybersecurity. Under its framework that means the model can build working zero-day exploits or run novel end-to-end attacks on hardened targets. It rates Terra High for biology and chemistry. The card reports 83.33% pass@1 on SEC-Bench Pro and runs Terra under the same safeguards as GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra.

Why it matters. A “Critical” cyber rating used to describe the frontier. Now it describes OpenAI’s fast, high-volume tier. Both labs now gate their strongest cyber models by customer instead of shipping them openly. That is a lab policy, not a law. The only government touchpoint is a voluntary test whose results neither company has published. The US testing body’s budget, about $10–15m against Britain’s £120m, was in this paper yesterday.

Divergence A — is the government actually testing these?

Substance (facts). Google says it is engaged in the government’s voluntary pre-release process, but does not name the agency or give scope or results. TokenPost reports it as “undergoing US government pre-release evaluation”. Politico and the BBC reported in May that CAISI (Commerce/NIST) signed testing agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI.

Cause. Access. Only the company and the agency know what was tested, and neither has published. Secondary outlets turned “engaged in” into “undergoing”.

What it means. Believe that an agreement exists. Do not assume Carbon was tested before the defenders got it. A CAISI statement or a Google model card that says what was evaluated, by whom and when would settle it.

Divergence B — is the benchmark lead real?

Substance (significance). Google and The Verge present SEC-bench Pro as a clear lead (88.2% against GPT-6 Astra). OpenAI’s card puts Terra at 83.33% on what appears to be the same benchmark. That would put the cheaper OpenAI tier within five points.

Cause. Every one of these figures is self-reported, on the company’s own harness. Scores from different harnesses are not strictly comparable.

What it means. The direction is clear: frontier cyber capability is now widespread across labs, not one firm’s edge. The exact ranking is not established. Independent evaluations, such as CAISI’s or the UK AISI’s, published on a common harness would settle it.

Sources: Google (primary) · OpenAI Terra system card (primary; via search excerpts) · The Verge · TokenPost · Politico (May) · Reuters (Bernstein note)

Who pays for the data centres’ power plants: PJM refiles its backstop today

Energy & gridAI & techNew subjectUnderreportedPrimary source

What happens today. PJM, the grid operator for 13 states and D.C., told FERC it will file a new Section 205 proposal today for its Reliability Backstop Procurement. That is an emergency purchase of about 6.8 GW of new capacity to cover the 2028/29 shortfall its auctions failed to fill (Utility Dive). On 29 September FERC accepted most of the plan but suspended it for five months over three issues: cost allocation, exit responsibilities and collateral. It urged PJM to refile rather than litigate (PJM deck).

What the refiling says. PJM’s 22 October stakeholder deck sets out the answers. Costs follow large-load growth between the 2026/27 and 2028/29 delivery years. Distribution utilities must give PJM the firm, customer-level large loads they forecast, and each zone pays pro rata by its share of that growth, with customer-level offsets. The municipal and co-op opt-out is removed, because FERC called it unduly discriminatory. Collateral is set at 40% of discounted obligations, on an assumed clearing price of $555/MW-day. A utility that leaves PJM keeps paying.

Why it matters. This is the first big US test of whether data-centre growth is charged to the zones that cause it. On 7 October DOE filed its first position statement at FERC in at least five years, saying large loads, “not American households,” must fund the build (Utility Dive).

Divergence A — does “by zone” mean “by data centre”?

Substance (significance). DOE’s framing is that the large loads pay. PJM’s mechanism assigns costs to zones by their share of large-load growth, then to the utilities that serve load in them. If no such allocation is made, the charge falls on all load in the zone. On that design, households in a fast-growing data-centre zone could still share the bill.

Cause. DOE writes at the level of principle, and PJM writes the tariff mechanics. Nobody has misread anything.

What it means. The filing moves cost toward the right places, but not all the way to the customer who causes it. The reader’s question is answered by how state commissions in Virginia, Ohio and Pennsylvania pass zone charges into retail rates. Watch those dockets after FERC rules.

Divergence B — 6.8 GW, or less?

Substance (facts, timing). Utility Dive and PJM’s fact sheet give 6.8 GW. The new deck resets the initial target to “the most recent information available” for 2028/29, and PJM has said the number could fall as new supply is counted.

Cause. The 6.8 GW is the old snapshot, and the formula will be re-run with newer data.

What it means. Treat 6.8 GW as an upper reference, not the procurement. The target in today’s filing settles it.

Sources: PJM stakeholder deck, 22 Oct (primary, read in full) · Utility Dive · RTO Insider · DOE (headline) · Troutman Energy Report (headline)

Dig deeper

The collateral arithmetic. PJM chose 40% by trading off the cost of posting collateral (bank facilities at 4.93%, SOFR plus a BBB spread) against the cost of a default that other utilities would have to absorb. It uses a 4.05% default probability from S&P’s 2025 study for the BBB average. The point for the reader: the backstop assumes some load-serving entities might not pay. If so, the shortfall is shared among those that do, unless the collateral covers it.

Other changes. Peak-shaving opt-outs need a five-year plan, matching the five-year minimum term for offsets. Site-control rules apply only to sell offers not already in PJM’s queue. Cure periods can now run past the close of bidding. Hyperscape Digital reports that the bid window has moved to March 2027. This paper has not read PJM’s timeline page in a form it could parse, so that date is single-sourced.

Why underreported. The national press has covered data-centre power as a price story. The allocation rule in today’s filing decides where that price lands. It turns on tariff language that few outlets read.

A judge refuses to set aside 20,000 California ballots, and the Justice Department appeals within hours

US politics & policyCourtsContinuationIndependent corroboration

What happened. On Wednesday evening US District Judge David O. Carter denied the Justice Department’s request for a temporary restraining order. The order would have made California set aside mail ballots from about 20,000 people the government calls “likely non-citizens”. He found the request would “wreak havoc” on an election already under way. He told the government it has a remedy, prosecution: “Go out and indict” (NBC). First Assistant US Attorney Bill Essayli said the Solicitor General had authorised an immediate appeal. A notice of appeal has been filed and an emergency motion to the Ninth Circuit is next (NBC). That answers the question this morning’s other edition left open.

How it’s known. NBC and NPR both quote the 26-page order, and California’s Attorney General has posted it. This paper read the two articles and the AG’s release, not the order itself. A Ninth Circuit panel had already ruled 2–1 on Monday that the claimed emergency was partly “self-constructed”, because the US had questioned California’s rolls since 16 July and filed only on 15 October.

Why it matters. This is the first ruling against the Department in its three-state push (California, Alaska, Washington state). The appeal decides whether federal courts will touch ballots in the five days left. Yesterday’s lead covered a grand jury, a magistrate and a district judge refusing the Department in other cases. By this paper’s count, Carter’s is the fourth refusal in two days.

Divergence — how many “likely non-citizens”?

Substance (facts). The suit says “over 20,000”. California says the Department’s July letter put the figure at 190,000, and that the Department has refused to say how its lists are made (NBC). Los Angeles County’s preliminary review found many of the names have no active registration (NPR, as read by today’s Claude edition). The judge cites Nevada: DHS flagged up to 15,903 voters, cut that to 6,218 and then to 185, and state officials then confirmed all 185 were citizens.

Cause. Each count comes from a matching process the government has not disclosed. Every step of manual review has shrunk the number. The Department has an interest in a large figure, and the state in a small one. The mundane explanation is crude database matching, refined by hand.

What it means. The evidence supports the judge. The government’s figures have fallen by orders of magnitude whenever anyone checked them, and no court has seen the method. Treat “20,000” as a list of names, not a count of non-citizens. Disclosure of the matching method, or an indictment tally from the list, would settle it.

Sources: NBC News · NPR · California AG · Order (posted; not read here) · AP (headline; page timed out)

40,000 DHS staff exposed: the hacker blames the Okta sign-on, Okta says it was not breached

Cyber & securityUS politics & policyNew subjectIndependent corroboration

What happened. A hacker calling itself the Department of Peace says it took DHS’s personnel directory. The data covers more than 40,000 employees and contractors: about 21,000 from CBP, 10,000 from ICE and HSI, and just under 1,000 from CISA. DDoSecrets has published it as a searchable database called “Icepick”. TechCrunch matched several known DHS employees against an offline copy and says the data appears authentic. Record timestamps suggest it was taken around mid-August. DHS says it is “aware of a potential breach” (TechCrunch; NPR).

What’s new since this morning’s other edition. Two things. First, Okta, the sign-on provider the hacker named, says “Okta is secure” and that it has no evidence its systems were compromised. Second, the dump is more than a phone book. It includes Active Directory group memberships showing who has access to which tools: Penlink’s PLX location-tracking platform, the Mobile Fortify face-recognition app (more than 200 CBP users), ChatGPT, and groups tied to the Salt Typhoon and Volt Typhoon investigations.

Why it matters. This is the third federal personnel breach in months, after the FBI and the Pentagon’s HR unit. It is also the second DHS breach this year by the same actor. The group lists show which surveillance tools each part of DHS uses, which is information procurement records do not reveal.

Divergence A — how did they get in?

Substance (facts). The hacker says it subverted DHS’s Okta single sign-on. Okta says its own systems were not compromised. DHS has not described a vector.

Cause. Interest on both sides: the hacker wants to embarrass, and the vendor wants to protect its product. The mundane reconciliation is common in SSO incidents. A stolen or abused DHS credential in DHS’s Okta tenant is not a breach of Okta.

What it means. Assume account compromise inside DHS’s tenant, not an Okta platform failure, until shown otherwise. DHS’s own incident notice, or a CISA advisory, will settle it.

Divergence B — home addresses, and whose note?

Substance (facts). NPR reports “names, phone numbers and addresses”, including people in undercover operations. TechCrunch, which examined a copy, lists work contact details and office locations. NPR attributes the note “They have our information, so it’s only fair that we have theirs” to DDoSecrets. TechCrunch says the hacker left it with the data.

Cause. Access. TechCrunch examined the data, and NPR reported from the publishers and an online statement. “Addresses” in NPR’s copy may mean email or office addresses.

What it means. Weight TechCrunch on contents: work directory data, with home addresses not established. That lowers the physical-safety risk without removing it. This paper will not open the database to check. A DHS notification to affected staff will say what was exposed.

Sources: TechCrunch · NPR (text edition) · Guardian (headline) · CNBC (headline)

Robotics joins the Section 232 list, and like wind turbines last week, no tariff comes with it

US politics & policyTradeAI & techNew subjectPrimary source

What happened. On Wednesday the President signed a Section 232 proclamation finding that imports of robotics, industrial machinery and their parts threaten national security. It imposes no tariff, quota or fee. It directs Commerce and USTR to negotiate agreements with trading partners and to report on any need for further action. The White House fact sheet says the US “produces less robotics and industrial equipment than it consumes”. Reuters’ headline says it plainly: national-security risk, no tariffs. A first update is due within 180 days, which CTOL dates to 26 April 2027.

The legal mechanism. A 232 finding is the legal predicate. Once it is made, the President can impose duties later without a new investigation. So this is a loaded instrument, not a decision. It follows the same form as last week’s wind-turbine proclamation, which also imposed nothing. The fact sheet lists the products already tariffed under 232: steel, aluminium, copper, autos, trucks, timber, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, polysilicon and drones.

The market half. Industrial robots and machine tools are inputs to the factories the administration wants built, and to the data centres in lead 5. A future tariff would raise the cost of reshoring. The negotiation route means trading partners now have six months to offer something. This paper has not seen an HTS list or a covered-products annex, and the fact sheet names none.

Divergence — “adjusts imports” or “does nothing yet”?

Substance (significance). The White House title says the President “adjusts imports”. Reuters leads with “does not impose tariffs”. Industry summaries on X read it as a security finding that welcomes reshoring.

Cause. The statute’s vocabulary. Section 232 actions are always styled “adjusting imports”, even when the adjustment is a negotiation.

What it means. Read the operative text: nothing changes at the border today, and the finding is the lasting part. The 180-day update, or a tariff annex before then, will settle what it costs.

Sources: White House fact sheet (primary) · Proclamation (title and listing) · Reuters via Investing.com (headline) · CTOL

Section 232 findings without tariffs:

JPMorgan and 32 other issuers refuse to let their shares trade as tokens on the OKX–ICE venue

US financialsMarkets structureNew subjectIndependent corroboration

What happened. OKXICE, the joint venture of crypto exchange OKX and NYSE owner Intercontinental Exchange, plans round-the-clock trading of tokenised US shares, settled in stablecoins. It would run through Uniswap v4 pools on OKX’s XLayer chain under the SEC’s five-year “Innovation Exemption”. Issuers get 30 days to object. The venue’s 27 October notice added JPMorgan Chase, Coca-Cola, Dell, Applied Materials, Adobe and Sandisk, bringing disclosed objections to 33 (CoinDesk; Decrypt). Wells Fargo is also among the objectors.

What JPMorgan says. A spokesperson told CoinDesk the bank is “encouraged by the potential of tokenization”, but must be careful how its shares are “represented and traded”. It is not joining “at this stage”. JPMorgan is building its own: tokenised deposits through Kinexys and The Clearing House.

Why it matters. The SEC’s exemption gives issuers a veto, and the largest US bank used it. A venue that cannot list the biggest names cannot offer a full market around the clock. Banks are not refusing tokenisation. They are refusing to let someone else tokenise their equity on rails they do not control. The same pattern appeared with banks blocking customers’ AI agents (this paper, 24 October).
