The House voted to end the Iran war at 10:32 last night. It is not a law and it was never going to be

US politics & policyNew subjectPrimary source

On roll call 307, taken at 10:32pm Eastern on Tuesday 15 September, the House agreed to H. Con. Res. 93 by 220 to 204, directing the President to remove US armed forces from hostilities with Iran under the War Powers Resolution. Nine members did not vote. The Democratic caucus went 213–0 in favour with one absence; Republicans went 7–203 against with eight absences; the chamber’s one independent voted no.

The seven Republicans were Thomas Massie of Kentucky, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Warren Davidson of Ohio, Tom Barrett of Michigan, Nancy Mace of South Carolina, and Mariannette Miller-Meeks and Zach Nunn of Iowa. Three of them had not voted for a withdrawal resolution before. The two earlier attempts, in June and July, drew four Republicans each. The resolution is Seth Moulton’s, cosponsored by fourteen Democrats, thirteen of them veterans.

Here is the part the coverage is getting wrong. This is a concurrent resolution. It is not presented to the President, it cannot be signed and it cannot be vetoed, and even if the Senate passed it tomorrow it would not have the force of law. What it is instead is a count: a recorded House majority against a war the President began in late February without authorisation, seven weeks before the midterms, with the Republican defection rate tripling since June.

What to watch: whether any senator forces the privileged equivalent onto the Senate floor, which would put fifty-three Republicans on the record; and the appropriations route, which is the one mechanism in this area that has ever actually bound a President.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Not the tally — everyone has 220–204. The dispute is about what the instrument is. Al Jazeera writes that the measure “faces an almost certain presidential veto if it reaches Trump’s desk.” UPI’s headline says the House “passes resolution to curb Trump’s ability to wage Iran war.” Moulton’s own press release says the House voted to “reclaim congressional authority to declare war.” This is a dispute about facts, and it is adjudicable.

Why they land differently. Mostly compression, not motive. A concurrent resolution and a joint resolution are one word apart on the docket and a universe apart in law, and the wire template for “chamber passes thing the President dislikes” has a veto sentence built into it. Moulton’s framing is the sponsor describing the intent of his own text, which is a different claim from describing its effect. Nobody here is inventing anything; the veto sentence is simply a category error.

What it means for you. Treat this as a political count with no operative legal effect, and discount any account that says otherwise — including any that tells you the war is now on a clock. The observation that would change the picture is a joint resolution, or a funding limitation in an appropriations bill. Either would be news of a different order. Watch the vehicle type, not the headline verb.

Dig deeper — why a concurrent resolution cannot do this, and what happened to the mechanism that could

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 was written with a self-executing enforcement clause: Congress could direct the President to withdraw forces by concurrent resolution, which requires both chambers and no presidential signature. That was the design, and for a decade it was the assumed remedy.

Then in 1983 the Supreme Court decided INS v. Chadha, striking down the one-house legislative veto and, in its reasoning, the general proposition that Congress can bind the executive through an instrument that never goes through presentment. The concurrent-resolution mechanism in the War Powers Resolution has been widely regarded as constitutionally infirm ever since. Congress responded the same year by adding expedited floor procedures for a joint resolution — the version that does go to the President, can be vetoed, and would need two thirds of both chambers to survive that veto. That is the live route. Last night’s vote did not take it.

So what was the point? Three things, and they are not nothing. First, the count itself: 220 members have now put their names to ending this war, and in a chamber this close that number is a whip check for anything that follows. Second, the Republican trend line — four, four, seven, with three first-time defectors. Zach Nunn’s stated reasoning is the tell: with the negotiating window closed, he argued, sustained combat operations now require congressional authorisation. That is a member of the President’s own party locating the moment the legal position changed, which is a more durable thing to have on the record than a floor speech. Third, it forces the question into the Senate, where the arithmetic is worse for the administration than the House’s was.

The related item, and the reason to keep the two separate in your head: Thomas Massie has also moved to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over the Iran war. That is a privileged resolution of a different kind, it will not succeed, and it is the sort of thing that generates a great deal of coverage while changing nothing. The war-powers count is the one worth carrying forward.

One limit on this item. What has been read here is the Clerk’s roll-call record for vote 307, which is authoritative on the question, the time, the tally and every member’s position. The text of H. Con. Res. 93 itself has not been read — congress.gov has been returning 403 to this brief since 14 September — so the description of what it directs comes from the Clerk’s question line and from reporting. If the resolution contains a reporting requirement or a certification clause, that would not be visible here.

Crypto market structure died on the Senate floor at 49–50, eleven votes short

US politics & policyUS financialsPrimary source

Senate roll call 234, Tuesday 15 September: cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633 — the CLARITY Act, which would divide oversight of digital commodities between the SEC and the CFTC — was rejected 49 to 50. Sixty were needed. This brief said yesterday that the vote was at 2:15pm and that there would be no result to report before publication. There is now.

The margin is the story. Not 57, not 58 — forty-nine. Every Democrat who had been publicly working on the bill voted against proceeding, including Kirsten Gillibrand, Mark Warner, Cory Booker, Raphael Warnock, Ruben Gallego, Angela Alsobrooks and Catherine Cortez Masto. On initial tallies at least one Republican, Susan Collins, voted no as well. A bill eleven votes from cloture with its own bipartisan negotiators voting against the motion to proceed is not a bill that was close.

The stated objection is not about digital assets. Gallego’s statement says Republicans abandoned bipartisan ethics negotiations to schedule the vote, and that he will not support legislation that he sees as enabling the President personally. Republican leaders had released a revised text on Sunday adding ethics restrictions on public officials profiting from crypto ventures. It was not enough.

What to watch: whether leadership files again this session, which would require finding eleven votes that were not there; and the two things that were waiting on this bill — the stablecoin-yield question, and the OCC stablecoin rules still targeted for November against a statutory deadline missed on 18 July.

Dig deeper — what the failure hands to the regulators, and why the community-bank angle survives the bill

The immediate consequence of a failed cloture vote is that nothing changes, which in this area means the status quo wins by default — and the status quo is regulation by enforcement and by agency rulemaking rather than by statute. The SEC and the CFTC keep their contested and overlapping claims over the same instruments. Nobody gets the jurisdictional line the industry spent two years and a great deal of money trying to buy.

The piece this brief has been tracking is narrower and it outlives the bill. The CLARITY text carried the compromise on stablecoin yield: community banks argue that a yield-bearing dollar token is a deposit substitute that pulls funding out of institutions in roughly the $3bn–$6bn asset band, and the bill answered that by directing the Treasury Secretary to restrict such rewards only if substantial deposit flight could be demonstrated. That was a trigger rather than a prohibition, and it is now gone along with everything else. The deposit-flight question does not go away; it simply has no statutory answer, which means it lands back with the banking agencies — the same four that yesterday proposed rescinding the 2023 third-party risk framework, eight days after doubling the extended-examination threshold. Three reductions in the structure around one band of banks, and the one piece of it that was going to arrive by statute just failed.

On the ethics fight, be precise about what is being claimed. Gallego is not arguing the market-structure provisions are wrong. He is arguing that a framework legitimising an asset class in which the President and his family have direct commercial interests requires conflict-of-interest provisions that the Sunday text did not contain. That is a claim about the bill’s context, not its content, and it is why the usual trade-off — soften a definition here, widen an exemption there — could not close the gap. A bill blocked on content can be renegotiated. A bill blocked on who benefits generally cannot be, until the beneficiary changes.

What has been read here: the Senate’s own roll-call record, which is authoritative on the question, the tally and the date, and Senator Gallego’s published statement. The member-by-member breakdown of the 49 and the 50 is from reporting, not from the roll-call detail page, and the Collins vote in particular is described in sources as an initial tally. CNBC’s article on the vote returned 403 to this brief, as its article pages have all week.

Commerce set duties near 250% on Indian solar cells today, and nobody covered it

Energy & gridNew subjectUnderreportedPrimary source

Six Federal Register notices today, published together and read here in full: the Commerce Department’s final antidumping and countervailing duty determinations on crystalline silicon photovoltaic cells from India, Indonesia and Laos. For India the countervailing rate is 126.09% and the dumping margin 123.04%, both applied to every named producer — the Adani-affiliated Mundra Solar entities, Kowa and Premier Energies — and to all others. Both rest on adverse inferences: the mandatory respondents did not participate, so Commerce used facts available rather than verified data.

Indonesia is lower and split: PT Blue Sky Solar Indonesia draws 173.70% on the subsidy side, also by adverse inference, while PT REC Solar Energy Indonesia and all others draw 73.20%. Commerce found critical circumstances almost everywhere, which pulls the suspension of liquidation back 90 days before the preliminary determinations — to 28 November 2025 for most subsidy findings, and to 28 January 2026 for the four named Indian exporters on the dumping side.

The US channel is the interconnection queue. This is the sequel to the 2024 Southeast Asia cases: cell manufacturing moved to India, Indonesia and Laos, and the duties have now followed it there. Effective rates near 250% are not a tariff, they are an exclusion.

What to watch: the International Trade Commission’s final injury vote, due within 45 days. If it finds injury, orders issue and the deposits become permanent. If it does not, the whole thing terminates and the money is refunded.

Dig deeper — adverse inference, the stacking question, and why this lands on the data-centre build

Start with the number, because it deserves a caveat. The headline arithmetic — 126.09 plus 123.04, call it 249% — is roughly right for what an importer posts as cash deposits, but not exactly. Where a countervailing duty offsets an export subsidy, the dumping margin is reduced to avoid double-counting the same benefit. That adjustment is in the orders rather than in the headline rates, so the true combined deposit rate for a given entry may be somewhat below the sum. It will still be far past the level at which anyone imports anything.

The adverse-inference point is the one that tells you what happened procedurally. Commerce sends questionnaires; respondents answer them; Commerce verifies. When the mandatory respondents do not participate — as here, for every Indian company and for Blue Sky in Indonesia — the agency is entitled to use facts otherwise available and to draw inferences adverse to the non-cooperating party, which in practice means selecting from the high end of the petition’s alleged rates. So 126.09% is not a measurement of Indian subsidy. It is the price of not showing up. REC Solar in Indonesia did participate and drew 73.20%, which is the only rate in the set derived from examined data, and it is still enormous.

Why this matters to a reader who does not own a solar panel. The same month EPA finalised the power-plant carbon repeal and FERC ordered all six market operators to justify how they interconnect large loads, the cheapest marginal source of new generating capacity in the United States just got considerably more expensive to import. Utility-scale solar has been the fastest thing to build into an interconnection queue — not because it is ideologically favoured but because the equipment is a commodity and the permitting is light. Data-centre load is arriving on a schedule that does not care about any of this. Take the commodity supply away and what is left to serve that load is gas turbines with multi-year delivery slots, and the cost of the difference is allocated in a state rate case.

The critical-circumstances finding is the sharp edge. Suspension of liquidation reaching back to November 2025 means importers who moved cargo in anticipation of the case now owe deposits on entries already sold, at rates nobody could have provisioned for. That is the mechanism’s intended deterrent effect and it also produces insolvencies.

The honest limits. The India countervailing and antidumping notices and the Indonesia countervailing notice were read in full here. The Laos determinations are in today’s issue under document numbers 2026-18941 and 2026-18942 but their rates have not been read, and the Indonesia dumping margin has not been read either — so no combined Indonesian figure is given above. The ITC schedule is stated in the Commerce notices as “within 45 days”; no specific vote date has been confirmed.

Sources: India, final countervailing determination (2026-18947) · India, final dumping determination (2026-18945) · Indonesia, final countervailing determination (2026-18940) · Federal Register notices, 16 September.

The Air Force Secretary said the United States has weapons in orbit

GeopoliticsNew subjectSingle-sourced

Air Force Secretary Troy Meink told the Air, Space & Cyber Conference in Maryland that the United States has fielded “on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the Joint Force against hostile adversary action.” He did not say what they are, how they work, or whether they are aimed at objects in space or at targets on the ground. Reporting dates the remarks to 15 September.

This is the first public American acknowledgment that it has deployed weapons in space. Until now the posture was studied ambiguity: the United States described adversary capabilities — principally the Russian nuclear anti-satellite programme reported in 2024 — while characterising its own space activity as defensive and unarmed. That asymmetry is now gone, by choice.

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty, to which the United States and Russia are both parties, bans placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit and declares space a commons for peaceful purposes. It does not ban conventional weapons in orbit. So the plain reading is that nothing said yesterday is a treaty violation — and also that the one legal line everyone cites is not the line being approached.

What to watch: whether the Space Force or the Department of War puts anything on paper — a posture statement, a budget line, a congressional hearing answer — that gives the category a name. An acknowledgment made in a conference speech and nowhere else is deniable in a way a budget justification is not.

Dig deeper — why a conference speech, and what the disclosure is probably for

Deliberate disclosure of a capability is itself the capability being used. States reveal weapons when the deterrent value of being known to have them exceeds the operational value of surprise, and the calculation almost always turns on a specific audience. The audience here is not the American public.

The sequence is worth holding. In 2024 the United States publicly accused Russia of developing a nuclear anti-satellite weapon designed to be launched into orbit, while saying there was no immediate danger. Chinese on-orbit rendezvous and proximity operations have been tracked and complained about for years. American military dependence on satellite communication, positioning and imagery is the deepest of any state and is therefore the most attractive thing to hold at risk. Saying out loud that there is something up there able to defend the joint force is a message to two capitals that the cost of a first move has changed.

The form of the disclosure carries information too. A conference keynote is the softest available channel: no document, no definitions, no compelled follow-up questions, and deniability about scope. Compare the section 338 proclamations this brief has been reading all month, where the operative content turned out to be in the severability clause. There is no severability clause in a speech. Until this appears in a budget justification or a posture statement, what exists is a sentence.

Meink used the same appearance to describe a service reshaped by 2032 around more autonomous systems, including replacing some artillery roles with uncrewed attack aircraft. That is a procurement argument being made in front of a procurement audience, and the space sentence was delivered inside it, which is a reason for some caution about how carefully it was drafted.

The confidence marker on this story is deliberate. NBC News, NPR, CNBC and US News all carried it within hours of each other, and their accounts agree closely — which is exactly what shared wire copy looks like. This brief could not establish that any two of them reported it independently, and the speech itself was not read or watched here. One further wrinkle supports the caution: the accounts differ on which day Meink spoke, with most placing it on Tuesday 15 September and at least one describing it as the day before. That is a small discrepancy and it is the kind that propagates when everyone is working from the same summary. Treat the quoted sentence as reliable and the surrounding detail as thinner than the number of bylines suggests.

One thing this brief did not do today: the UAP disclosure thread’s own sources — the AARO records contract and the implementing guidance — were not reached this run, so nothing here should be read as bearing on them.

Sources: NBC News · NPR · US News.

The Congo Ebola epidemic is at 6,779 cases, and the US entry ban now runs to 11 October

Science & public healthUnderreportedPrimary source

Today’s Federal Register carries the CDC order continuing the suspension of the right to introduce certain persons from countries where a quarantinable communicable disease exists. It is document 2026-18948, it took effect at 5:00pm Eastern on 11 September, and it expires at 4:59pm on 11 October. It covers the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda and South Sudan, and the disease is Ebola caused by Bundibugyo virus.

The numbers in the order are the news. As of 9 September the DRC has 6,779 confirmed cases and 3,267 deaths across 61 health zones — a case fatality ratio just under half. Contact tracing is running at roughly 87% when the order says 95% is needed to bring an outbreak under control. Uganda has completed its 42-day monitoring period with 20 cases and two deaths, its last case on 21 June. South Sudan has no confirmed cases and is included on cross-border risk alone.

The order reaches non-citizens who were in those three countries in the preceding 21 days — and on its own terms that includes lawful permanent residents. US citizens and nationals, military personnel and certain government employees are excepted, and customs officers may grant case-by-case exceptions on humanitarian or public-health grounds.

What to watch: 11 October, when this either lapses or is renewed a fourth time; and whether the contact-tracing figure moves, since the order itself names 95% as the threshold.

Dig deeper — the scale of this outbreak, and the part of the order that is about immigration rather than disease

Set the case count against the historical record. The 2018–20 Kivu outbreak, the second-largest Ebola epidemic ever recorded, ran to roughly 3,470 cases and 2,280 deaths and dominated global health coverage for two years. At 6,779 confirmed cases this one has passed it, and is now second only to the 2013–16 West Africa epidemic. That comparison is drawn from the historical record rather than from today’s document, and it is the single most useful thing to know about a story that is receiving a fraction of the attention Kivu did.

The legal instrument is worth understanding because it is a familiar one wearing a different coat. Sections 362 and 365 of the Public Health Service Act, implemented at 42 CFR 71.40, are the authority used for the Title 42 border expulsions of 2020–23 — a public-health power to suspend entry, exercised by the CDC Director, renewable in short increments, and subject to essentially no adversarial process. Its use here is squarely within what the statute contemplates: a lethal haemorrhagic fever with active transmission in three named countries. That is a much better fit to the text than the 2020 use was.

The provision that deserves scrutiny is the coverage of lawful permanent residents. An LPR is not a visitor; they hold a statutory right to reside in the United States, and suspending their re-entry on the basis of recent travel is a serious thing to do by order rather than by rule. There is a public-health case for it — a virus does not check immigration status, and the 21-day window matches Ebola’s incubation period. There is also an obvious precedent risk in establishing that the entry rights of permanent residents can be suspended by a renewable 30-day administrative order, and the case-by-case exception at the discretion of a customs officer is thin process for a right of that weight. Both things are true. Recording that they are both true is the point.

The contact-tracing number is the operational signal to watch and the reason this is not near an end. Eighty-seven per cent against a required ninety-five is not a small gap; in an outbreak spread across 61 health zones it is the difference between a receding epidemic and one that keeps reseeding itself. Expect renewal on 11 October unless that figure moves sharply.

What has been read here is the full text of the order, which is the authority for every figure above. Congolese and WHO situation reports were not reached this run, so the case counts are the CDC’s as of 9 September and are already a week old.

Diesel set a seventh straight record, and the freight market had already turned before it got here

US fuel pricesMacro & marketsPrimary source

AAA this morning: on-highway diesel $6.3103 a gallon, a seventh consecutive all-time record. Yesterday $6.2694, a week ago $5.9424, a month ago $5.4460, a year ago $3.7008 — up 70.5% year on year and 15.9% in thirty days. Regular gasoline is $4.3672 against $3.1862 a year ago.

The new information today is not the price. It is Cass’s August freight data, and it changes the transmission story. Cass’s truckload linehaul index — which measures rates excluding fuel surcharges — rose 11.3% year on year in August, up 70 basis points from July, a twentieth consecutive annual increase and the largest since June 2022. Shipment volumes rose 2.1% year on year, the first increase after 42 straight months of decline.

Read those two series together. The freight market turned before the diesel shock arrived, on capacity that left during a three-and-a-half-year downturn. So carriers now have contract pricing power and a fuel shock at the same time, and the fuel component reaches shippers separately through surcharges that reset weekly. August’s 11.3% is the floor under what happens next, not the effect of $6 diesel — none of that is in these numbers yet.

What to watch: September CPI, published in October, the first month lived entirely inside $6 diesel. Food-at-home was 0.0% month on month in August. It will not stay there.

Dig deeper — why the linehaul index excluding fuel is the number that matters this week

Freight pricing has two parts and conflating them is the commonest error in reading a fuel shock into the economy. The linehaul rate is the price of moving the load: it reflects capacity, demand and the carrier’s cost of everything except fuel. The fuel surcharge is a separate line, usually indexed to the EIA weekly diesel print and reset weekly, and it is designed to pass diesel straight through to the shipper with a lag of days. Cass’s truckload linehaul index deliberately strips the surcharge out.

That is what makes August’s 11.3% significant. It is not diesel showing up in freight rates — it cannot be, by construction. It is the end of the longest freight downturn on record: 42 consecutive months of year-on-year shipment declines, during which small carriers left the market and capacity was permanently retired. Volumes turning positive against that thinned capacity is what produces pricing power, and the twentieth consecutive annual increase in the linehaul index says carriers have been rebuilding margin for most of two years.

So the shipper’s delivered cost is now rising on both lines at once, and only one of them has been measured yet. The surcharge line tracks a diesel price 70.5% higher than a year ago. The linehaul line is rising 11.3% for reasons that have nothing to do with diesel and will not reverse when diesel does. This is why the pass-through argument this brief has been making since 14 September should be expected to show up later and to stick longer than a simple fuel-cost model would imply: a fuel spike is transitory, and a recapitalised truckload market with pricing power is not.

The second-order evidence is in the carrier accounts. FreightWaves’ front page today carries Hub Group warning of a possible Nasdaq delisting after a first-half operating loss, alongside a run of smaller carriers and brokers seeking court protection. A market where rates are rising 11% and operators are still failing is one where the cost increase is arriving faster than contracts reprice — which is exactly the condition under which surviving carriers end up with more pricing power still.

Provenance, precisely. The AAA figures were read from AAA’s own page this morning and are primary. The Cass figures are Cass Information Systems’ monthly report as described by FreightWaves and corroborated in trade coverage; the Cass report itself was not read here, so the 11.3%, the 70 basis points, the 2.1% and the 42-month figure are secondary. The EIA weekly print charted in yesterday’s issue — $6.285 for the week ending 14 September — is unchanged; there is no new federal print until next Tuesday.

Sources: AAA national averages · FreightWaves on the Cass August report · FreightWaves front page · EIA weekly retail prices. Continues yesterday.

A drone was shot down short of Mecca, and three price feeds cannot agree what Brent costs

GeopoliticsEnergy & gridIndependent corroboration

Saudi air defences intercepted a drone before it entered restricted airspace over Mecca on Tuesday evening, according to coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki, who called the security of the two holy mosques and of pilgrims a “red line”. Reporting describes it as the first security alert activated in Mecca in nearly a decade. The Houthis deny it, calling the accusation a “nauseating lie”. Mecca is about 1,100km from the Yemeni border.

Attribution is genuinely open and probably will stay open. What is not open is the trajectory: a UN-brokered truce in April 2022 largely ended Houthi strikes on Saudi territory, and they resumed after the group declared a blockade on Saudi shipping in July. Saudi cities faced fresh missile and drone alerts through the day.

The physical oil position is unchanged and bad. The East–West pipeline — 1,200km, up to 7m barrels a day, the single largest bypass of Hormuz — remains shut since 11 September. Houthi forces are advancing toward Bab el-Mandeb. Libya has production halted. Against that, the American Petroleum Institute reported a 7.1m barrel build in US crude stocks last week, which is why the price is falling on a day the news got worse.

What to watch: a restart date for the East–West line, which is the only near-term supply relief available; and whether an attack on the Hejaz rather than the Eastern Province changes Riyadh’s stated decision not to retaliate.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What Brent costs. Three feeds read this morning: Trading Economics has $107.61, down 1.04% on the day; Handelsblatt’s front page has $102.65, up 1.34%; a third quote circulating from Tuesday has $109.21. That is a $6.56 spread and a disagreement about direction. This is a dispute about facts, and yesterday’s issue flagged a five-dollar version of the same thing.

Why they differ. Almost certainly the mundane explanation: these are not the same instrument. “Brent” is variously the ICE front-month future, the next-month future, dated physical Brent, and a contract-for-difference derived from any of them — and in steep backwardation, which is what a supply shock produces, the gap between adjacent contract months is large. Add timestamps taken at different hours across European and US sessions on a day with 3% intraday ranges and a $6 spread needs no conspiracy. Handelsblatt is a German paper quoting a European feed at European midday; Trading Economics is quoting later.

What it means for you. Do not use the level for anything. Use the direction on a single feed held constant, and prefer physical indicators over the screen price: whether the East–West pipeline restarts, whether Bab el-Mandeb transits fall, and what AAA prints at the pump. This brief will quote Trading Economics’ Brent consistently and say so, rather than picking whichever feed suits the sentence. What would settle it properly is a settlement price from ICE itself, which has not been reachable here.

Dig deeper — why Mecca is a different category of target, and the inventory number underneath the price

Distinguish the military question from the political one. Militarily, a single drone intercepted 1,100km from its presumed launch point changes nothing: no damage, no casualties, no infrastructure affected. Politically, the target set matters more than the outcome. Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia have historically concentrated on the Eastern Province and the energy infrastructure there — Abqaiq, Ras Tanura, the pump stations struck on 11 September. Those are economic targets with an economic logic, and Riyadh has treated them as costs to be absorbed while it decides.

Mecca is not that. An attack on the Hejaz, and specifically on the haram, is an attack on the basis of Saudi legitimacy in the Muslim world — the monarchy’s central claim is custodianship of the two holy mosques. That is why al-Maliki reached for “red line” language he has not used for the oil facilities, and it is why the Houthi denial is both immediate and plausible on motive: claiming a strike at Mecca costs the Houthis standing across exactly the constituency they have spent two years courting. Both a Houthi launch they have chosen to disown and a launch by someone else are consistent with what is known, and this brief cannot distinguish them.

The reason it matters to a reader in Florida is the retaliation question. On 11 September Riyadh said it would not respond to the pump-station strikes for now, at Baghdad’s request, reserving the right. That restraint is the thing keeping a regional war from becoming a Gulf-wide one, and it was priced on the assumption that the targets stayed economic. If Mecca moves the Saudi calculation, the next escalation is on the Saudi side rather than the Iranian one, and the East–West pipeline does not restart.

The inventory number deserves attention because it is the first genuinely bearish datum in a month. A 7.1m barrel build in US crude stocks is large, and it says American refiners are not able to run the crude they have — which is consistent with utilisation already at 98% and with a market short of distillate capacity rather than short of barrels. What to watch: the adopted millage against the rolled-back rate, which is the number that tells you whether this is a tax increase in the statutory sense regardless of what the rate does.

Dig deeper — what the rolled-back rate means, and why Pinellas cutting the rate may still raise your bill

Florida’s truth-in-millage law defines a tax increase not against last year’s millage but against the rolled-back rate: the millage that would raise the same total revenue from the same properties as last year, after removing new construction. Because assessed values in Pasco and Pinellas have risen for years, the rolled-back rate is normally below the current rate. A county can therefore cut its millage, advertise a tax cut, and still be above rolled-back — which means it is collecting more money from the same houses, and the statute requires it to advertise a tax increase. Both things can be in the same press release.

This is the number to ask for at either hearing, and it is the reason the reporting on Pinellas is not sufficient on its own. What is established is that commissioners reduced the tax take by $6.026m from what was originally proposed, and that this is the fifth rate cut in six years. What is not established, from anything read here, is the adopted millage, the rolled-back rate, or the total budget. A cut against your own earlier proposal is a cut against a plan, not against last year’s bill.

The Homestead exemption and the Save Our Homes 3% assessment cap mean the effect differs sharply by how long you have owned. A long-held homesteaded property in New Port Richey will see assessed value rise by the capped amount regardless of market value; a recent purchase resets to market and absorbs the full increase. So the same millage decision lands very differently on two neighbours, and the county-level average tells an individual household almost nothing.

Pasco’s stated pressures, from the county’s budget office in earlier cycles, are the operating cost of the detention centre expansion, constitutional-officer budget growth, and personnel costs driven by workers’ compensation and retirement contributions. Those are structural and none of them fall next year. The school board levy is separate and already decided: on 8 September it set operating millage at 4.684 and capital outlay at 1.5, for a levy of $436.6m against $419.6m last year — about $17m more, roughly 4%, on an operating budget 0.9% smaller with around 2,001 fewer students and 476 posts cut. The gap is assessed values, not the rate, which is exactly the rolled-back-rate point above.

Arms transfers. The administration has informally notified Congress of a $2.8bn sale of 40,000 bombs to Israel, including 20,000 MK-84 two-thousand-pounders.

The package is reported as 20,000 MK-84 general-purpose bombs, 20,000 BLU-117 variants and a quantity of I-2000 penetrator warheads designed for hardened underground targets. The Washington Post and AFP reported it on 16 September. The State Department declined to comment, saying it does not discuss proposed sales before formal congressional notification. Funding is through Foreign Military Financing — American money, used to buy American weapons.

The stage matters and is easy to misread. Informal notification is the pre-clearance step in which the administration circulates a proposed sale to the leadership and ranking members of the House Foreign Affairs and Senate Foreign Relations committees. Those four members can place an informal hold, which is not a statutory power but has historically delayed or killed sales, and nothing becomes public or reviewable until formal notification starts the statutory clock. So this is a sale at the point where it can still quietly not happen. The penetrator warheads are the detail to watch: I-2000 class weapons are for buried targets, which is a different mission profile from the MK-84s and points at a different target set than Gaza.

Below the line because informal notification is a stage in a process, not a decision, and the package is described from reporting rather than from a notification document. It becomes a lead on formal notification, on a public hold, or on a congressional resolution of disapproval.

Immigration. DHS quietly corrected an interim final rule that had accidentally deleted the provision letting US-born children of foreign diplomats and government employees register for permanent residence.

Federal Register document 2026-18949, effective on publication today and applied retroactively to 4 September, is a correcting amendment to 8 CFR part 264. It restores language removed by an interim final rule published on 9 September that permits children born in the United States to non-citizen foreign government employees to register for lawful permanent residence. DHS waived notice and comment on the ground that the change is technical and corrects an error rather than making policy, citing 8 U.S.C. 1103, 1201 and 1302–1305.

The substance is narrow — this affects children of accredited diplomats and other foreign government personnel, a small population that does not acquire citizenship at birth because their parents are not subject to US jurisdiction in the constitutional sense, and who have a separate registration route to residence. What is worth noting is the shape rather than the effect: an interim final rule issued without comment on 9 September silently removed a benefit provision, and a second rule issued without comment a week later put it back, with a retroactive effective date covering the gap. Nobody outside the agency had an opportunity to see either. Rulemaking at this pace produces exactly this kind of error, and the ones that get corrected are the ones somebody noticed.
