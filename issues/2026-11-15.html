This brief withdrew a true story. The ten-year did go through 5%

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Correction of a correction Primary source

Correction

On 18 September this brief published a correction as its second lead. It said that on 14, 15, 16 and 17 September it had printed the ten-year Treasury yield as 5.00%, 5.041%, 5.016% and 4.943%, that Treasury's own Daily Par Yield Curve showed 4.88%, 4.91%, 4.94% and 4.86% for those dates, and that “the 5% crossing did not happen in the series of record”. It withdrew the lead story of 15 September and the claim that the ten-year was at its highest since July 2007.

Those four figures are the seven-year, not the ten-year. Treasury's published series for 14–17 September 2026 reads 4.97, 5.00, 5.01, 4.94 at the ten-year and 4.88, 4.91, 4.94, 4.86 at the seven — an exact match, on all four dates, to the column one place to the left. The ten-year reached 5.00% on 15 September and 5.01% on 16 and 18 September. The crossing happened, on the day the withdrawn story said it did, in the series of record. The vendor quotes this brief disowned were accurate to within four basis points.

The 18 September box ruled out a tenor mix-up explicitly — it checked the twenty-year and the thirty-year and found no counterpart. It did not check the seven-year, which is the adjacent column. The rule that came out of that morning (take a dated level from the issuer, not from a search summary) is still right. What failed was reading the issuer's table.

Treasury's ten-year par yield closed at 5.15% on Friday 13 November, up 29 basis points in four sessions from 4.86% on 6 November. The two-year is at 4.88%, up 37 basis points over the same four sessions. The twenty-year is 5.52% and the thirty-year 5.47%.

This is not a high. The 2026 maximum on the official series is 5.31%, set on 5 October; Friday's 5.15% ranks below it, and twenty-eight of the year's 218 sessions have closed at or above 5.00%. Checking the rank before writing “a record” is this file's 3 November rule, and it applies to the brief's own excitement as much as to anyone else's.

Dig deeper: the second publisher, and what kind of move this is

The Federal Reserve's H.15 release, which the 18 September box named as the thing that would settle the residual and said it had not reached, was reached this morning. It prints the ten-year at 4.86, 4.88, 4.98 and 5.10 for 6, 9, 10 and 12 November and the seven-year at 4.74, 4.76, 4.89 and 5.02 — identical to Treasury's file on every date, and distinct between the two tenors. Two independent publishers of the same constant-maturity construction agree, which is as close to settled as a market level gets. The error was never in the data.

H.15's own description of the method explains why the two tenors cannot be casually swapped: the constant-maturity yields are interpolated by Treasury from the daily yield curve for non-inflation-indexed securities, built from closing bid yields on actively traded issues collected by the New York Fed, and read off that curve at fixed maturities — 1, 3 and 6 months and 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 20 and 30 years. The seven and the ten are separate readings of the same curve, eleven to fifteen basis points apart this autumn, and nothing about the construction makes one a stale version of the other.

H.15 also answers what kind of move November's is. Its inflation-indexed ten-year went from 2.63% on 6 November to 2.86% on 12 November — 23 basis points — while the nominal rose 24. The implied ten-year breakeven moved one basis point, from 2.23% to 2.24%. Essentially the whole of the move is real yield. That matters for how to read it: a nominal rise driven by inflation expectations is a market pricing an energy shock, and a nominal rise that is all real yield is a market repricing either the expected path of policy or the premium it demands for holding duration. The two-year rose 37 basis points over the same four sessions, which points at the policy path; the thirty-year rose only 21, which argues against a fiscal or credit premium story. On that reading the November move is about what the Fed does in December, and the mid-December meeting carries a live 50-basis-point possibility.

For completeness on the year: 11 November was a federal holiday, markets were closed, and H.15 prints an asterisk rather than a number. That is the same calendar effect that moved the EIA weekly release from Wednesday to Thursday that week, and it is why the four-session comparison in this story runs 6 to 13 November rather than over five days.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Four numbers, on four named dates, attributed to one published series: whether Treasury's ten-year par yield on 14–17 September 2026 was 4.88/4.91/4.94/4.86 or 4.97/5.00/5.01/4.94. It is a dispute about facts between this brief's 18 September issue and the issuer, and it is fully adjudicable because the issuer publishes a machine-readable file with a header row.

Cause. Reach for the mundane explanation. Treasury's CSV for 2026 carries fifteen columns, and the ten-year sits at index twelve because the file gained a 1.5-month tenor in 2025; the 2023 and 2024 files have fourteen columns and the ten-year at index eleven. A reader who counts columns rather than reading the header lands on the seven-year, and the seven-year was the figure printed. The offset is uniform across all four dates, which is the signature of a column, not of staleness or of an intraday quote. The 18 September box argued against a systematic error on the grounds that the thirty-year ran the other way — but it was comparing the official thirty-year against a vendor's thirty-year, a different test, and that reasoning sent the investigation away from the one explanation that fits.

What it means for you. Treat the 15 September lead as restored and the 18 September withdrawal as void. The ten-year did cross 5% on the official series, on 15 September 2026, and has closed at or above it on twenty-eight sessions since. One claim stays withdrawn: “highest since July 2007” is still unverified here. What this brief checked today is that the par ten-year did not reach 5.00% in 2023, 2024 or 2025 — the closest approach in those three years was 4.98% on 19 October 2023 — and it has not pulled the years between 2008 and 2022. The generalisable rule, which now goes in the file: address a column by its header, never by its position, and when a correction depends on one cell, print the neighbouring cells too.

What to watch: whether the ten-year holds above 5% into the mid-December FOMC, which carries a live 50-basis-point possibility; the ten-year real yield, which is where this move actually is; the twenty-year and thirty-year, which have not confirmed it; and the next ten-year auction's tail.

Confidence: Primary source — the US Treasury's own Daily Par Yield Curve CSV for 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026, read by header index with the match asserted, and the Federal Reserve's H.15 release as an independent second publisher. Iran says it has mined the eastern Strait of Hormuz, and three tankers hit them

Geopolitics Energy & grid Continuation Single source

Iranian authorities said on Saturday that three oil tankers had struck naval mines while travelling through areas of the Strait of Hormuz that Iran designates unauthorised or restricted, and that all three caught fire. Iran's navy said on Friday that a liquefied natural gas carrier had struck a mine and suffered an explosion on what it called an unauthorised and dangerous route, and on Saturday morning that an oil tanker was ablaze in the eastern strait after hitting one. Reuters said it could not immediately verify the reports.

The navy's statement, carried by Iran's state broadcaster, is a declaration of policy, not an incident report: it said in terms that any offending tanker in the restricted area east of the strait will be set on fire by striking naval mines. Paraphrasing rather than quoting, because the original is Persian: Iran is saying the mines are the enforcement mechanism of the licensing regime this brief has been describing since 18 September.

The United Nations put numbers on the same waterway on Friday. Khaled Khiari, assistant secretary-general for the Middle East, Asia and the Pacific, told the Security Council that at least 25 seafarers have been killed in and around Hormuz since the war began in February, counting to 11 November, and that the International Maritime Organization has tracked 136 security incidents involving vessels in Hormuz and the wider Middle East between 1 March and 11 November — 35 of them in October and 10 so far in November. “This situation cannot be allowed to become the new normal,” Khiari said.

Set that 35 beside the 37 October attacks this brief printed on 12 November, which came from a different counter with a different geographic scope. The two are close and they are not the same measurement; the IMO figure covers Hormuz plus the wider region and counts security incidents, not attacks.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Not whether ships are burning — that is agreed — but how the explosive got to the hull. Iran's account is that vessels struck mines already in the water. The best-evidenced incident of the past month, on 30 October, was the opposite: maritime security sources described a small boat attaching two limpet mines to a tanker's hull, causing an engine-room fire. A dispute about facts, and the two accounts imply opposite risk structures.

Cause. Interest and vantage both point the same way, so prefer the mundane reading with care. The only source for Saturday's three incidents is Iranian state media, and the “hit a mine on an unauthorised route” framing does two things for Tehran at once: it moves blame to the master's routing decision, and it claims a capability — a mined approach — that is far more frightening than a boat crew with limpets, without having to demonstrate it. Against that, the 30 October limpet case shows Iran is willing to let an active method be reported. The timing matters too: the UN briefing and the Iranian claims are a day apart, and the UN was counting incidents to 11 November, before any of these three.

What it means for you. The distinction is the whole commercial question. Limpet attacks are selective: a shipowner can price them, insure them, and in principle comply its way out of them. A mined channel is indiscriminate within its area, cannot be un-laid quickly, and makes a licence worthless as protection — which is why war-risk underwriters, not navies, would be the first place the difference shows. Believe for now that Iran has mined something and that its extent is unverified. What would settle it: an underwater survey or a mine-countermeasures deployment by any navy; hull-damage photographs showing a below-waterline breach rather than a side-shell charge; a war-risk premium that rises for all eastbound transits rather than for non-compliant ones; or a single non-Iranian account of Saturday's three vessels by name. None of the three has been named.

What to watch: names, flags and insurers for the three Saturday casualties; whether any navy announces mine-clearance; the war-risk quote for the eastbound transit; the IMO's November incident count when it closes; and whether Iran repeats the mine claim or reverts to attacks it owns.

Confidence: Single source for the three Saturday incidents and for the mine claim — Iranian state media, relayed by Reuters via gCaptain and by Al Jazeera, with Reuters stating it could not verify. Independent corroboration for the UN and IMO figures. This brief has read no classification-society or underwriter document and has named none of the vessels. The nation was grounded because a contractor was swapping copper for fibre

US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said at a Saturday press conference that Friday's nationwide ground stop traced to work by the FAA's contractor L3Harris, which was converting a communications link from copper to fibre-optic cable at a remote site. Duffy said the department has not yet identified the source of the fault and named two candidates: an installation or user error by the contractor, or a defective router. The failure then caused a data overload at other communications centres.

The scale, now that the weekend reporting has settled: the FAA halted nearly all departures for almost five hours on Friday, the longest nationwide stop since the attacks of 11 September 2001. Friday produced close to 10,000 cancellations and more than 7,000 delays. On Saturday the major carriers cancelled roughly 4,000 more flights and delayed about 3,000 while trying to return to normal operations.

FAA administrator Bryan Bedford addressed the conspiracy traffic directly, saying there is “zero evidence of anything nefarious and zero evidence of anything AI-related”. The initial 4pm Eastern announcement had said only that departures were stopped due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety, which is a sentence that explains nothing and permits everything.

Then two planes collided at Miami on Saturday night — a Copa 737's wingtip into the nose of a UPS 767 while taxiing, two injured, controllers having told the UPS crew to give way. It runs below the line, because a taxi collision with an instruction on the tape is not evidence about Friday.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Where the failure was. Duffy locates it in a contractor's hands on a cable — a discrete, fixable, someone-else's-fault event. CNN's analysis locates it in the government's own communications, arguing that the official messaging turned a technical outage into a crisis of confidence and seeded theories about the real cause. A dispute about significance: both accounts are accurate and they weight different failures.

Cause. Vantage, straightforwardly. A transport secretary at a podium is answering “what broke”, and a media analyst is answering “why did Friday feel like the end of the world”. Neither is wrong and the second is cheaper to fix. Note the timing too: Duffy's naming of L3Harris came on Saturday, a day after the event, which is itself the thing the communications critique is about — the 4pm notice on Friday carried no cause at all, and the Saturday briefing began late because of technical problems.

What it means for you, and the part neither reading covers. The evidence supports Duffy on mechanism and the critics on consequence, and the question worth more than either is why a cable cutover at one remote site could propagate into a national stop. That is a question about the FAA's telecommunications architecture and about the contract under which it is being modernised, and it is answerable from documents: the incident report, the L3Harris task order, and the FAA's own resilience standard for the link that failed. What would settle the stated dispute: the preliminary incident report naming either a hardware fault or an installation error. Those two answers point at completely different remedies — a parts recall versus a contract and oversight problem — and the administration has told you it does not yet know which.

What to watch: the FAA's preliminary incident report; whether L3Harris or the FAA is identified as the responsible party; whether the same cutover programme runs at other sites and whether it is paused; the congressional response when the new Congress is sworn in; and Microsoft's still-missing post-incident review of Thursday's outage.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — Handelsblatt, in German, for Duffy's account of the L3Harris cable work and the cancellation totals, and CNN for Bedford's statement, the 4pm wording and the communications critique. Everything from the German source is paraphrase. This brief has read no FAA document. Sources: Handelsblatt · CNN · 14 November

Eight countries have had their US health funding cut to zero

US politics & policy US debt & fiscal New subject Single source

An internal State Department budget report and a memo to lawmakers, both obtained by CNN, show the administration redirecting or withholding almost the whole of the $607.5 million Congress appropriated this year for international family planning — about $605 million of it. The same preliminary fiscal 2026 allocation moves close to a billion dollars into a global health innovation fund that benefits US corporations, while cutting health assistance for low- and middle-income countries.

Tens of millions of the family planning money is being redirected to the “energy security needs” of US partners facing market disruption from the wars in Iran and Ukraine, under a memo signed by Secretary of State Marco Rubio in September. The memo gives little detail on how the money would be spent.

The country-level numbers come from an analysis by Public Citizen with Partners In Health and Population Action International. South Africa, Tanzania and Zimbabwe each lose more than $100 million. Eight countries — Afghanistan, the Central African Republic, Gambia, Somalia, South Africa, Sudan, Timor-Leste and Yemen — have had health funding cut to zero since 2024.

A State Department spokesman told CNN the administration is continually evaluating foreign assistance against critical needs, including unanticipated contingencies such as the energy crisis, within its appropriated budget and “the authorities available to the president and the secretary”. Vincent Lin of Partners In Health said the executive branch is “flagrantly not spending the money that Congress has written into law”.

This is not the first instance of the mechanism. CNN has previously reported $1.35 billion in global health money redirected to pay for closing USAID, and Public Citizen is suing the State Department to force release of records on the bilateral health agreements the department has kept confidential.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether moving appropriated money to a different purpose is reprogramming, which presidents do, or impoundment, which the Impoundment Control Act forbids without notifying Congress and obtaining its assent. A dispute about facts with a legal test, not about significance: both sides agree the money is not being spent as the appropriation describes.

Cause. The State Department's answer is a careful one and worth reading closely: it rests on “the authorities available to the president and the secretary”, which is a claim that the transfers are inside existing transfer and reprogramming authority, and it emphasises what the money now funds rather than what it was appropriated for. The advocacy organisations' answer rests on the appropriation's own text. Each has the vantage of what it can see. The department holds the allocation document and the legal opinion; the nonprofits hold a leaked copy and a FOIA docket. Note the publication timing: this is a preliminary allocation for a fiscal year already under way, which means the obligation deadlines that would make an impoundment legally final have mostly not arrived.

What it means for you. The evidence as it stands supports the factual claim — the money is going somewhere else — and does not yet establish the legal one. Reprogramming within a statutory authority and impoundment look identical in a budget table and differ in paperwork. Believe meanwhile that roughly $605 million of a $607.5 million appropriation has been diverted and that eight countries are at zero. What would settle it: a Government Accountability Office opinion, which is the body that formally decides Impoundment Control Act violations; a special message to Congress proposing rescission, whose absence is itself evidence; or the transfer authority cited by section number in the memo to lawmakers. This brief has read none of the three documents in the story.

What to watch: any GAO opinion; whether appropriators attach a reporting requirement in the next bill; the Public Citizen lawsuit's document releases; whether the global health innovation fund publishes its recipients; and what the energy-security transfers actually bought.

Confidence: Single source — one newsroom holds the budget report, the memo to lawmakers and the Rubio memo, and the country analysis was shared with it exclusively before publication. The State Department's response is on the record. This brief has read no primary document in this story. Sources: CNN

The Jones Act is waived for another ninety days from midnight tonight

Energy & grid US politics & policy New subject Single source

President Trump has decided to extend by ninety days the waiver of the Jones Act's requirement that cargo moving between US ports travel on American-owned, American-flagged and American-crewed ships, according to a White House official briefing on condition of anonymity before the decision was public. It takes effect at 12am Monday New York time, two days before the existing waiver expired, so there is no gap.

The scale of what the waiver has already done is on the record. Government data analysed by the Cato Institute shows 338 voyages on foreign vessels since Trump first waived the restrictions in March, including Texas crude to Pennsylvania and propane to Puerto Rico. The White House official said California has taken the majority — 23 million barrels of additional product this period against 8 million in the same period last year — and that about 90 million barrels, mostly refined fuels, have moved between US ports under the waiver.

The breadth is unchanged from August, when the administration narrowed the waiver to require case-by-case government review of whether a US vessel was available.

The stated reason is the cost of energy after the war in Iran, following midterm elections that turned on the cost of living and went badly for the party holding the waiver pen. The maritime industry had publicly urged the president to let this waiver lapse on 15 November.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the waiver lowers fuel prices enough to justify the damage to the domestic fleet. Oil and chemical shippers say it has moved crude, fertiliser and refined product between US ports cheaply and offset the war's disruption. Shipbuilders and their congressional allies say it undercuts the mandate that sustains US shipbuilding capacity. A dispute about significance — both descriptions of the effect are true — with a quantitative core that is not being argued honestly on either side.

Cause. Interest, undisguised, on both sides; this is a classic protected-industry fight and neither party is reporting from a vantage the other lacks. What is more interesting is the asymmetry in what each can measure. The shippers can point to 338 voyages and 90 million barrels, which are counts of activity, not of savings. The shipbuilders cannot show a single cancelled hull, because the fleet's decline long predates March and no new ship would have been ordered in eight months anyway. Both arguments substitute a visible number for the number that matters.

What it means for you, and the measurement nobody has published. The waiver's price effect is a differential — the Jones Act freight rate minus the foreign rate, times the barrels moved — and the administration has published the barrels without the rates for eight months. At a plausible Gulf-to-East-Coast differential of a few dollars a barrel, 90 million barrels is a few hundred million dollars spread across a national fuel bill measured in hundreds of billions: real money, and not the reason the pump price has fallen 51 cents a gallon in a month. Believe that the waiver is a modest, genuine saving and not the cause of the recent fall. What would settle it: the case-by-case vessel-availability determinations the August narrowing created, which are decision documents that must exist and have not been published, and a published Jones Act versus foreign rate spread for the Gulf-to-East-Coast clean products run.

What to watch: the proclamation text when it publishes and whether it narrows the scope; whether the vessel-availability determinations are released; the 90-day expiry around 14 February 2027; whether the new Congress legislates on the waiver authority; and whether California's share holds.

No proclamation has been published; this brief has read no document. Sources: Bloomberg via gCaptain

Russia promised America diesel. One cargo turned around 400 miles off New York

Energy & grid Macro & markets Continuation Single source

The tanker Agioi Fanendes, carrying about 40,000 tonnes of ultra-low-sulphur diesel — almost 300,000 barrels — signalled New York after leaving Russia's Baltic port of Primorsk in late October, then changed its destination to “for orders”, turned away about 400 nautical miles off the US East Coast and has been zigzagging since Monday. Its manager, Eastern Mediterranean Maritime, says the ship is proceeding for orders because the discharge port is not confirmed.

No Russian diesel cargo has reached America under the deal President Putin agreed with President Trump last month, according to ship-tracking data. Russia said it had loaded four tankers with US-bound diesel, per Interfax. Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak said in late October that Russia aimed to export more than two million tonnes of diesel to global markets in November, while warning that Ukrainian attacks might trim supply.

Those attacks are the other half of the story. Six Russian refineries have been hit since 1 November, on a Bloomberg tally, totalling about 1.7 million barrels a day of design capacity.

Meanwhile the American price is falling fast without it. EIA's weekly retail diesel average was $6.127 a gallon on 9 November, down 16.5 cents in a week; AAA's daily average this morning is $6.0198, down 17.3 cents on the week and 49.5 cents below the $6.5276 record of 22 September.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the Putin–Trump diesel arrangement exists as a flow of cargoes. Russia's position, via Interfax and Novak, is that four tankers were loaded for the United States and more than two million tonnes will be exported this month. Ship-tracking data says zero cargoes have arrived and at least one turned back within sight of the coast. A dispute about facts, and an unusually clean one: arrivals are observable.

Cause. Three mundane explanations, and they are not exclusive. First, loading is not delivery — both claims can be true if the cargoes exist and cannot be discharged. Second, the buyers. A US refiner or terminal taking a Russian-origin cargo needs a counterparty, an insurer and a bank comfortable with the sanctions position, and the deal drew immediate criticism from congressional Democrats on the grounds that it finances the war; no terminal operator wants to be the first name in that story. Third, supply. Six refineries hit in two weeks is 1.7 million barrels a day of capacity under attack, and a country short of its own diesel does not export it. The U-turn 400 miles out and the “for orders” status fit the second explanation best: a ship with a cargo and no buyer behaves exactly like this.

What it means for you, and what changed underneath. The arrangement was sold as a way to lower US fuel prices before the midterms. The midterms have happened, no cargo arrived, and the price fell anyway — 49.5 cents off the September record at the pump, on a crude price that dropped $9.62 in the week to 6 November. So treat the deal as commercially stalled and analytically irrelevant to the US diesel price. The settling observation is a discharge: one Russian-origin ULSD cargo landing at a named US terminal. Until then the question worth watching is not the deal but the margin: the crack spread was $97.99 on 6 November, and the next EIA weekly release on Wednesday 18 November is the test of whether a falling pump price is reaching the refiner or the driver.

What to watch: whether the Agioi Fanendes names a port; any US terminal receiving Russian-origin ULSD; the other three loaded tankers; Russian refinery outages against Novak's two-million-tonne target; and Wednesday's EIA release.

Wholesale inflation is 6.5% and the pump is falling. Both are true

Macro & markets Continuation Primary source

The producer price index for final demand rose 6.52% in the twelve months to October, on BLS's own published index: 160.248 against 150.439 a year earlier. That is the highest annual rate since November 2022 according to the reporting; this brief has verified the October figure from the series and has not verified the 2022 comparison, for reasons set out below. The reported seasonally adjusted monthly rise was 0.3%; the unadjusted index rose 0.45% in October after 1.18% in September, which was the energy shock's largest single month.

The composition is where this stops being an energy story. Food prices rose 1.3% in October after 1.1% in September — the fastest monthly increase since July 2022 — with the sharpest rises in produce, which moves by truck. Freight transportation costs for trucking are up nearly 25% on the year.

And at the same time, retail fuel is falling hard. AAA's national average this morning: regular $3.8242, down 19.6 cents on the week and 51.0 cents on the month; diesel $6.0198, down 17.3 cents on the week and 29.8 cents on the month. The diesel premium over regular is $2.1956 this morning, four hundredths of a cent below yesterday's $2.1960 — which ends a run of eight consecutive record highs. That is a rounding error in itself and the end of a streak is not a turn; what it establishes is only that the ninth record did not happen.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Nothing about the numbers — the dispute is what the reader is being told about their own costs. One set of figures says US input-cost inflation is running at a four-year high; another says fuel has fallen half a dollar a gallon in a month. A dispute about significance, and the cause is the one this file says is most often missed.

Cause: timing, almost entirely. The PPI print is a measurement of October. AAA's average is a measurement of this morning. Crude fell $9.62 in the week to 6 November, which is after the reference period the PPI covers, so the October print cannot contain it and the November print will. There is also an instrument difference of the kind this file warned about on 22 September: a daily card average measures today, and a monthly producer index measures a month, so they cannot contradict each other about the same quantity.

What it means for you. Both readings are right and the fuel one is ahead by a month, so expect the headline PPI rate to fall from here and the food component not to. That is the asymmetry worth holding: fuel passes through to the pump in days, and through trucking into produce in months. Food PPI rising 1.3% then 1.1% while trucking freight is up 25% is a pass-through already in motion that a falling diesel price will not reverse before the new year. What would settle it: the November PPI print on the food and freight components specifically, not the headline.

One limit, disclosed rather than glossed. The 6.52% is this brief's own calculation from BLS's published index for final demand, read through the public API. The core rate was not independently verified: bls.gov returned 403 this morning, and the API's unregistered daily request allowance ran out before the brief could confirm which series the reported 5% core figure is drawn from. Given that today's first lead is about a column read by position instead of by name, printing a number whose series this brief cannot name would be the same mistake twice in one issue. So it is not printed.

What to watch: the November PPI, especially food and freight; whether the trucking freight index decelerates as diesel falls; the diesel premium over regular, now that the record run has stopped; and the mid-December FOMC, which is reading these prints.

Single source for the composition figures (food, produce, trucking freight) and for the November 2022 comparison, which come from CNN's report of the release. A regulator deleted its own fair-lending rule, in a sweep of four

US financials New subject Underreported Primary source

The National Credit Union Administration will publish a final rule on Monday removing 12 CFR 701.31, its nondiscrimination regulation, in its entirety. The section was promulgated in 1972 to centralise the lending-discrimination requirements applying to federal credit unions; it covers nondiscrimination in loans and appraisals, advertising, notices and logos, and compliance guidance under the Fair Housing Act. Its last substantive amendment was in 2001. The rule takes effect thirty days after publication.

The Board's reasoning is that the section is redundant. The Fair Housing Act and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, with HUD's and the CFPB's implementing regulations, apply to federal credit unions whether or not the NCUA restates them; the Federal Credit Union Act does not require the agency to help credit unions comply; and 701.31 has not kept pace with case law. Sixteen public comments were received by the 16 March close. The rule never applied to federally insured state-chartered credit unions.

It is one of four NCUA final rules in Monday's issue. The others cover registration of mortgage loan originators, privacy of consumer financial information, and accuracy of advertising and notice of insured status — 28 pages between them, 37 with the rescission, all filed for the same day's issue.

This brief had this thread marked closed since 15 October. It is open again, on a dated threshold event, and no wire sweep reached this morning carried it.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether removing a duplicative rule changes anything a borrower experiences. A dispute about significance, and both positions are legally correct about different things.

Cause. The agency is reasoning about statutory obligation and its critics about supervisory practice, and those come apart in a way that is easy to miss. The agency is right that the statutes bind regardless: nothing a credit union may now do was unlawful yesterday and lawful tomorrow. What a rule in the agency's own chapter provides is a hook for the agency's own examiners — an NCUA examination cites NCUA regulations, and an FHA claim is brought by HUD or a private plaintiff in court. Remove the hook and the standard survives while the cheapest enforcement route for it does not. The vantage difference is complete: the Board sees a statute book, an examiner sees a checklist. Note also what the comment count tells you — sixteen comments is almost nobody, which is how a change with this shape moves without an argument.

What it means for you. Believe the agency on law and the critics on practice. If you borrow from a federal credit union your rights under the Fair Housing Act and ECOA are unchanged; what changes is who is likely to notice a violation first and at what cost to you. The measurable consequence is the examination manual, not the regulation — and the four rules together describe a direction rather than an incident. What would settle it: the NCUA's examiner guidance after the effective date, whether fair-lending findings appear in its next supervisory priorities letter, and the sixteen comments themselves, which this brief has not read. The preamble's response to them is the document to get.

What to watch: the NCUA's 2027 supervisory priorities letter and whether fair lending survives in it; the preamble's treatment of the sixteen comments; the effective date, thirty days after Monday; whether other prudential regulators follow with their own restatements; and the other three rules' substance.

Supreme Court · The Court took a securities case on Friday that will decide whether failing to disclose interim financial results in a registration statement can be liability on its own, and no wire sweep this morning had it.

In Robinhood Markets, Inc. v. Sodha, No. 25-944, the Court granted certiorari limited to one question: whether an issuer can be held liable under the misleading-omissions prong of section 11(a) of the Securities Act of 1933 on a finding that it failed to disclose material interim financial information, without a further inquiry into whether the omission made any affirmative statement misleading.

That is a narrow-sounding question with a wide reach. Section 11 imposes near-strict liability for a defective registration statement, with no need to prove intent or reliance, and the limiting principle most courts apply is that an omission must render something actually said misleading. Remove that requirement and every IPO and secondary registration carries exposure for any material interim number the issuer did not volunteer. The Court took the question as framed by the petitioner, which usually signals interest in reversing.

Below the line because it is a grant, not a decision: argument will be in the new year and the judgment will not land before July, and the question as limited may narrow further at argument. It is here rather than absent because the grant itself reprices disclosure risk for every company planning a registration, and because this brief read it in the Court's own order list rather than in anyone's coverage.

Supreme Court · The Court will decide whether the president may categorically bar migrants arriving at the southern border from applying for asylum, on two of the questions presented.

The same order list granted certiorari in Mullin, Secretary of Homeland Security v. Refugee and Immigrant Center, No. 26-239, limited to Questions 1 and 2 presented by the petition. The policy is a proclamation signed on the president's first day back in office suspending entry for migrants described as engaged in an invasion across the southern border, and restricting their ability to invoke the asylum protections in federal law until the president finds the invasion has ceased.

The statutory problem is plain: the Immigration and Nationality Act, enacted by Congress, permits a migrant to apply for asylum while leaving the grant to the administration's discretion. Thirteen individuals and three immigrant-rights organisations sued last year, and a federal appeals court in April upheld the district court's finding that the policy violated the statute. Argument is expected early next year with a decision before July. The term already carries the administration's removals to unconnected third countries and its mandatory-detention push.

Below the line because the grant was widely covered and the substance is a year away. What this brief adds to the coverage is the order's own limitation to Questions 1 and 2, which bounds what the Court will actually answer.

Confidence: Primary source for the grant and its limitation; independent corroboration for the procedural history. Sources: Order list, 13 November 2026 · CNN

AI & tech · The Treasury secretary said on television that Treasury, State, the Pentagon and the trade representative all wanted Nvidia's customer Megaspeed blacklisted, and that Commerce did not do it.

Scott Bessent, speaking on CNBC on Friday, accused the Commerce Department of delay in designating Megaspeed International a possible national security threat, and said a wide span of the administration — Treasury, State, the Pentagon, the US Trade Representative — favoured putting the company on an export blacklist. Advisers are discussing whether Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick should go; the White House said on Thursday the president retains complete confidence in him.

The mechanism underneath the blame is the part that matters. Megaspeed went in three years from a dormant Chinese gaming subsidiary to one of Asia's largest buyers of top-end US semiconductors, operating from Singapore and installing billions of dollars of Nvidia chips in data centres across South-East Asia. US export controls govern where a chip is shipped; they do not by themselves govern who computes on it once it is racked in a permitted country. Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, 43, whom US officials believe to be a Chinese intelligence asset, helped establish the company. Beijing calls the claim groundless.

Below the line because today's movement is an interagency blame exchange and a personnel question, which this file's anti-topics discount; the Entity List decision itself has still not been made, and that is what would make it a lead.

Aviation · A Copa 737's wingtip hit the nose of a UPS 767 taxiing at Miami on Saturday night, a day after the national grounding; controllers had told the UPS crew to give way.

Platforms · Italy's data protection authority fined Meta €805 million over a Facebook feature that read the whole photo library of about 20 million Android users, and Meta's argument that only Ireland may judge it did not hold.

The Garante fined Meta €805 million for improper access to photos and videos on smartphones, finding that users of the Android app were not adequately informed and that no effective consent was obtained. The authority says about 20 million Android users in Italy were affected and that the penalty is about 0.47% of Meta's worldwide 2024 revenue.

The feature, available since 2023, surfaces photos and videos from the phone's gallery as suggestions for the user's own posts. Gallery access could be switched on with a single general permission covering several features, after which the app could read the entire photo archive including metadata, normally permanently. The investigation began in July 2025 on a tip from the consumer protection centre in Bolzano. Meta disputed the Italian authority's jurisdiction, pointing to the Irish regulator where its European headquarters sits — the one-stop-shop argument that has absorbed most GDPR enforcement against US platforms for eight years.

Below the line because the US channel is real but indirect: a fine at 0.47% of global revenue is not material to Meta's accounts, and the jurisdictional holding matters more than the money but will be appealed. It is this morning's negative check — the biggest story no thread pointed at — and it cleared on that channel.

Confidence: Single source — Handelsblatt, in German, reporting the authority's announcement; everything here is paraphrase. This brief has not read the Garante's decision. Sources: Handelsblatt

Space · SpaceX caught a Starship with the launch tower's arms for the first time, which turns the largest rocket ever built into one that might be reused in days rather than months.

SpaceX's Starship was captured by the launchpad's arms on its return, the first time the upper stage of the vehicle has been caught rather than landed or expended. Handelsblatt's German service reports the same event as a first for the largest rocket system ever built.

The engineering point is narrow and consequential. A rocket that lands on legs needs recovery, transport and inspection; a rocket caught by the tower it launched from skips all three. The economics of everything Starship is supposed to carry — large constellations, lunar cargo, anything priced per kilogram to orbit — depends on turnaround time, not on launch success. A catch is the first physical demonstration that the turnaround can be short.

Below the line because it is a test milestone, not an operational capability: one catch is not a cadence, and the US channel is a company's roadmap rather than a policy or price that has changed. It is here because this brief's Starshield and FCC space threads both run on the assumption that launch cost keeps falling, and this is the mechanism by which that assumption is either vindicated or not.

Regulation · The FCC is correcting three effective dates it failed to update in its submarine cable security rules — a clerical fix that is the best available description of the regime it governs.

A final rule publishing Monday corrects amendatory instructions 13, 17 and 19 in the FCC's submarine cable landing licence rules (OI Docket 24-523, MD Docket 24-524, FCC 25-49). When the Commission announced the rules' effective date of 8 July 2026, it did not update those three instructions; this document fixes them.

What the three provisions require is the interesting part. Licensees holding a cable landing licence granted before 8 July 2026 must file a route position list within sixty days of that date, waivable only on a showing that the information is unavailable. Licensees must file an initial Foreign Adversary Annual Report within six months of it, and annually after. And each licensee must certify within sixty days that it will not add equipment or services on the Commission's Covered List to its cable systems — except where the licensee is itself on the Covered List and holds a licence granted before that date. Every undersea cable landing in the United States now carries a mapped route on file with the regulator and an annual adversary disclosure.

Below the line because it is a correcting amendment and nothing substantive changed today. It runs because a reader who follows data-centre and cloud concentration should know this regime exists, and because the carve-out for licensees already on the Covered List is the kind of clause that decides cases.

Confidence: Primary source — the rule as filed for public inspection, read in the original. Sources: FCC, correcting amendment
