The Justice Department wants an Alaska Senate result withheld if the Republican loses

US politics Courts New subject Independent corroboration

Late on Thursday the Justice Department sued in federal court in Alaska to stop the state from certifying its Senate result. The relief it asks for is conditional on who wins. Certification would be blocked, after the election, until the government has investigated whether votes cast for a candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan — a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg — were actually intended for the Republican incumbent, Senator Dan Sullivan, who is in a tight race against the Democrat Mary Peltola. It is a request to hold a result open only if one side loses it.

The mechanism has an obvious problem and the Department has not addressed it. Ballots are secret. There is no lawful way to ask a voter who marked "Daniel J. Sullivan" which Sullivan they meant, and the suit does not say how the investigation it demands would be conducted. The legal hook is the National Voter Registration Act and a duty on states to avoid voter confusion.

Two facts sit awkwardly with the stated rationale. Alaska's own Republican election officials already tried to remove the second Sullivan from the ballot, and a state court and the Alaska Supreme Court kept him on — a decision Attorney General Todd Blanche says "explicitly didn't consider federal law". And the state's other Republican senator, Lisa Murkowski, who is backing her colleague's re-election, said Alaska had already distinguished the two candidates and the Department should not have sued.

Asked on CNN whether this was groundwork for the President to challenge next week's results, Blanche said: "We're not laying any groundwork for the president to do that. President Trump is not trying to take over a state election in any way, shape, or form." He had earlier told reporters the Department could get involved in certification in a "doomsday" scenario. When the interview turned to 2020 and 6 January he said, "Oh, you got to stop it with that. You are not being a real person. Come on, man." On Friday, in Florida, the President said of the suit: "I'm the one that said do it."

Divergence. The dispute is about what the suit is for, and the two readings turn on the same facts. Read one, the Department's: this is ordinary election-law enforcement. Two candidates share a name, a state court declined to consider the federal question, and the remedy is narrow — Blanche's formulation is "not to certify if the law is broken", which he says Alaska's courts would decide. Read two: it is a rehearsal. The suit is conditional on a Republican loss, the President says he ordered it, the remedy has no workable mechanism, and the effect of filing is to establish that a federal department may ask a court to pause a certification.

Why the difference matters. Under read one the case is about Alaska and ends there. Under read two the filing is the point: the precedent is available in any state next month, and the value is in having asked, not in winning. The cause of the divergence is that intent is unobservable and both readings fit the record. How to read it: watch what the Department does in the other close races. One suit about a genuinely duplicated name is a coincidence; a second filing elsewhere is a pattern, and it will arrive, if it does, in the ten days after 3 November.

What to watch: whether the Alaska court sets a hearing before election day; whether DOJ explains how intent behind a secret ballot could be determined; and whether similar suits are filed in other states with close Senate races.

Measles has killed five people in Pennsylvania and cases have more than doubled nationally

Science & public health New subject Independent corroboration

The CDC's count stands at more than 4,580 measles cases in the United States this year, more than double the total for all of 2025. It is the worst resurgence in decades, and the vaccination status of 96 per cent of this year's cases is recorded as "unvaccinated or unknown".

Pennsylvania is the centre of it. 1,338 confirmed cases across 44 counties, 21 of them new in the week to 21 October; more than 250 people hospitalised; five dead. Three of the five deaths were in Lancaster County, and Lancaster and Mifflin are where Amish communities with low vaccination rates have been hit hardest. The state's health secretary, Dr Debra Bogen, spent Thursday with Mifflin County providers, saying the state's own metrics "tell part of the story" and that listening to clinicians is "essential to refining our public health response".

The deaths are the number to hold on to. Measles kills roughly one to three per thousand of those infected in a country with this standard of care, so five deaths in 1,338 confirmed cases is a high ratio, which usually means the true case count is larger than the confirmed one — a figure of 1,338 among communities that are not routinely vaccinated and may not routinely present is a floor.

There is one encouraging line. Pennsylvania administered more than 39,000 measles vaccines in the first three weeks of October, against a normal month's 25,000. Two doses of MMR are about 97 per cent effective, one dose about 93. Elsewhere: New York has 160-odd cases and Governor Hochul has declared a disaster emergency; Utah has had more than 700 this year but none in three weeks; South Carolina recorded 997 before its outbreak ended in April.

What to watch: whether Pennsylvania's vaccination surge shows up as a slowing case count in three to four weeks, which is the incubation lag; whether the Utah pattern — a large outbreak that simply stops — repeats; and whether any federal action follows beyond data publication.

Reactor designs can now be licensed partly on the Pentagon's say-so

Energy & grid Regulatory New subject Primary document Underreported

A final rule published this morning changes who gets to decide that a new reactor design is safe. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission has added §50.43(f), letting a commercial licence applicant reference a prior Department of Energy or Department of War authorisation as the means of showing that a design performs its safety functions. A matching change lands at §53.440 for the risk-informed framework, and a parallel provision sits in the pending microreactor rule.

The context is that DOE and the military authorise reactors under their own authority, for research and defence purposes, through processes that are not the NRC's. Those processes have no public licensing proceeding, no intervenor, and no hearing. What the rule does is let that record do work inside a civilian licensing case, so that part of the safety demonstration has already happened somewhere a member of the public could not have participated.

The NRC is not hiding the purpose. The stated intent is "to improve NRC licensing review efficiency, and in turn increase capacity, for conducting licensing reviews" — which is to say the Commission has more applications than it can process and is looking for work it does not have to repeat. On its own terms that is reasonable: a design that has been built, tested and run at a national laboratory has produced real evidence, and making an applicant generate it again is waste.

The question the rule does not answer is what the NRC does when it disagrees. Referencing is an option for the applicant, not a binding finding on the Commission, and the rule preserves flexibility to weigh the material "as appropriate and supported by an applicant". But an agency short of capacity, handed a safety case already blessed by a sister department, is under a quiet pressure to accept it. Independence is cheapest to give away when you are busy.

What to watch: the first licence application to use §50.43(f), and whether it is a microreactor; whether any party seeks a hearing on the sufficiency of a referenced DOE authorisation; and whether the pending part 57 microreactor rule carries the provision through to a final rule.

Confidence: Primary document, published today and read at source. The summary and the operative provisions were read; the statement of considerations and the response to comments were not, so this brief cannot say what objections were raised during the rulemaking or how the Commission answered them. Lula has won a fourth term, and the loser says he will try to annul it

Foreign policy New subject Independent corroboration

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has been elected president of Brazil for a fourth, non-consecutive term, taking more than 51 per cent against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro's more than 48, with over 96 per cent of Sunday's runoff counted. Lula turns 81 on Tuesday.

One structural fact makes this unusual rather than merely close. Since Brazil restored democracy in 1989, no candidate who lost the first round had ever won the second. Bolsonaro led on 4 October; Lula overturned it in three weeks. Lula called the result "a defeat of denialism. A defeat of lies," and, in a line aimed at Washington, said Brazil would not accept being "anyone's backyard".

Election day was disorderly in a way that matters for what follows. Both campaigns asked for voting hours to be extended — Lula's citing reduced bus services in some municipalities, Bolsonaro's citing heavy rain. Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin granted two extra hours to 7 p.m., but some polling stations had already shut down their electronic voting machines and could not resume. That is a genuine irregularity, it was created by a remedy both sides requested, and it is now the raw material for the challenge.

Bolsonaro, speaking outside his home in Brasília, called the result "inexplicable" and said he would seek to annul the vote: "I will not give up on my Brazil, and I hope you won't give up either." He is 45, campaigned on closer ties with President Trump, and had promised to pardon his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year sentence under house arrest for plotting to overturn his 2022 defeat to the same opponent.

What to watch: whether the annulment claim is filed with the electoral court and what it rests on, since the two-hour extension is the only concrete irregularity on the record; whether the US recognises the result promptly; and what Lula's "backyard" line means for Brazilian policy on Venezuela.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. Figures are NPR's reporting of official results at 96 per cent counted; this brief did not reach the TSE's own tally, and the result was not final at the time of writing. The historical claim about first-round losers is NPR's. Sources: NPR · Al Jazeera

María Corina Machado went home, and Washington is briefing against itself

Foreign policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief corrected itself for claiming its archive had never covered the US capture of Nicolás Maduro. Today the thread it should have been running produces a story. María Corina Machado, the Venezuelan opposition leader and Nobel peace laureate, has returned to Venezuela after ten months abroad, posting "I'm here, with you all." She appeared first among worshippers at the Church of Santa Rosa in Lara state, then drove to Caracas, where crowds surrounded her car.

Her return is a problem for the arrangement the United States installed. Interim president Delcy Rodríguez took over after US forces captured Maduro in January. Machado wants elections soon; Washington's position is that Venezuela must stabilise first, and the administration has repeatedly sidelined her despite her standing in the opposition. A Nobel laureate with crowds in the street is harder to sideline from inside the country than from Oslo.

The two American responses do not match, and that is the story. Secretary of State Marco Rubio issued a statement claiming her return was a "direct result" of US-supported reconciliation talks, which "represent the best chance Venezuela has had in thirty years" at an enduring democracy. The President, leaving the White House on Sunday afternoon, said he knew nothing about it, called Machado "a very nice woman", noted that she had given him her Nobel Prize, said "I hope she's safe" and added that "we're doing really well in Venezuela."

One of those is a policy achievement being claimed and the other is a head of state learning the news from reporters. Either the State Department did not tell the President, or the claim of credit was made after the fact. Edmundo González, who ran against Maduro in 2024 and is in exile in Spain, said he expects to join her: "this is until the end."

What to watch: whether Rodríguez's government moves against Machado, which would test Rubio's claim that freedoms "continue to be respected"; whether any election date is named; and whether González actually returns.

Tech & AI policy Driverless cars would get their own colour: NHTSA proposes cyan marker lamps.

NHTSA has proposed amendments to FMVSS No. 108, the lighting standard, to accommodate vehicles with Automated Driving Systems and no manual controls. Most of it is housekeeping: a test-voltage change, removal of vertical headlamp arrangement rules, and limiting requirements for manually operated turn signal, hazard and beam-switch controls to vehicles that actually have manual driving controls. A car with no steering wheel does not need a stalk.

One proposal is not housekeeping. The rule would permit cyan-coloured ADS marker lamps as optional equipment on ADS-equipped vehicles. That is a proposal to give "this vehicle is driving itself" a colour — a signal aimed at other drivers, pedestrians and police rather than at the occupant, in a standard that has reserved colours for a century. It is optional, which means the public would learn the convention unevenly, from whichever manufacturers adopt it.

Below the line because it is a proposal on a standard that governs lamps, and the substantive question — whether the public can tell what the car is doing — is one NHTSA is approaching sideways through an optional lighting colour. Comments close 25 November.

Confidence: Primary document, summary read. Sources: FR 2026-21794

Regulatory The FCC's other robocall document asks whether to require a "revoke all" button — in the same edition as the rule that narrowed revocation.

On 24 October this brief led on an FCC final rule titled "Enhancing the Ability of Consumers To Control the Robocalls", reporting that it lets a caller read a revocation as applying only to one category of message and lets the caller designate an exclusive channel for revoking. That was accurate and it was incomplete. A companion proposed rule sits in the same edition, titled "Modernizing the Ability of Consumers To Control Robocalls", and it proposes the opposite movement.

The NPRM seeks comment on reducing the time callers have to honour a revocation (now ten business days), on requiring two-way texting functionality so a reply actually reaches someone, on requiring a "revoke all" method, and on the treatment of affiliates — which is the loophole where one consent covers a family of companies. A "revoke all" requirement would substantially undo the category-splitting the final rule permits.

Below the line because it is a request for comment and the final rule is the thing with legal effect on 25 November. It is here because the 24 October story should have carried it: both documents were in the docket this brief had already pre-read. Comments 25 November, replies 28 December.

Confidence: Primary document, summary read. Sources: FR 2026-21820 · 24 October

Starshield Musk says SpaceX did restrict Starlink over Ukrainian drone strikes, and that Starshield is the government's to decide.

After more than a week of disruption, Ukraine said on Saturday that Starlink and Starshield are back to their previous speeds; President Zelenskyy said the restoration began overnight. Musk has now confirmed the restriction was real. "Ukraine military violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people," he wrote. "That loophole was closed last week." He did not say who closed it.

The division he draws is the point this brief has been chasing since September. Starlink is a commercial service whose terms SpaceX enforces; Starshield, he said on 14 October, "is owned and controlled by the U.S. government", and "any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS." Zelenskyy told NBC he believed the US government was behind the restrictions and that SpaceX acted on "a message from [the] American government". Both accounts can be true at once, and between them they describe a single private network in which the commercial half is throttled by its owner and the military half by Washington, with no visible line between them from Kyiv.

Below the line because the restriction has been lifted and what remains is two parties characterising a resolved episode. The governance question it exposes is a lead when a document appears.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. Posts on X as reported by NPR; none read directly. Sources: NPR

Health A nurse answering an Ebola alert was killed in Tshopo province.

A nurse has been killed and another health worker severely injured after being attacked while responding to an Ebola alert in Bafwasende, in the DRC's eastern Tshopo province, on Saturday. The WHO director-general announced it. Authorities say it is the latest in a series of attacks on responders, and that those attacks are hampering efforts to slow what the WHO calls the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak in the world.

This matters mechanically, not only morally. The vaccine trial this brief reported on Saturday works by ring vaccination — immunising the contacts of confirmed cases, and their contacts. That method depends entirely on health workers being able to go to where a case is reported and trace the people around it. An attack on a responder answering an alert is an attack on the one technique available against a strain with no licensed vaccine.

Below the line because it is a single incident reported in a post, with no count of the wider series of attacks reached and no WHO situation report read. It is the clearest sign yet that the limiting factor in this outbreak is access rather than medicine.

Confidence: Single-sourced. Sources: Al Jazeera · 24 October

Regulatory The FCC is building a satellite "licensing assembly line" and calls part 25 a confusing tangle.

The Commission has adopted a Further Notice of Proposed Rulemaking in its Space Modernization for the 21st Century proceeding, alongside an order creating a new part 100 for satellite licensing. The structure is deliberate: subpart B for application and review, subpart C for operations under an authorisation, subpart D for enforcement. The Commission's own description of the regime it is replacing is that "part 25 is a confusing tangle", and its metaphor for the replacement is a "licensing assembly line" moving requests "in standard and predictable ways".

The framing is unusually explicit about purpose. The FNPRM opens by citing Executive Order 14369 of December 2025 — "[s]uperiority in space is a measure of national vision and willpower" — and places the rulemaking inside a "Build America Agenda". A licensing regime justified by national superiority is one in which the tie-breaker on a contested application is already named, and the question for the comment round is what remains of the countervailing considerations: interference, orbital debris, and competitors who are not American.

Below the line because the order's substance is in an appendix this brief did not read and the FNPRM is a request for comment on further reforms. It is a lead when the new part 100 is read in full.

Confidence: Primary document, introduction only. Sources: FR 2026-21818
