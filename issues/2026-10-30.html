The refineries are running harder than in any of the last four autumns

Energy & grid Correction Primary source

Yesterday this brief reported that US refinery utilisation had fallen from 98.0 per cent in late August to 92.8 per cent in the week to 23 October, and argued that this production squeeze explained the diesel price better than distillate stocks did. It also said, in the story and in the footer, that the comparison against previous autumns had not been done and that autumn maintenance turnarounds are routine. The comparison has now been done, from the same EIA series, and it says the opposite.

Here is the final reading in the same late-October week, by year: 2022, 90.6 per cent. 2023, 85.4. 2024, 89.1. 2025, 86.0. 2026, 92.8.

The drop from the summer peak is also the smallest of the five. 2022 fell 4.7 points, 2023 fell 8.3, 2024 fell 6.6, 2025 fell 9.2 — and 2026 fell 5.5, from a far higher starting point of 98.0 per cent. American refineries are not constrained this autumn. They are running at a four-year high for the date, after a shallower-than-usual maintenance season.

So the mechanism offered yesterday is withdrawn, twenty-four hours after it was offered. That is the second withdrawn explanation for this price in nine days — the inventory story went on 22 October, the refining story goes today. What remains is the observation and no mechanism: distillate stocks oscillating in a four-million-barrel band, refineries running hard, and the diesel-minus-petrol spread setting a sixth consecutive record at $2.1158 this morning, with diesel at $6.3204 and regular at $4.2046, both down again.

What has not been examined, and is where the answer probably is: distillate demand, which this brief has never pulled; distillate exports, which can tighten a domestic market while refineries run flat out; and the crack spread, which would show whether refiners are being paid more for diesel than the crude price explains. Naming three unexamined candidates is not an explanation either, but it is an honest statement of where the brief is.

What to watch: those three series, which are owed; and whether the spread breaks its run of records, which would at least date the episode.

Confidence: Primary, and the correction is the strongest-evidenced thing in this issue — the full EIA weekly series back to 1990 was parsed this morning and the five-year window extracted programmatically. A newspaper report has turned the Alaska Senate race inside out

US politics Courts Continuation Single-sourced upstream

Alaska's governor has ordered an investigation after a New York Times report on the state's toss-up Senate race — the race this brief has covered for four days because the Justice Department asked a court to withhold certification if the Republican loses. The Times reports efforts by Democratic campaign operatives to recruit the second Dan Sullivan on the ballot, the retired schoolteacher whose presence Republicans have long called a spoiler.

The report is based on interviews and on communications among Mary Peltola's attorneys and advisers and senior aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee. NPR says it has not independently confirmed it. Both the Peltola campaign and the DSCC repeated their denials after publication; Peltola called it a distraction; the non-incumbent Sullivan did not respond to Alaska Public Media and has previously said running was his own decision.

This changes the weight of the Justice Department's case without changing its law. On Monday this brief set out two readings of the DOJ suit and said the discriminator would be whether similar filings appeared elsewhere; they did, in California and Washington state, and a judge rejected the California one on Wednesday in strong terms. Today's report supplies something the suit never had: a factual basis for the claim that the second candidacy was engineered. It does not supply the thing the suit most needs, which is a lawful way to determine, after a secret ballot, which Sullivan a voter meant.

The reactions are predictable and the official ones are not nothing. A spokesperson for Senator Sullivan said Peltola's campaign had "spent months gaslighting Alaskans". The President called the findings "Voter Fraud at the Highest Level". Justice Department officials said on Thursday the story "expands its options" for investigating the race — which is a department telling the public that a newspaper report has enlarged its investigative appetite four days before an election.

Two facts hold their shape through all of it. The Alaska Supreme Court ruled in June that the non-incumbent Sullivan could appear on the ballot, after state officials had probed the candidacies. And election law experts have broadly called the DOJ's case weak, largely because the Constitution gives states control over elections.

What to watch: whether anything in the governor's investigation is produced before Tuesday; whether the DOJ amends its filing to cite the Times reporting; and whether the Alaska court rules before the election.

Confidence: Single-sourced upstream. Everything about the recruitment allegation traces to one New York Times report, which this brief has not read and which NPR explicitly has not independently confirmed; the denials are on the record. This brief is reporting that the allegation was made and what followed, not that it is true. Sources: NPR · 26 October · 29 October

The VA ended a mortgage programme, and 63% of veterans who needed help pay more

US politics Macro & markets New subject Independent corroboration

When an American homeowner falls behind on a mortgage, the usual remedy is a loan modification: the lender rewrites the terms so the borrower can catch up. For most of the market it works as designed. For loans guaranteed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, 2 per cent of modifications leave the borrower paying more. For VA-backed loans, 63 per cent do.

The difference is a programme the Department of Veterans Affairs shut down last year — a low-interest facility designed for exactly this situation. NPR obtained previously unreported VA data. Among veterans whose loans were modified after a financial hardship, more than 15,000 saw their monthly payment rise by at least $150. More than 700 saw it rise by $1,000 or more.

The mechanism is the point and it is not complicated. A modification reprices the loan at something near current rates. Veterans who bought in 2020 and 2021 hold mortgages at rates that no longer exist; with the low-interest programme gone, asking for help means giving up the old rate. So the instrument that exists to keep distressed borrowers in their homes now raises the payment of the distressed borrower nearly two-thirds of the time, in the one corner of the market reserved for people who served.

Scott Boyd is the case NPR follows. He enlisted at 16 with his mother's signature, sent his pay home, served in the infantry and later in Army intelligence, left in 2002, built a career in technology, and bought a house near Columbus in 2021 at the low rate everyone got then. He lost his job in a mass layoff, his combat service returned as PTSD, he checked himself into the Dayton VA hospital, and he fell behind.

Representative Mark Takano, the ranking Democrat on the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, after being shown the findings: "This is the height of insanity. These are veterans that are already financially distressed — they were already in trouble."

What to watch: whether the VA responds with numbers of its own, since the data is its own; whether the committee holds a hearing after the election; and whether the 15,000 figure grows as more modifications are processed.

Confidence: Independent corroboration of a single investigation. The figures are NPR's analysis of VA data it obtained and this brief has not seen; no VA response appears in what was read, and the 2 per cent GSE comparison is NPR's. The explanation of why a modification raises a payment is this brief's own. Sources: NPR

The 50 per cent duties on Canada were rewritten again, effective this morning

Trade & supply chains New subject Primary document Underreported

A proclamation published this morning changes which Canadian goods carry an additional ad valorem duty of 50 per cent. Annex I Part A takes products off the list; Annex I Part B puts products on it. Both took effect at 12:01 a.m. eastern time today, and the duties apply on top of any section 232 tariffs already owed.

The authority is the striking part. These duties are imposed under section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a Depression-era provision, almost never used, that lets the President respond to foreign discrimination against US commerce with duties of up to 50 per cent, and, if the discrimination continues, to exclude the country's goods altogether. The rate being applied is the statutory maximum, and the exclusion power is recited in the proclamation's own recitals.

The sequence, from the document: Proclamation 11048 of 20 July found that Canada's motor vehicle tariff scheme discriminates against US commerce and imposed the duties from 19 August. Proclamation 11056 of 18 August suspended them for three days after Canada "expressed a commitment to remove the discrimination". On 21 August, the proclamation says, "Canada reneged on its commitment, ceased negotiating in good faith"; the suspension lapsed and the duties took effect at 12:01 a.m. on 22 August. Proclamation 11065 of 8 September modified the scope. Today's is the second modification.

Four proclamations in just over three months, each adjusting which goods are caught, is a tariff being steered rather than set. For an importer that means the rate on a given product can change overnight on a Friday, which is what happened today, and the only way to know is to read an annex in the Federal Register.

What to watch: what is actually in Annexes I and II, which this brief has not read; whether section 338's exclusion power is invoked, which would be an embargo; and whether any of this is litigated, since section 338 has very little case law precisely because it has been dormant for ninety years.

Confidence: Primary document, recitals and operative paragraphs read in full; the annexes listing the affected products were not read, so this brief cannot say which goods moved in which direction, and that is the whole practical content for anyone importing. The characterisation of section 338 as near-dormant is general knowledge, not sourced from the document. Sources: FR 2026-22292 · GovInfo PDF

The general who ran Pentagon planning has been fired over a leak inquiry

Cyber & security Geopolitics New subject Single-sourced

Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth has fired Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, the Army officer who oversaw planning at the Pentagon. Officials not authorised to speak publicly say Sylvia is the subject of a leak investigation and allege he passed information about Iran and Ukraine to reporters. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell issued a statement naming no one, confirming an investigation into the release of classified information, and saying the matter has been referred to the Justice Department for possible legal action.

One detail narrows it. The probe was run by the Air Force Office of Special Investigations, which points at least partly to the air campaign over Iran — thousands of strikes since earlier this year, flown from airfields in the United States, Europe and the Middle East. What was allegedly disclosed has not been said.

Sylvia's record, per NPR's Pentagon correspondent: West Point graduate, former commander of the 101st Airborne Division, extensive combat service in Afghanistan and Iraq, a Purple Heart and six Bronze Stars including one for valour, less than a year in the planning job.

The comparison the reporting draws is the one that will be drawn everywhere. Eighteen months ago Hegseth transmitted classified details of airstrikes in Yemen — aircraft launch times, numbers of aircraft, strike times on target — over a Signal chat including his wife, his brother and others without top secret clearance. The Pentagon inspector general found that the information could have imperilled US service members had it been intercepted. There was talk of replacing the Secretary; the President stood by him. The same Secretary has now fired a general over an alleged disclosure and referred it for prosecution.

Sylvia is one of a number of senior officers Hegseth has removed. NPR's account gives the stated reasons for others as disagreement over deploying US troops on American streets, or the Secretary's belief that they held their posts because of race or gender.

What to watch: whether the Justice Department charges anyone, which would make this a prosecution rather than a personnel action; who replaces Sylvia in a planning job during an active air campaign; and whether the OSI investigation's scope is disclosed.

Confidence: Single-sourced, and the substance rests on anonymous officials. The Pentagon statement names no individual; the allegation that Sylvia leaked, and that it concerned Iran and Ukraine, comes from officials not authorised to speak. Sylvia has not been heard from and no charge has been filed. The Hegseth Signal findings are as NPR's correspondent described them; this brief did not read the inspector general's report. The gatekeepers for $100 billion a year have had their rulebook rewritten

Education Regulatory New subject Primary document Underreported

Accrediting agencies decide which colleges may take federal student aid, which the Department of Education's own rule puts at more than $100 billion a year in Pell Grants and federal student loans. A final rule published this morning rewrites the regulations under which the Secretary recognises those agencies, implementing Executive Order 14279, "Reforming Accreditation to Strengthen Higher Education". It takes effect 1 July 2027.

The largest change is geographic. The rule amends §§602.11 and 602.12 to remove geographic restrictions that stop institutions choosing an accrediting agency, and requires agencies to state the scope of their accrediting activities plainly. American accreditation has been organised regionally for a century; ending the boundaries turns accreditors into competitors for the same institutions nationally. Whether that raises standards through competition or lowers them through shopping is the entire argument, and it will take years to show.

Several provisions point the other way, toward tightening. §602.14 strengthens the criteria an agency must meet to prove it is fully separate and independent from any related trade or membership organisation — a long-standing complaint that accreditors are captured by the colleges they accredit. §602.2 requires the Department to publish notice when it denies, limits, suspends or terminates an agency's recognition. §602.15 requires conflict-of-interest controls.

And one line is doing work out of proportion to its length. §602.13 is unreserved and amended to specify that recognition by the Department "does not provide any immunity from antitrust laws." That is the federal government telling accreditors, in regulation, that they may be sued as a combination — which, in a system where agencies set standards collectively for competing institutions, is a substantial new exposure.

What to watch: whether any institution switches accreditor across the old regional lines before July 2027; whether an antitrust suit follows §602.13; and whether the "two-year rule" elimination brings new agencies forward for recognition.

Confidence: Primary document, executive summary and summary of major provisions read; the regulatory text, the analysis of public comments and the impact analysis were not. The provisions above are the Department's own descriptions of what it is doing. The historical point about regional accreditation and the reading of the antitrust clause are this brief's own. US politics Sites that look like local newspapers are boosting Democratic candidates.

Websites built to resemble local news outlets are running coverage favourable to Democratic candidates, according to reporting published this week. The format — a plausible masthead, a town name, wire copy around partisan stories — has been documented on the Republican side for most of a decade; what this reporting establishes is that it is now bipartisan practice.

The reason it works is the hole it fills. Local newspapers have closed across the country, and a site carrying a town's name in a place with no remaining newsroom faces no competitor who would recognise it as fake. The counterfeit is convincing in proportion to the absence of the real thing, which means the remedy is not detection but local journalism, and that is not arriving before Tuesday.

Below the line because this brief read a headline and a summary, not the investigation, and cannot say how many sites, in which states, or with what reach — the three numbers that would distinguish a pattern from an anecdote.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: NPR
