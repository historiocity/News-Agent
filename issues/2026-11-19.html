The crack spread broke, and the same day Washington moved against the thing that was lowering it

Energy & grid Macro & markets Continuation Primary source

For four mornings this brief has named one number as the test of whether the diesel squeeze was ending: the crack spread, $97.99 on 6 November. Yesterday's EIA release answered it. For the week to 13 November, ULSD at New York Harbor fell 35.3 cents to $4.265 a gallon and WTI fell $5.19 to $90.78, putting the simplified crack spread — ULSD times 42, minus WTI — at $88.35, down $9.64 in a week.

Rank it before calling it anything. $88.35 is the lowest since 7 August and the 15th highest of 46 weeks in 2026. The 2026 peak was $115.83 on 18 September; the 2026 low was $32.03 on 9 January. So the margin has unwound to where it stood in early August and remains about 2.8 times the January level. This is a real break and it is not a return to normal.

The rest of the report points the same way without overstating it. Distillate stocks rose 1,571 thousand barrels to 104,901, a second consecutive build off the year's low of 99,259 set on 30 October — but still only 30th of 46 weeks. Refinery utilisation rose to 95.7%, which is 15th of 46 and not a high; the 2026 maximum is 98.0%. Distillate product supplied recovered to 3,785 thousand barrels a day from 3,411. WTI at $90.78 is the lowest since 28 August. At the pump, AAA this morning: diesel $5.9298, regular $3.7212.

And on the same day, CENTCOM widened the blockade to cover the workaround. An advisory issued Wednesday says any ship that receives cargo via a ship-to-ship transfer from a vessel subject to the blockade carrying cargo loaded at an Iranian port or coastline may be considered to be evading the blockade, even if it never entered Iranian waters, and may be “stopped, boarded, diverted, captured, or directed to proceed to the Iranian port where the cargo originated”. The blockade was first imposed on 13 April, lifted in June after a draft understanding with Tehran, and reimposed on 14 July when it collapsed.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the price relief now visible in the data is durable. The margin and stock data say the squeeze is unwinding. Wednesday's CENTCOM advisory targets the precise mechanism that produced the unwind. A dispute about facts that only exists because the two things happened on the same day and nobody has connected them.

Cause, and this brief has been building the connection all week. On Monday and Tuesday the reporting here was that Gulf exports recovered to a wartime high of 11.4 million barrels a day using ship-to-ship transfers and shuttle tankers in the Gulf of Oman, which is how cargo reached buyers whose ships would not enter the strait. That recovery is what collapsed tanker rates 64%, and the crude price with them, and now the margin. CENTCOM has just declared that exact manoeuvre a blockade evasion when the cargo originates in Iran. The two are not in contradiction because they cover different oil — the recovery is largely Gulf Arab crude, the advisory is about Iranian cargo — but they share hulls, crews, insurers and transfer locations, and a master who cannot tell whose barrels are in the other ship's tanks will price that uncertainty.

What it means for you. Take the relief as real and the trend as newly conditional. The margin break is in the series of record and a driver will keep feeling it for some weeks. What would reverse it is not an attack but a boarding: the first ship stopped under the new ship-to-ship rule will reprice war-risk and transfer operations for every vessel doing the same thing legally. What would settle it: next Wednesday's crack spread, whether distillate stocks post a third build, and whether CENTCOM actually interdicts a ship-to-ship recipient rather than merely warning that it may. The IEA's monthly report, which carries the export figures underneath all of this, has refused this brief for six consecutive days.

What to watch: next week's EIA release and the crack spread; a third stock build; any interdiction under the new advisory; Gulf of Oman transfer volumes; and whether the diesel premium over petrol, $2.2086 this morning, keeps falling.

The technicians' union says the FAA man was there to unlock the door

US politics & policy Continuation Single source

On Tuesday the FAA's administrator told reporters on Capitol Hill that the L3Harris engineer who replaced a router in south Arkansas “was monitored by an FAA technician” and that a risk assessment was done beforehand. On Wednesday, two sources familiar with what happened in the room, and the union representing FAA technicians, said that is not what happened.

Their account: an FAA technician escorted the contractor into the remote radio communications site to replace a decades-old router, believed his role ended once the contractor was inside, and went about his own work without knowing exactly what the engineer was doing. One source put it that the technician “was only there to unlock the door”. Another said: “There was no monitoring.” Sources describe the hands-off arrangement as a recent development and a stark change from how this work used to be handled.

The mechanism of the failure is now also on the record. Not long after the replacement, the new router flooded the FAA's aging network with data, which cascaded into the communications failure that produced the ground stop. About 3,500 aircraft were airborne when departures were halted for more than four hours.

This is the third consecutive day the FAA story has produced a fact the department's account did not want: Monday the majority leader's $8 billion of unspent money, Tuesday the Juniper installation pause, and now the union contradicting the administrator.

Divergence

The point in dispute. A single factual claim made to Congress: whether the contractor was monitored. The administrator says yes, and adds that a risk assessment was done. Two witnesses and the technicians' union say the escort unlocked the door and left. A dispute about facts, narrow, dated, and resolvable from records that exist.

Cause. The mundane reading is that both are describing the same arrangement under different definitions of a word. If the agency's procedure defines “monitored” as an FAA employee being present at the site and responsible for access, the administrator's statement is accurate and useless. If it means someone watching the configuration being entered, it is false. Reach for the definitional explanation first — but note who is contradicting him. A union speaking on behalf of the technician whose presence is being invoked has an obvious institutional interest in not letting its member carry the blame, and it also has the best knowledge of what the procedure actually requires. The vantage difference is total: the administrator is describing a process document and the union is describing a Friday morning.

What it means for you. The question that matters is not who is lying but when the supervision changed, because the sources say it changed recently. If a contractor can now make a configuration change on a national communications network without an agency employee watching, that is a procurement and oversight decision somebody made and documented, and it applies to every other site in the programme that is now paused. Believe that an FAA employee was present, that no one has yet produced evidence he observed the work, and that the router flooded the network. What would settle it: the site access and work records for that morning, the risk assessment the administrator says exists, and the contract clause defining contractor supervision — three documents, all of which exist, none of which has been published.

What to watch: whether the risk assessment is released; whether Bedford is asked this under oath in January; the preliminary incident report; whether the Juniper pause is lifted; and what the technicians' union files.

Confidence: Single-sourced — one newsroom, two unnamed sources and the technicians' union, against the administrator's on-the-record statement. This brief has read no FAA document and no contract. Sources: CNN · 18 November

The NSA outage reached the facility doing signals intelligence on the Iran war

US politics & policy AI & tech industry Continuation Single source

The IT disruption that has kept much of the National Security Agency's workforce at home for two days also affected operations at a Georgia facility supporting signals intelligence and cybersecurity work focused on the Middle East — where the United States is at war with Iran — according to four sources. By Wednesday many employees still could not return; an operational status update seen by CNN told staff the restriction was to secure continued restoration.

Officials made two claims in mitigation, and the second is the interesting one. The most sensitive collection systems are still functioning, and the agency has multiple back-ups and workarounds for the frozen internal classified network. And many of the agency's computer networks are so old, and so heavily compartmented — deliberately walled off from other systems to protect classified material — that they were untouched. One source noted that wryly.

The administration's explanation moved again. On Tuesday the director of national intelligence attributed the outage to an emergency patch for PHP vulnerabilities found last month. On Wednesday administration officials described the episode as a consequence of the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence — the AI-assisted exploitation that made those vulnerabilities urgent in the first place. Neither account is the agency's own, which remains “temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance”.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this outage had operational consequences. The agency and several officials say the important systems ran and the rest was covered by workarounds. Four sources say a facility supporting signals intelligence on an active war was affected. A dispute about significance rather than facts: both can be true, and whether that is reassuring depends entirely on what you think internal communications are for.

Cause, and the wry observation is the key to it. The systems that survived are the oldest and most isolated ones. That is not luck; it is the security architecture working exactly as designed, because compartmentation that frustrates an attacker also frustrates a patch. The corollary is uncomfortable and nobody in the official accounts has stated it: the agency's modernised, interconnected systems are the ones that went down, and its obsolete ones are the ones that stayed up. Set that beside this issue's second lead, where an agency is asking Congress for $50 billion to replace decades-old equipment with modern interconnected equipment, and the week has produced an argument that neither institution wants to have. The shifting explanation — routine maintenance, then an emergency patch, then AI — is better read as three true descriptions of one causal chain than as a cover story, because each is narrower than the last.

What it means for you. Hold two things at once: modernisation reduces the number of failures and increases the blast radius of the ones that remain. That is the lesson of this week, and it is the opposite of the lesson being drawn aloud in Washington. Believe that NSANet is still not fully restored on the third day, that a Middle East intelligence facility was affected, and that no evidence of an attack has emerged. What would settle the operational question: how long restoration takes; whether the intelligence committees say anything; and whether any collection gap is later acknowledged. cisa.gov, which would carry an advisory on patch-induced failures, returned 403 to this brief yesterday.

What to watch: restoration and the campus reopening; any acknowledged collection gap; whether CISA publishes on the patch rather than the vulnerability; the intelligence committees in January; and whether other agencies report the same failure.

Ten months after the raid, Hegseth calls Venezuela a compliant security partner

Geopolitics Continuation Single source

The United States has signed an agreement to re-establish military ties with Venezuela, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced outside the US Embassy in Caracas on Wednesday, on his first trip to the country — more than ten months after the American military raid that removed its leader. “Venezuela has gone from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner,” he said.

What the deal is said to contain. Hegseth called it historic and said it will let American and Venezuelan forces train together and share intelligence, defence equipment and other capabilities. He said it would not be cosmetic and would require Venezuelan defence and security reforms to modernise its military and eliminate persistent threats to both countries. He appeared to sign it earlier at the presidential palace with Defence Minister Gustavo Gonzalez Lopez, on footage broadcast there.

What is not known is most of it. The timeline and scope of the agreement remain unclear. The Defense Department has not answered questions about the agreement or the trip, and did not publish a schedule for the secretary on the day of the visit. No text has been released.

For context on how far this has moved. In January an American military operation removed Venezuela's leader; this brief has carried the aftermath as a thread since. Ten months later the defence secretary is standing outside the US embassy in Caracas describing the same country as a partner whose forces will train with American ones and receive American equipment and intelligence. No intervening election, negotiation or treaty has been reported here that would explain the distance travelled — which is precisely why the absence of a published text matters more than it normally would.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What “compliant” is doing in that sentence. One reading: a successful outcome — a hostile government was replaced, the replacement cooperates, and a formal partnership regularises it. The other: a description of a client, in which a government installed after a foreign military operation signs a training and intelligence agreement with the power that installed it, and compliance is the product rather than the evidence. A dispute about significance, and the same facts support both.

Cause. Interest explains the framing on one side and vantage on the other. A defence secretary announcing an agreement at an embassy describes the relationship in the register of alliance. Critics measure it against the January operation and read a chain of causation rather than a partnership. The mundane point that decides which reading travels further is procedural: an agreement of this kind normally has a published text, a Senate notification if it commits equipment, and a schedule. None exists here, and the department declined to answer. Reach for ordinary disorganisation before conspiracy — a first visit to a country the US invaded ten months ago is a security problem as much as a diplomatic one — but the absence of a text is what prevents anyone from saying which reading is right.

What it means for you. The thing to ask is whether this is an executive agreement or a treaty commitment, because only one of them survives the next administration and only one needs the Senate. Intelligence sharing and equipment transfers also carry statutory notification requirements that produce paper. Believe that something was signed, that it concerns training and intelligence, and that nobody outside the Pentagon knows its terms. What would settle it: the text; any congressional notification of equipment transfer; whether the incoming Senate majority asks for it in January, which this issue's sixth lead says is already being prepared for; and what Venezuelan reforms are actually specified.

What to watch: publication of the text; any arms-transfer notification; whether the agreement is called executive or treaty; the January oversight letters; and whether joint training is scheduled.

Confidence: Single source — CNN, from the announcement, with the secretary quoted on the record and the Defense Department declining to answer questions. No text has been published and this brief has seen no document. Sources: CNN

Anthropic priced at $83 and raised $81.3 billion, the largest flotation ever

AI & tech industry Macro & markets New subject Disclosure Single source

Disclosure

This brief is researched and written using a model made by Anthropic, the company in this story. This file's standing rule is that coverage touching the reader's own interests gets the same scepticism as anything else, and that applies with more force where the brief's own producer is the subject. Everything below is from one newsroom's report of a public pricing, and this brief has not read the prospectus.

Anthropic priced its initial public offering on Wednesday at $83 a share, above its marketed range, selling 980 million shares to raise $81.3 billion. That passes SpaceX's $75 billion to become the largest flotation on record. The company had first targeted $70 to $78 and had planned to sell 825 million shares; both the price and the size went up, which is the conventional signal of demand exceeding supply at the original terms. Anthropic is selling about 956 million shares and its seven co-founders about 24 million between them. Trading begins on Nasdaq today under the ticker ANT.

The timing is the part a reader should hold onto. It prices into the same fortnight in which Nvidia lost roughly $2 trillion of market value, its founder resigned and its board refused him a return, and the Commerce Department made remote access to advanced chips a licensing question. A record sum is being raised for AI software in the weeks when the market repriced AI hardware by a third.

Two numbers put the size in proportion. The previous record holder raised $75 billion; this raise is $6.3 billion larger. And the sellers are almost entirely the company rather than its owners: of 980 million shares, about 956 million are newly issued and about 24 million come from the seven co-founders, which is roughly 2.4% of the offering. A flotation where the founders sell that little is structured as a financing, not as an exit — whatever else it is, the money is going into the business.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What a record raise at an above-range price means. One reading: validation — investors who have just marked down the chipmakers are distinguishing between the hardware cycle and the applications built on it, and paying up for the second. The other: the top — the largest flotation in history, priced above range, in the same month a bellwether fell 35%, is the sort of event that marks a peak rather than a beginning. A dispute about significance, unresolvable today by construction.

Cause. Both readings are reasonable and the evidence that would separate them does not exist yet, which is worth saying plainly rather than picking a side. What can be said is mechanical. An IPO price is set by a bank's book of institutional demand, not by a market; raising the price and the share count means the book was oversubscribed at the lower number, and nothing more. The first genuinely informative price is the close on the first day, and the first useful one is the close after the lock-up expires. Note also what a $81.3 billion raise implies about capital intensity: a company does not raise that sum unless it expects to spend it, and the thing it would spend it on is compute — which is Nvidia's revenue, now sold under a new export regime.

What it means for you. For almost every reader the practical exposure is indirect and already present: an index fund will hold this within weeks, and the AI trade has driven the US market for four years. Treat today's price as information about a bank's order book and nothing else, and wait for a quarter of public reporting before treating any of it as information about the business. What would settle the two readings: the first-day close against the $83 price; the lock-up expiry; the first reported quarter; and whether Nvidia's next guidance shows the capital this raise represents actually arriving as orders.

What to watch: today's open and close against $83; the lock-up expiry; the first quarterly report; whether other AI companies accelerate their own offerings; and Nvidia's next guidance.

Dozens of officials have been told to preserve their documents

US politics & policy New subject Single source

Democratic lawmakers sent letters on Wednesday to dozens of administration officials and allies instructing them to preserve documents, ahead of taking committee control and subpoena power in January. Rep. Robert Garcia, poised to chair the House Oversight Committee, sent a dozen letters on Wednesday alone, to recipients including Vice President JD Vance.

The stated scope is wide, running from the business dealings of the president's sons to the Iran war. The lawmakers' account is that they were broadly stonewalled over the past two years and are casting a wide net to force compliance; the White House has taken a hostile posture toward the coming inquiries.

A preservation letter is not a subpoena and that is the point of sending it now. It creates a dated record that the recipient was on notice, which is what converts a later deletion from an administrative lapse into an obstruction question. Sending them in November rather than January is the cheapest available way to freeze evidence during the two months when the senders have no power to compel anything.

Four of the eight leads in this issue describe conduct these committees have already named as targets: the Iran war, the FAA ground stop, chip export decisions, and the Venezuela agreement whose text has not been published.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is oversight or opposition research. The lawmakers' framing is that two years of stonewalling require a wide net. The administration's framing is that a dozen letters in a day to targets ranging from the vice president to the president's family is a political operation wearing a committee's letterhead. A dispute about significance; nobody disputes the letters were sent.

Cause. The honest answer is that the instrument does not distinguish, and never has. A preservation notice costs nothing to send and imposes a real duty on the recipient, so the incentive is always to send many and narrow later — which is exactly what the other party did when it held the gavels, and what the party now receiving them will do again. The vantage difference is simply who is holding the committee. Note the one genuinely new element: the senders are telling you their targets two months early, which is unusual and is itself a choice. A list published in advance is a negotiating position as much as an investigative plan.

What it means for you. The signal worth reading is the list, not the letters. It tells a reader which decisions of the past two years will be documented in public during 2027, and that is a better guide to what will become knowable than any statement from either side. Believe that dozens of officials are now on notice and that nothing can be compelled until January. What would settle whether this is serious: whether any recipient certifies compliance; whether the first subpoenas in January match these letters or go wider; and whether the administration asserts privilege pre-emptively, which would be the clearest sign it expects the documents to matter.

What to watch: the full list of recipients; any pre-emptive privilege assertions; whether the first January subpoenas track these letters; the Senate's parallel effort with 56 seats; and whether any agency announces a records change.

A Russian came through Mexico to stalk the employees of a company arming Ukraine with software

Geopolitics AI & tech industry New subject Single source

A Russian man, Igor D'iu, was arrested in the United States and charged in October with stalking and harassing employees of Palantir because of the company's support for Ukraine, according to two sources familiar with the case. He faces charges including acting as an agent of a foreign government, interstate stalking and fraud. Court documents say he believed he was gathering information “for the Motherland”.

The tradecraft described by the Justice Department is modest and that is the notable part. D'iu is said to have entered the United States through Mexico over the summer and to have worked with co-conspirators he communicated with only through an encrypted messaging app, who supplied instructions, equipment and money. The court documents do not name the targeted company; two sources identified it as Palantir, which has supplied Ukraine with software to identify Russian targets, guide operations and investigate war crimes.

The target set is the story. This is not an attempt on a defence contractor's networks or its classified work. It is an operation against the people who work there, conducted in the United States, by someone recruited and run remotely for money.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What kind of operation this was. Read as espionage, it is a Russian intelligence service collecting on a company arming an adversary — serious, professional, and consistent with decades of practice. Read as harassment, it is an intimidation campaign against individuals, whose purpose is to make working at Palantir unpleasant rather than to learn anything. A dispute about significance, and the charges straddle it: acting as a foreign agent is the first, interstate stalking is the second.

Cause. The mundane explanation is that it was both, because the modern version of this is cheap. A handler who can recruit through an encrypted app, fund a man who walks in through Mexico, and task him against named employees does not need a professional officer, a cover job or a station — which is why the tradecraft looks thin and why the operation was detectable. The two readings also reflect two vantage points: prosecutors charge what they can prove, and stalking is far easier to prove than espionage, so the charge sheet understates the alleged purpose by design. Reach for that before concluding the operation was unserious.

What it means for you. The generalisable fact is that employees of American companies supporting Ukraine are now a targeting category inside the United States, approached through a method that costs almost nothing to repeat. That reaches well beyond Palantir: software, logistics and satellite firms with Ukraine contracts employ tens of thousands of people who are not security-cleared and have no protective detail. Believe the arrest and the charges; treat the attribution to a Russian service as alleged, since the co-conspirators are unnamed in the documents. What would settle it: the indictment's description of the co-conspirators; whether any foreign-agent count survives to trial; and whether other companies report similar approaches.

What to watch: the indictment and whether the foreign-agent count holds; whether other Ukraine-linked firms report similar approaches; any FBI advisory to industry; and whether the co-conspirators are identified.

A Houston ship pilot is the first ever charged with seaman's manslaughter

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Single source

Darris Ornel Jefferson, 39, a Houston ship pilot, has been indicted on a federal seaman's manslaughter charge over a 2024 collision in the Houston Ship Channel — what federal officials describe as the first prosecution of its kind involving a ship pilot. He is accused of misconduct, negligence and inattention to duty while piloting the bulk carrier Yangze 7 on 19 July 2024, when it collided with the towing vessel Miss Peggy near Lynchburg, Texas.

The Miss Peggy capsized and sank with five crew aboard. Four were rescued. A fifth, 25-year-old deckhand Aquarius Lowman, was later found dead in the wreckage. One survivor was seriously injured and another slightly. The indictment follows a National Transportation Safety Board investigation.

Seaman's manslaughter is an old and unusual statute. It reaches masters, officers, pilots and engineers of vessels, and it is built around misconduct, negligence or inattention to duty rather than the recklessness ordinary manslaughter requires — a lower threshold applied to a narrow class of people. Applying it to a compulsory state-licensed pilot for the first time changes the risk calculus of a job that every large vessel entering a US port is legally required to employ.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether criminal liability makes shipping safer. The prosecution's premise is deterrence — a man died, the conduct was below standard, and consequences change behaviour. The maritime profession's standing objection is that it does the opposite, because safety investigation depends on mariners talking freely to the NTSB, and a pilot who expects indictment will say nothing. A dispute about facts with decades of evidence on both sides and no agreement on it.

Cause, and the sequence here is what sharpens it. The indictment follows the NTSB investigation. Board findings are statutorily inadmissible as evidence in most proceedings, but an investigation still produces the roadmap — the voyage data, the bridge audio, the named witnesses. Mariners read a prosecution that follows a safety investigation as the investigation feeding the prosecution, whatever the rules of evidence say, and they adjust what they say next time. Reach for that before dismissing the objection as professional self-protection. On the other side, the vantage that matters is a family's: four crew were rescued and a twenty-five-year-old was not, and no safety recommendation is a remedy for that.

What it means for you. This is a precedent in a system most Americans never see and all of them depend on. Compulsory pilotage is how every tanker, container ship and LNG carrier enters a US port; there are only a few thousand pilots, their work is irreducibly judgement under time pressure, and the supply is already tight. If the first such prosecution succeeds, expect it to be charged again, and expect its effects to show up first in what mariners tell investigators and second in recruitment. What would settle it: the trial's outcome; whether the NTSB's report is used or kept out; whether pilots' associations seek a statutory change; and whether any similar charge follows the New Orleans capsizing now under investigation.

What to watch: arraignment and plea; whether the NTSB record is admitted; pilots' association responses; whether the Justice Department charges again; and whether the New Orleans case takes the same path.

Hormuz · The LPG tanker Tara Gas is still burning off Oman, eleven of its crew are injured and one is still missing, three days after it was hit.

The Panama-flagged LPG carrier Tara Gas continues to burn off Oman after the attack in the Strait of Hormuz reported on Tuesday. ADNOC L&S, the Abu Dhabi state shipping subsidiary that operates it, has said one crew member remains unaccounted for and eleven were injured; Omani authorities rescued 24 and have continued search and rescue alongside firefighting.

The ship is a gas carrier, not a crude tanker, which is why the fire has lasted. Liquefied petroleum gas burns until it is exhausted or the containment is cooled, and a vessel abandoned by its crew cannot be cooled from aboard, so the practical options are a salvage tow or waiting.

Below the line because the facts have not changed since Tuesday except in duration, and a burning ship is not a new development each morning. It runs because the missing crew member is a person this brief named and has not accounted for, and because the fire's persistence is the most visible evidence available that the attacks continue while the freight market prices them as over.

Energy · At least four US LNG cargoes have turned toward Europe in days, away from Egypt and Asia, easing a market that hit a four-year high last month.

At least two tankers carrying American LNG have rerouted away from Egypt in the Mediterranean, and two more laden with US fuel turned sharply north toward Europe away from Asia since last week, on ship-tracking data compiled by Bloomberg and the analytics firm Kpler. The diversions follow weaker demand from major Asian buyers, where LNG has been fetching a discount to Europe, while global supply stays tight because of continued Middle East disruption. European benchmark gas futures hit an almost four-year high last month, and the flows have curbed prices there.

For an American reader this is the gas-side version of the crude story in the first lead: cargo goes where the spread pays, and a European premium pulls American molecules across the Atlantic regardless of anyone's energy policy. It also tightens Asia at the margin, which is where the next price signal will come from.

Science · Voyager 1 is now a full light-day from Earth — a signal sent today arrives tomorrow, from a spacecraft launched in 1977.

The most distant human-made object has passed one light-day from Earth, meaning radio signals now take a full twenty-four hours to travel each way. Voyager 1 launched in 1977, crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and still returns data from instruments running on a plutonium source that loses a few watts a year.

The engineering fact worth keeping is the communications one. Every instruction sent to Voyager 1 is a two-day round trip, which means the craft must handle anything that goes wrong for at least a day on its own, using a computer with less memory than a modern key fob and software written before the internet existed.

Below the line because a milestone is an anniversary with arithmetic, and this file's anti-topics list is explicit about anniversary pieces. It runs anyway, on one ground: this issue contains three stories about complex systems that failed under maintenance, and the oldest, least modernised, most isolated system any American institution operates has just reported in from a light-day away.

Courts · A federal judge has struck down parts of Florida's 2023 financial disclosure law for local officials, after 175 municipal officers sued over having to publish their precise net worth.

US District Judge Melissa Damian ruled earlier this month that parts of Florida's 2023 Ethics Requirements for Public Officials act, SB 774, are unconstitutional. The provisions struck required local government officers and candidates to disclose their precise net worth and any source of income greater than $1,000. Some 175 municipal officers sued the state in 2024, arguing the law compelled disclosure that intruded on their privacy; the judge agreed.

The practical history is the part a resident should know. When SB 774 took effect, municipalities across Florida saw resignations from unpaid and low-paid local boards and councils by people unwilling to publish a household balance sheet to hold a seat worth little or nothing. The state's answer was that anyone exercising public power should be transparent about their finances.

Below the line because this brief has not read the opinion and cannot say which provisions survive, which is the question that determines what a Pasco or Pinellas officeholder must file next year. It runs because it is the first judicial limit on a law that reshaped who is willing to serve in Florida's smallest offices.

Confidence: Single source — WUSF. The opinion has not been read and the state's appeal options are unknown. Sources: WUSF

Education · Sarasota's new school board adopted a seven-page AI policy three to one, over objections from high school students who called it vague and self-contradictory.

Sarasota County schools adopted an artificial intelligence policy on Tuesday, 3–1, at the first meeting of a newly elected board. The seven-page policy says the district will integrate AI into learning while emphasising critical thinking and creativity, and board members said it brings the district in line with state rules and remains a work in progress. High school students spoke against it, pointing to flaws and contradictions and saying it was far too vague. Tom Edwards was named chair and Liz Barker vice chair; Edwards was absent from the vote. Three new members were elected this year, ousting incumbents.

Seven pages is short for a policy governing how a technology is used across a district's classrooms, and “a work in progress” is an accurate description of what was adopted.
