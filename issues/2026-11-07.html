The US struck Qeshm island while saying the talks are going well

Geopolitics Continuation Single source

President Trump confirmed on Friday that US forces carried out what he called "minor strikes" against Iranian air defence systems on Qeshm island, in the Strait of Hormuz. "They knocked out — they built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device, and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five," he told reporters in West Palm Beach. In the same remarks he said Washington had made "a lot of progress" in its negotiations with Tehran.

Iranian state media gave a different account of the same hours. IRIB reported two missile strikes in the Suza area of Qeshm, and carried an Iranian army statement that air defence forces intercepted and destroyed an American MQ-9 drone over the island. Qeshm sits inside the Strait of Hormuz, through which a fifth of the world's seaborne oil moves.

Divergence. A dispute about facts, and an unusually clean one: two accounts of one night, neither verifiable from here. The US account: an anti-aircraft emplacement was built and destroyed in five to fifteen minutes. No American losses are mentioned. The Iranian account: two missile strikes at Suza, and an MQ-9 shot down. These are not quite contradictory — a drone can be lost in a raid the attacker still calls minor — but they cannot both be complete.

Cause of the divergence. It is not confusion; it is that each side is reporting the number that serves it, and the two numbers are about different things. "Fifteen minutes, maybe five" is a claim about how little Iran's air defence is worth. "We destroyed an MQ-9" is a claim about how much it is worth. Both are assertions about Iranian capability dressed as reports of an engagement, and the engagement itself — one emplacement, one drone — is small enough that neither claim would change anything if true. The detail that does matter is unremarked by both: this happened while the President says talks are progressing, and on an island in the Strait.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the strike as confirmed and both damage claims as unverified. The MQ-9 is the cheap discriminator: an airframe either exists or it does not, and losses of them are eventually acknowledged. More useful than either claim is what follows at sea. This brief has carried Hormuz since the war began and has had no working transit instrument for four weeks — UKMTO returns 403 and the Kpler series has not updated since 11 October. If strikes inside the Strait are now routine enough to be called minor, the thing to measure is tanker transits, and this brief currently cannot measure them. That is the gap to close before the next one.

What to watch: whether CENTCOM acknowledges an MQ-9 loss; whether the "progress" in talks produces anything dated; Iran's Hormuz restrictions and the reported exemptions for India, China, Qatar and Iraq; and any insurer or owner response, which is where a strait closes before it closes.

Confidence: Single source — an Al Jazeera file with AP, which carries both the Trump quotation and the IRIB report. This brief did not reach CENTCOM, IRIB directly, or any shipping or insurance source, and has no independent confirmation of either damage claim. Sources: Al Jazeera

Medicare cuts physician pay again, and more for doctors outside the new models

Health & pharma Continuation Primary source

Yesterday this brief named the CY2027 Medicare payment rules as the largest thing it had seen and not opened. The Physician Fee Schedule final rule (CMS-1848-F, RIN 0938-AV82) has now been read. It publishes Monday and amends fourteen parts of 42 CFR.

The number that sets physician pay is the conversion factor, and from 2026 there are two of them. For 2027 the qualifying APM conversion factor falls /bin/bash.62, or 1.83 per cent, from 3.5675. The nonqualifying APM conversion factor falls /bin/bash.77, or 2.31 per cent, from 3.4009. Both are described by CMS as projected decreases. A qualifying APM participant is a clinician with enough of their practice inside an advanced alternative payment model; everyone else is on the lower factor and the larger cut.

The two-factor structure is statutory — section 1848(d)(1)(A) of the Act, as amended — and 2026 was its first year. 2027 is therefore the first year in which the gap compounds.

Divergence. A dispute about what the split is for, and the two readings give opposite advice to a practice deciding whether to join a model. Read one: an incentive. Congress built a smaller cut for clinicians inside advanced alternative payment models precisely so that the differential would pull practices into them; a 48-basis-point gap in a single year is the intended nudge. Read two: a sorting mechanism with the causation running backwards. Joining an advanced APM requires scale, capital and administrative capacity. The practices that can qualify are the large ones, and the smaller cut therefore lands on the practices least in need of relief, while the 2.31 per cent falls on solo and small-group physicians who cannot qualify however much they want to.

Cause of the divergence. Both readings accept the same arithmetic and differ on what determines which side of it a practice lands. That is an empirical question with a published answer: the share of clinicians who are qualifying APM participants, and how that share varies by practice size. CMS tracks it. If QP status is broadly attainable, the incentive reading holds; if it tracks practice size closely, the rule is a transfer from small practices to large ones that happens to be described as an incentive. This brief read the conversion factor discussion and did not read the QP participation figures, which are in the same document.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat both cuts as settled fact and the distributional question as open and answerable this week. The rule contains specialty-specific impact tables; those plus the QP participation rate by practice size would settle it outright. That is the next thing owed on this story, and it is owed from a document already downloaded — which is the same failure mode as yesterday's, caught one step earlier.

What to watch: the specialty impact tables; the QP participation rate by practice size; the efficiency adjustment to work RVUs carried over from the CY2026 rule; and whether Congress legislates a patch before January, as it has in most recent years.

Confidence: Primary source — read from the filed PDF on the public inspection desk, 6.4MB, before publication. This brief read the conversion factor provisions and the summary; it did not read the impact tables, the comment responses, the telehealth provisions or the efficiency adjustment discussion. The characterisation of who can qualify as an APM participant is this brief's and is the part the divergence box says is unverified. Sources: CMS-1848-F · 6 November

5,182 measles cases, the most since 1991, and 59 per cent are children

Science & public health Continuation Single source

The CDC has released its count: 5,182 confirmed measles cases in the United States so far in 2026, the highest number it has reported since 1991. Children under five are 18 per cent of cases and those aged five to nineteen are 41 per cent — 59 per cent in all. The overwhelming majority, 4,993 of 5,182, are outbreak-associated rather than isolated.

Yesterday this brief ran the death of a Wisconsin infant below the line and said in print that it had no national denominator because the CDC's data page returned 403. Here it is, and it changes the size of what was reported. Wisconsin's 431 cases across 13 counties are about 8 per cent of the national total, and its death was the third reported in the United States in that week alone.

Two things in the CDC numbers are worth separating. That 96 per cent of cases are outbreak-associated means this is not importation producing scattered cases; it is sustained domestic transmission in communities, which is the condition under which elimination status is lost. And a disease whose cases are 59 per cent children is one spreading where vaccination coverage has fallen, not one circulating randomly.

Divergence. Not between sources — the CDC figure is the figure — but between two ways of reading "highest since 1991", and the choice changes what the number means. Read one: a bad year in a long decline. 1991 was the tail of a genuine epidemic; measles was declared eliminated in the US in 2000 and annual counts since have been in the hundreds, with occasional spikes driven by importation into undervaccinated communities. On this reading 5,182 is a spike, and spikes end. Read two: the elimination is going or gone. Elimination means no continuous transmission for twelve months; 96 per cent outbreak-associated cases is the signature of continuous transmission, not of spikes.

Cause of the divergence. The readings differ on one measurable thing: whether any single chain of transmission has run unbroken for twelve months. That is not a matter of interpretation and it is not inferable from a case count, however large. It depends on genotype linkage between outbreaks, which the CDC does determine and which this brief has not got.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Use 5,182 and "most since 1991" as established, and do not repeat either framing about elimination until the chain data is in hand. The settling evidence is a CDC or PAHO statement on whether continuous transmission has been sustained for twelve months, and the date that clock started. Those are specific published things and this brief could not reach cdc.gov today — the figures here come from a wire report of the CDC release, not from the CDC.

What to watch: whether the CDC or PAHO addresses elimination status; the genotype linkage between the Wisconsin, Texas and other outbreaks; weekly case counts from here; and the national death toll, which this brief still does not have.

Confidence: Single source — Al Jazeera's report of the CDC release. cdc.gov returned 403 yesterday and was not retried in time today, so every figure here is second-hand from a primary release this brief has not opened. That is the weakness of this story and it is a reachability problem, not a reporting one. Sources: Al Jazeera · 6 November

A third-place Senate candidate was subpoenaed days before an election still being counted

Courts Continuation Single source

Federal prosecutors ordered Gerald Heikes, a longshot Senate candidate in Alaska, to appear before a grand jury with records relating to political groups and other candidates in the race. The subpoena is dated days before the midterms, one day after a New York Times story alleging that operatives aligned with the Democratic candidate Mary Peltola had tried to recruit spoiler candidates — including one who shares a name with the sitting Republican senator. The Alaska Senate race is still undecided, pending the ranked-choice tabulation in mid-November, and Heikes is in third place.

Two details carry the story. The subpoena is signed by a top leader of the Justice Department's civil rights division in Washington, not by a prosecutor in Alaska. And the recipient says the premise is wrong: "They want me to appear and tell them what I know and how I was recruited, but I wasn't recruited. I just took a chance," Heikes told CNN. He says an operative whose name he does not know pitched him on running, but that he had already intended to seek office. Peltola has denied knowledge of any recruitment effort. The Justice Department declined to comment.

Divergence. A dispute about significance, between two readings that agree on every fact. Read one: ordinary, if fast, law enforcement. Recruiting spoiler candidates to split a vote can be a federal offence; a newspaper alleges it; prosecutors subpoena the person best placed to say whether he was recruited. The timing follows the story, not the election. Read two: the signature is the story. An election-fraud investigation run out of the civil rights division in Washington, rather than by the US Attorney in the district, is the configuration this brief has been watching since the summer under the heading of election certification and the Justice Department.

Cause of the divergence. The two readings weight the same two facts differently: the one-day gap after the Times story, and the Washington signature. Speed alone is weak evidence — prosecutors do move on news. The signature is stronger, because it is a choice about who controls the case, made before any charge exists. What neither reading can settle from here is whether that choice is unusual, and this brief does not know how often the civil rights division signs grand jury subpoenas in election matters. Without a base rate it is an anomaly only in the sense that it is unfamiliar.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the subpoena, its date and its signature as established; treat the inference about centralised control as unproven. The settling evidence is the base rate — how many election-related federal grand jury subpoenas in the last several cycles were signed out of Washington rather than the district — and the near-term tell is whether any charge, if one comes, is brought in Alaska or in DC. Read alongside yesterday's resignation of three Justice Department officials, including the official behind the "grand conspiracy" probe, both facts point at the same question and neither answers it.

What to watch: whether Heikes appears; whether the grand jury sits in Alaska or Washington; the ranked-choice tabulation in mid-November and whether the investigation touches the count; and whether the civil rights division's election work survives the change of Congress in January.

Confidence: Single source — CNN, which says it reviewed the subpoena. This brief did not see the subpoena, the Times story it follows, or any filing, and has no base rate for the signature point its own divergence box says is decisive. Sources: CNN

Stephen Miller takes the chief of staff job, which answers the question about course correction

US politics New Independent corroboration

Susie Wiles is leaving as White House chief of staff and Stephen Miller is replacing her, four days after the midterms cost Republicans both chambers. It is the first senior personnel change since the election and the President indicated on Friday that it will not be the last: asked by CBS whether he intended to fire people, he said "I do." He added, "I love my Cabinet. I think they're good. But I'll be doing maybe a few changes. I don't want to mention who." Wiles moves to his political operation; Trump said she "has done an outstanding job" and, of Miller, "Stephen's been great, and sometimes in life, you gotta make moves."

Miller, until now deputy chief of staff and homeland security adviser, is the architect of the administration's immigration programme. "Stephen's been with me since the beginning. He gets it," Trump told reporters. Elevating him is a decision about direction, not only about staffing, and it is being made in the week a new Congress with subpoena power was elected — and, as NPR notes, in a week when polling attributed part of the Democratic vote to the aggressiveness of immigration enforcement.

Divergence. A dispute about what a personnel change signals, and both readings are available from the same two facts. Read one: no course correction. A party that has just lost both chambers replaces its chief of staff with the most ideologically committed figure in the building; the conventional post-defeat move is to bring in an operator who can deal with the other side, and this is the opposite of that. Read two: this is about the next two years being defensive, not legislative. With both chambers gone, nothing passes anyway; what remains is executive action and withstanding oversight, and for that the president may reasonably want his most aggressive lieutenant rather than a negotiator.

Cause of the divergence. The readings diverge on what the job is about to become, and that is genuinely uncertain this week. Where they do not diverge is the prediction: both say less dealmaking and more executive action. The first calls it a mistake and the second calls it a correct read of a bad hand, but they forecast the same behaviour. That is worth stating plainly, because "Miller means no moderation" is being reported as an insight when it is the one thing nobody disputes. The real uncertainty is whether the Cabinet changes he hinted at follow the same logic.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Do not treat the appointment as evidence of anything beyond itself until the next two or three personnel moves are known. One hardliner promoted is a data point; a Cabinet reshuffle in the same direction is a strategy. The discriminating observation is cheap and close: who replaces whoever goes next, and whether any of them has legislative experience. The arch and the Oval Office landmark designation this brief covered this week belong to the same pattern — actions taken now because a hostile Congress is seated in January.

What to watch: the Cabinet changes Trump declined to name; whether Wiles's political-operation role is real or a landing place; who runs White House relations with a Democratic House; and whether the pace of executive orders rises before January.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — CNN and NPR, both on the record for the appointment, with CNN resting on "people familiar with the matter" for the reason. This brief did not see the CBS interview or the Truth Social post, and has no White House statement. A departure framed as a move to the political operation is the standard form for both a promotion and a dismissal, and nothing here distinguishes them. Sources: CNN · NPR

The AI trade bounced, and the bond market only partly agreed

Macro & markets Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief led on $2.5 trillion coming off the market on the theory that the midterms would slow the AI buildout, and named two things to watch: whether the Treasury move held or retraced, and whether the political reading survived. Both have partly resolved in one day, and in opposite directions.

AI stocks bounced on Friday. The equity market took back part of Thursday's fall. The bond market was less convinced: the two-year par yield rose 5 basis points to 4.51 per cent, recovering under half of Thursday's 12-point drop, and the ten-year rose 2 to 4.86. A full reversal of a flight to quality would have retraced the whole move. This one did not.

Meanwhile the political premise got firmer rather than weaker. Democrats are reported to be building a campaign around data-centre siting, targeting Ken Paxton among others in Texas, which makes the moratorium issue a continuing programme rather than one governor-elect's campaign line. And in Amsterdam, protesters broke into a Microsoft data-centre construction site — the same objection, outside the US, with no election attached to it.

Divergence. Yesterday's two readings were a political repricing versus a valuation correction that needed a trigger. One day of evidence cuts both ways and it is worth being precise about how. For the political reading: the Democratic campaign programme and the Amsterdam break-in both say data-centre opposition is broader and more durable than one state's result, which makes a durable repricing more reasonable, not less. For the valuation reading: a next-day bounce in the same names is what a sentiment overshoot looks like, not what a repricing of policy risk looks like — policy risk does not improve overnight when no policy has changed.

Cause of the divergence. The bond market is the useful witness because it has no view on data centres. Equities bounced; the two-year gave back less than half. Those two facts together say the equity move was partly sentiment and the flight to quality was partly real — which is the mixed answer, and the honest one. The curve above shows the wider setting: the whole long end is about 40 basis points lower than a month ago, so Thursday was an acceleration of something already running, not a new direction.

How to read it, and what would settle it. The discriminator named yesterday is unchanged and nothing this week touches it: hyperscaler capital expenditure guidance. Share prices moving in both directions in two days is not evidence about whether data centres get built. Add one observation that is now available: whether the Amsterdam protest is isolated or the start of a European pattern, because the political reading depends on the objection generalising and that is the first test of it outside an American ballot.

What to watch: hyperscaler capex guidance; whether the two-year completes the retrace; whether the Texas governor-elect restates the moratorium; further European data-centre actions; and whether the 20-year hump persists past the refunding.

Confidence: Independent corroboration for the market direction; the yield figures are this brief's own read of Treasury's series. The Amsterdam and Paxton items are headlines this brief read and did not open, which is thin support for the part of the argument they carry, and is flagged as such. Sources: CNN · Treasury · Al Jazeera · 6 November

Trade & supply chains The export-control Affiliates Rule has been stayed again, now to 10 January 2027.

BIS is extending the suspension of its Expansion of End-User Controls to Cover Affiliates of Certain Listed Entities rule, published as an interim final rule on 30 September 2025 and stayed on 10 November 2025 for a year. The suspension was due to end on 9 November 2026; it now runs to 10 January 2027.

The Affiliates Rule would extend Entity List controls to companies majority-owned by listed entities — the single largest expansion of export-control reach proposed in recent years, and the one with the most direct bearing on China trade. It has now been suspended for its entire existence, fourteen months, in two instalments.

Below the line because a stay is the absence of an event, and because this brief has not read the original interim final rule or anything explaining why the suspension keeps being extended in short increments. The two-month extension rather than another year is the detail worth noticing: it reads like a rule being held open as leverage.

Regulatory FinCEN and the Fed withdrew a six-year-old plan to cut the funds-transfer reporting threshold to $250.

FinCEN and the Federal Reserve are withdrawing the joint proposed rule published on 27 October 2020 that would have cut the Bank Secrecy Act recordkeeping threshold for cross-border funds transfers from $3,000 to $250, and the parallel threshold for transmitting information to other institutions in the payment chain. The withdrawal also drops the proposed clarification that the rules cover convertible virtual currencies and digital assets with legal tender status.

That last part is the quiet half. The 2020 proposal would have put cross-border crypto transfers above $250 squarely inside the travel rule by regulation rather than by interpretation. Withdrawing it leaves the question where it was: answerable, but not answered.

Below the line because this brief read the summary and not the withdrawal's reasoning, and a six-year-old proposal being formally abandoned is the kind of housekeeping that can mean a policy reversal or simply a docket being cleared. Which it is depends on the explanation, which has not been read.
