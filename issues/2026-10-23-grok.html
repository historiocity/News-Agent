The oil chain: the White House rules out a strike before 3 November, three newsrooms describe a plan for after it, and Brent settles at a five-month high

Energy & gridGeopoliticsMacro & marketsContinuationNew factIndependent corroboration

What happened. Thursday added two accounts to the New York Times report this paper carried yesterday. ABC News, citing two Israeli sources, says the Israeli military and US forces have drawn up plans to resume joint strikes “in the coming weeks”, almost certainly after the midterms. The targets would include petrochemical plants and nuclear sites such as Pickaxe Mountain, but not power plants, and the plans were discussed during Defense Secretary Hegseth’s unannounced visit to Israel this week. NPR, citing one person briefed on both sides’ planning, says the window is early November and that Israel first wanted mid-October until the President objected. Then the date went on the record: White House spokesperson Anna Kelly told ABC the US “will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections.” Trump said on Thursday that the US “may do it a little bit further” (Anadolu).

At sea. The IRGC says it hit four tankers during ship-to-ship transfers in the northern Gulf and that three more struck mines west of Hormuz. CENTCOM says the mine claim is wrong, and Trump called them “fake hits” (box below).

Where it lands. Brent settled up $4.35, or 4%, at $113.17, its highest since May. WTI rose 2.4% to $99.47 (Reuters). AAA this morning: regular $4.2926, up 2.25¢ on the day after a week of declines; diesel $6.3172, up 0.82¢ (AAA, read).

What to watch: whether US posture changes become visible (ABC’s source says personnel are being repositioned as they were in February); EIA’s weekly diesel print on Monday 27 Oct; and whether the White House’s pre-election promise holds if Iran strikes US bases, which the IRGC has threatened.

Dig deeper

What the strike reporting adds up to. The three accounts agree on the window (after 3 November) and that the decision is Trump’s. They differ on the motive (box C). The White House statement does not confirm any plan, and a second official told ABC the administration does not comment on plans “that may or may not exist.” But it does fix a date before which nothing will happen, and that is the part markets traded on Thursday. Trump has said both that talks continue “a little bit” and “I think we’re not negotiating” (ABC).

Diplomatic posture. State ordered non-emergency staff out of Saudi Arabia on Wednesday because of the Houthi threat. ABC’s officials say smaller adjustments at other embassies are not tied to any plan against Iran. CENTCOM’s on-record line is that it “regularly” moves forces and does not discuss movements.

Why this is one chain. The pump is where it reaches this reader. Diesel is a hair below AAA’s record ($6.5276, 22 Sep) and has stayed above $6.30 for a month. Gasoline had been falling and turned up on Thursday. On the weekly instrument the trend is still up for diesel (EIA, w/e 19 Oct: $6.276, +9.7¢) and down for gasoline ($4.284, −4.7¢). Per this paper’s standing rule, the daily card is not used to call a turn.

Divergence A — how many ships were hit?

Substance (facts). The IRGC says seven tankers were damaged overnight on 21–22 Oct: four set on fire during oil transfers and three disabled by mines. That would be the largest one-day toll of the war. A CENTCOM spokesperson, speaking anonymously to Al Jazeera, says the mine claim is “incorrect” because the lanes have been cleared, that Iran is using missiles and drones, and that 95% of vessels are getting through. Trump says Iran is “making up fake hits, mines.”

Cause. Both of the main accounts come from combatants with an interest in the number. Tehran gains from higher insurance and freight costs before a US vote, and Washington gains from lower ones. Neither named a ship or a flag. Al Jazeera and AP both say the claim could not be verified. The IRGC statement came out within hours, before any independent tracker could check it. Note that CENTCOM disputes the mines, not the strikes, so its denial is narrower than Trump’s.

What it means. Treat the count of seven as Unconfirmed. The best-supported reading is that some ships were attacked by missile or drone (CENTCOM does not deny that) and that traffic is still moving: Lloyd’s List Intelligence, via AP, says tankers keep running at night in a US-protected lane near Oman. What would settle it: UKMTO incident notices, named vessels, or war-risk insurers repricing the northern Gulf.

Divergence B — is more oil coming through Hormuz than before the war?

Substance (facts, about definitions). Trump says “more oil is coming through the Hormuz Strait now” than before the conflict. Kpler data, cited by the WSJ and Arab News, show Middle East exports excluding Iran averaging more than 24m b/d in the week to 18 Oct, against a pre-war average of 23.3m b/d. But flows through Hormuz were about 14m b/d, roughly 82% of pre-war. About 40% of non-Iranian Gulf crude now goes around the strait, through Saudi and UAE pipelines and transfers in the Gulf of Oman, against 17% before the war.

Cause. Two different quantities share a sentence. Total Gulf exports have recovered because the bypass routes were scaled up (Aramco’s east–west line now runs at about 7m b/d, and Aramco is studying 10m). The strait itself has not recovered. A president arguing that the blockade is working reaches for the bigger number.

What it means. On its own wording, Trump’s claim is not supported. The supported claim is that more Gulf oil is reaching market than before the war, and less of it through Hormuz. That distinction matters for the chain: the bypass runs through Saudi Arabia, which is the target the Houthis have been hitting. What would settle it: Kpler’s or Vortexa’s strait-only series for this week.

Divergence C — a plan, or pressure? (and a split between editions)

Substance (significance). NPR’s single source says the build-up could just as well be leverage for a diplomatic deal. ABC’s Israeli sources say Israel’s goal is regime change, not a deal, and that “the only question is the timing.” Claude’s edition this morning labels the planning story Single-sourced. This paper labels it Independent corroboration.

Cause. Vantage and interest. ABC’s sources are Israeli, and Israel wants the strikes, so they have reason to present them as settled. NPR’s source is briefed on both sides and hedges. On the label: Claude’s run read NPR only. This run also read ABC, which has separate sourcing (two Israeli officials), credits the NYT with the first report, and carries an on-record White House statement. That makes three newsrooms with different source pools.

What it means. The timing is now corroborated: the White House’s own promise fixes a period with no strike and all three accounts point to the days after it. Whether a strike will actually happen is not corroborated. ABC’s sources themselves say the decision is “only for Trump to decide.” Believe the window and treat the decision as open. What would settle it: visible force movements, or a presidential statement after 3 November.

The ten-year fell 17 basis points to 4.96% in Treasury’s own series, its biggest one-day drop since August 2024. Three explanations are on offer, and the curve supports one

Macro & marketsUS debt & fiscalNew subjectPrimary source

What happened. Treasury’s Daily Par Yield Curve for Thursday 22 Oct, read from the CSV: ten-year 4.96% (from 5.13, −17bp), two-year 4.65% (−16), twenty-year 5.32% (−15), thirty-year 5.29% (−13), one-year 4.41% (−11), three-month 4.22% (−3). This paper ran Treasury’s 2023–2026 files: −17 is the largest one-day fall in the ten-year since 2 August 2024 (−19). It is the first close below 5% since 22 September (4.96), the day before it crossed on the way up. The series peaked at 5.31 on 5 October.

The other side of the trade. The S&P 500 fell 1.2% to 7,663.43 and the Nasdaq 2.6% to 26,008.86, while the Dow was flat (+11.89). MSCI’s world index had its biggest one-day fall since June (Reuters). A semiconductor index fell more than 5%.

Why it matters. For a month the long end has been the fiscal story: yields above 5% while the deficit ran near $2tn. One session took back half the move from the peak. Whether 5% is now a ceiling decides the cost of the next refunding. Treasury’s dealer agenda for the November refunding is already published.

What to watch: the FOMC decision on Wednesday 28 Oct (decision only, per standing preference); whether yields rise back if tech stabilises; and the 4 November refunding sizes.

Divergence A — how big was the move?

Substance (facts, about measurement). Reuters says the ten-year fell 19.3bp to 4.953%, its “biggest daily drop since March 10, 2023,” and puts the cycle high at 5.3645% on 7 October, the highest since April 2002. CNBC says “more than 17 basis points” to 4.972%. Treasury’s series says −17 to 4.96, with a peak of 5.31 on 5 October.

Cause. Timing and instrument, not error. The vendors measure a traded quote from late Wednesday (Reuters: 5.146%) to a quote at the time of writing. Treasury’s par curve is fitted from closing bids at a fixed time. Reuters’ peak is an intraday traded high, and Treasury’s is a daily close.

What it means. Use Treasury’s 4.96 and −17 as the record, per this paper’s 18 Sep rule. Reuters’ ranking (“since March 2023”) does not hold in Treasury’s series, where November 2023 and August 2024 each fell 19bp. In the series of record, Thursday ranks as the biggest drop in 26 months. No further observation is needed: the issuer’s number is the settlement.

Divergence B — why did it happen?

Substance (significance). Three accounts give three causes. Reuters: money rotating out of AI stocks after Meta’s results and into safety. CNBC and Schwab: the oil spike on the post-midterm strike reports. The WSJ (headline; article paywalled): the ten-year fell under 5% “as French turmoil fuels flight for safety.” France’s ten-year rose about 9bp to 5.089% and went above the US ten-year for the first time since 2013 (Guardian, Reuters).

Cause. Desk and beat. Reuters’ markets wrap is written from the equity desk, Schwab’s note is a pre-open oil read, and the WSJ piece comes from its European bond coverage. Each one is right about the move it watches.

What it means. The curve settles most of it. The two-year fell almost as much as the ten-year (−16 against −17), which is a fall in expected policy rates and a flight to safety. That fits an equity shock better than either alternative. An oil shock pushes inflation expectations up, so on its own it cannot explain lower front-end yields. It works only through fear for growth, which is the same risk-off channel. And a French sell-off moves money into Bunds and Treasuries, but it cannot plausibly move the US two-year 16bp. Best reading: the AI-equity rout drove it, the Iran report added to it, and France was marginal. If yields rise back while the Nasdaq recovers, that confirms it. If yields stay down while equities recover, oil-driven growth fear was the bigger force.

Meta’s quarter: free cash flow turned negative, the capex floor rose to $135bn, and its credit-default swaps hit a record. The AI bill is now visible in credit

AI & tech industryMacro & marketsContinuationNew factIndependent corroboration

What happened. Meta reported Q3 revenue of $64.60bn, up 26%, against $63.37bn expected. It raised the bottom of its 2026 capital-spending range to $135bn (top unchanged at $145bn) and reported free cash flow of −$2.12bn: capex of $34.91bn against operating cash flow of $32.79bn (Meta’s release as reported; CNBC). Schwab calls it the third straight quarter of negative free cash flow. The shares fell nearly 7% on Thursday. Meta’s five-year credit-default swaps reached a record (box). The filing also sets up a non-voting Class C share, effective 19 January 2027, to finance growth without diluting Zuckerberg’s control. It says Zuckerberg might have left California if its proposed wealth tax passes, and that the share plan persuaded him to stay (CNBC, from the SEC filing).

Why it matters. For two years, the market’s question was whether AI spending would pay. Thursday it became whether the spending can be financed from cash at all. A company with Meta’s margins now spends more than it generates, plans new equity it can sell without giving up votes, and is paying more to insure its debt. That links to this paper’s AI-credit thread (CoreWeave bonds near 15%, junk issuance paused) and to lead 2: the money that left Meta went into Treasuries.

What to watch: the other hyperscalers’ quarterly results, and whether each raises capex again; whether Meta issues bonds before year-end; and its CDS spread as the cleanest daily gauge.

Divergence — did Meta beat, and how high did the CDS go?

What it means. Revenue beat; whether profit missed is unresolved and should not be stated either way until the reconciliation is in Meta’s 10-Q. On CDS, both numbers are records, so the direction is not in dispute, only the level. Use “about 115–119bp, a record.” What would settle it: the 10-Q’s EPS bridge, and the closing CDS mark from a named source.

The stablecoin rulebook is final and takes effect on 18 January. The OCC dropped the anti-evasion test on yield, and the banks say that lets deposits leak

US financialsUS politics & policyNew subjectUnderreportedPrimary source

What happened. The OCC’s final rule implementing the GENIUS Act is in today’s Federal Register (FR 2026-21706, 195 pages). It is effective 18 January 2027, the same day the Act takes effect. It covers permitted payment-stablecoin issuers and foreign issuers under OCC jurisdiction, plus custody by OCC-supervised entities. It leaves out Bank Secrecy Act, AML and sanctions rules, which come in a separate rule with Treasury. OCC bulletins the same day set weekly and quarterly reporting forms (open for comment 30 days after publication) and the process for applications and waivers. The OCC will announce separately when it starts taking applications. Bessent compared the Act to the National Currency Act of 1863 (OCC release).

The fight. The statute bars issuers from paying holders interest or yield. The final rule keeps that ban but, per the ABA, removes the proposed safeguards against indirect arrangements through affiliates, exchanges and distribution partners, and declines a broader presumption against evasion. American Banker reports the OCC will judge reward arrangements case by case. CryptoSlate, a crypto trade outlet, reports the rule also cut the immediate-liquidity reserve requirement from 10% to 5%. This paper did not verify that in the 195-page text.

Why it matters. The yield question decides whether stablecoins stay payment rails or become deposit substitutes. That affects funding costs for every bank, including JPMorgan and its peers.

What to watch: the FDIC’s and Fed’s parallel rules, and whether they diverge from the OCC on yield; the OCC’s application-opening date; and the Treasury BSA/AML rule.

Divergence — a strong framework or a loophole?

Substance (significance; one fact claim open). The OCC and Treasury describe “strong standards for reserve backing, redemption, and risk management.” ABA president Rob Nichols says issuers “can provide economically equivalent yield” while avoiding the statutory ban, and that the result preserves “the status quo for crypto platforms.” Both can be true: the standards on reserves and redemption are real, and the yield perimeter is narrower than the proposal.

Cause. Interest, plainly stated. The ABA represents the deposit-takers that would lose funding. It has lobbied on this point since 2025 and lists its own letters. The OCC and Treasury have made US leadership in digital assets an explicit goal. Crypto outlets frame the same changes as removing “safeguards banks wanted.” Neither side disputes what the text says on affiliates. They disagree on whether it matters.

What it means. The ABA’s factual description, that the proposed affiliate and anti-evasion provisions were dropped, is consistent with American Banker’s independent reporting, so take it as accurate. Whether deposit flight follows is an empirical question with no data yet. The reserve-ratio change (10% to 5%) is single-sourced to a trade outlet and should be held until checked against the rule text. What would settle the significance question: stablecoin “reward” products launched through exchanges after 18 January, and deposit data at community banks.

PHP2Shell, day two: millions of attempts and probing that began four days before the patch, but still no confirmed break-in

AI & tech industryUS politics & policyContinuationNew factIndependent corroboration

What’s new since Thursday’s lead. Cloudflare saw tens of thousands of attempts in the first 45 minutes after disclosure, then hundreds of thousands, and now “millions” (Matthew Prince, via the Guardian). It also saw thousands of attempts in the 10 hours before disclosure. Amazon Threat Intelligence says its MadPot honeypot was first hit on Saturday 17 Oct, four days before the patches, from an anonymising network. It says the flaw is “now being broadly exploited in the wild” but that it is unclear whether any attempts would succeed on real systems (BleepingComputer). The PHP team says details leaked two days before coordinated disclosure, including the CVE IDs and mitigation advice shared with critical-infrastructure providers, which forced an early release. The UK has taken 32 government services offline as a precaution (Guardian). VirusTotal counts more than 5.1m exposed PHP hosts (BleepingComputer).

Why it matters. This is the first mass-patching event in which frontier models did the hard part, chaining decades-old flaws into a working exploit (Wiz). The leak shows the other side: an AI-found bug is only as safe as the embargo around it. The NSA says the risk requires immediate patching across US agencies.

What to watch: whether CISA adds the CVEs to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, which is the formal line between attempts and exploitation; the first named victim; and older systems that cannot take the patch.

Divergence — is it being exploited?

Substance (facts, about definitions). Amazon says “broadly exploited in the wild.” Dataminr says activity “remains limited to scanning, fingerprinting, and use of non-working exploits, with no known compromises.” CISA had observed no exploitation when it issued its alert. The Calif researcher who co-found the flaw says exploitation does not look “widespread.”

Cause. Vantage, plus a word used two ways. Amazon runs honeypots, which are built to be attacked, so for Amazon “exploited” means attack traffic carrying exploit payloads. Dataminr and CISA count confirmed compromises of real systems. Amazon also sells security services, and so does Cloudflare, whose “millions” counts attempts blocked at its edge.

What it means. Both are right on their own definitions. Attacks are broad, and successful break-ins are unconfirmed. For anyone running PHP the practical answer is the same: patch or take the site offline. Do not read “exploited in the wild” as evidence of breaches yet. KEV listing or a named victim would settle it.

The Post says the Pentagon is building a deep-cover spy program for economic warfare against China. The Pentagon says it is “simply not true”

US politics & policyGeopoliticsNew subjectSingle-sourced

What happened. The Washington Post (Zach Dorfman and Warren Strobel, 21 Oct), citing about six people familiar with it, reports that the Pentagon is planning a program of “nonofficial cover” officers. These would be operatives posing as businesspeople with no visible government link, working as a covert arm of its economic-warfare campaign against China and others. The Post says it is overseen by Tom Rakusan, a 39-year CIA veteran who was the agency’s deputy director of operations, followed Deputy Secretary Steve Feinberg from Cerberus, and is now his senior adviser. It says the program is in planning and not operational. Congressional committees do not appear to have been briefed, and the legal authority is “murky”: former officials joke that it would be a “Title 60” hybrid of military (Title 10) and intelligence (Title 50) powers. Planners dropped the idea of housing it in Feinberg’s Economic Defense Unit because of scrutiny that would grow if Democrats win Congress. Pentagon deputy press secretary Jacob Bliss: the query “appears to be built on information that is simply not true.”

Why it matters. If true, it moves human espionage against Chinese companies from the CIA, under Title 50 and its oversight and findings, to a department with a $1.5tn budget request and looser rules. It does so just as a former CIA deputy argues publicly that spy-gathered secrets should be shared with US firms.

What to watch: a letter from the intelligence committees; any budget line or reprogramming; CIA comment, which it declined.

Divergence — does the program exist?

Substance (facts). The Post has about six sources, names the official overseeing the program, and describes its planning. The Pentagon flatly denies that “such an initiative is underway.”

Cause. A program in the planning stage can truthfully be denied as not “underway,” so the two statements may not conflict. The Pentagon also has every operational reason to deny a covert program, whatever the facts. On access, the Post’s sources include former senior CIA officials hostile to the idea (“build his own CIA”), who have an interest in killing it by exposure. Xinhua’s write-up adds nothing; it is a rewrite of the Post.

What it means. The Post’s account is detailed and specific, and the denial is generic and on a narrower point, so the evidence leans toward planning being real. Whether it goes ahead is open. Single-sourced: no second newsroom has independent sourcing. What would settle it: a second newsroom, a committee statement, or the program showing up in budget documents.

The Supreme Court lets New Hampshire drop student IDs as voter ID for November, with only Justice Jackson dissenting

US politics & policyNew subjectPrimary source

What happened. In Scanlan v. Coalition for Open Democracy (No. 26A512), the Court on Thursday stayed District Judge Joseph Laplante’s preliminary injunctions of 2 and 5 October, pending appeal in the First Circuit and any timely petition for certiorari. The unsigned order cites the Purcell principle that lower federal courts should not change election rules on the eve of an election. Justice Jackson was the only dissenter (order; SCOTUSblog).

What it changes. New Hampshire’s HB 323, in force since June, removed student ID cards from the photo IDs that are expressly valid to obtain a ballot. Laplante left that part of the law alone. What he blocked was Secretary of State David Scanlan’s April directive, insofar as it stopped or discouraged election officials from considering a student ID as evidence under the law’s separate safety-valve route for voters without qualifying ID. The stay revives that directive for 3 November. The coalition argued that the state had already changed its website to comply. Scanlan argued that the order forced him to undo six months of voter guides, posters and radio ads.

Why it matters. With eleven days to go, the Court has again treated a federal judge’s late order as the thing that disrupts an election, rather than the state guidance that order corrected. Jackson’s dissent argues the reverse: that Purcell is meant to prevent voter confusion, and an order correcting misstatements about the rules does exactly that. The practical effect is on student voters who have no other qualifying photo ID.

What to watch: the First Circuit’s merits schedule (nothing before the vote); and whether election officials get new guidance before 3 November.

Google says it is short of compute to serve its own new model, and in the same week it began selling OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s models on its cloud

AI & tech industryEnergy & gridNew subjectIndependent corroboration

What happened. On 21 October OpenAI announced that its frontier models and Codex agent workflows are coming to Google Cloud’s Model Garden. Customers will be able to buy them through Google Cloud Marketplace and count the spend against existing Google Cloud commitments, and the models will “soon” be in Google’s Antigravity and Gemini Agent. General availability is due in weeks. The announcement says Microsoft “remains OpenAI’s primary cloud partner” (OpenAI). The same day, the Anthropic-managed Claude Platform became generally available on Google Cloud, including Managed Agents and same-day model launches, billed against Google Cloud commitments (AI Compass). Also on 21 October, Sundar Pichai told the Sources interview that Gemini 4 Argon handled almost 25 trillion tokens in its first 72 hours, about three times Gemini 3’s launch, and that Google is hitting capacity limits serving it (ToolSift, CMSWire). CMSWire’s account says Google gives frontier-model work first call on compute, and that Cloud gives up near-term revenue as a result. ToolSift’s summary says Demis Hassabis has stepped back from day-to-day leadership of DeepMind, with Koray Kavukcuoglu taking over. That detail comes from one summary of the interview and was not checked against the transcript.

Why it matters. The three US frontier labs are now sold through one hyperscaler’s storefront, and that hyperscaler says demand for its own model exceeds its capacity. Compute, more than model quality, is the binding constraint, and cloud contracts are where the labs compete for it. That runs straight into the power and interconnection fight over data centres.

What to watch: Alphabet’s next capex guidance; whether Microsoft’s contract terms with OpenAI change again; and when Gemini 4 Argon reaches free users, which tells you whether the capacity limit is easing.

US financials · Treasury has proposed the rule that bars anyone controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying another. Comments close 23 November.

Primary source

The proposed rule (FR 2026-21675, 29 pages) implements section 1001 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. It defines “large institutional investor”, clarifies the statutory exceptions and the enforcement mechanism, and is meant to settle these questions “before the effective date” of the Act (FR abstract, read). Claude’s edition this morning read the body and reports a threshold of 350 homes under direct or indirect “investment control”, a 25% equity look-through, and civil penalties of up to the greater of $1m or three times the purchase price. This paper read the abstract and dates only and relies on that reading for the figures. The exceptions, build-to-rent above all, decide whether the ban has any force. Below the line because: it is a proposal with a month of comment ahead, the statute is already law, and Claude’s edition leads it in full. Sources: Federal Register 2026-21675; Claude Issue 49.

US politics & policy · USDA proposes moving SNAP benefit cards off magnetic stripes to chip, contactless or smart-card technology. Comments close 22 December.

Primary source

The Food and Nutrition Administration proposes to bring Electronic Benefit Transfer technology in line with “consumer payment processing industry standards”, to protect benefits from theft (FR 2026-21658, 40 pages; abstract and dates read). Claude’s edition, which read the technology provisions, says the rule permits rather than mandates a menu of options: an online stripe card, an offline smart card, an EMV chip, or a digital wallet. Skimming of stripe cards is the problem it targets. This brief covered the scale of the theft on 15 October. Below the line because: it is a proposed rule, the open questions are cost and mandate, and neither will be settled for months. Sources: Federal Register 2026-21658.

US politics & policy · DOJ and Education declare seven joint civil-rights guidance documents from 2015–2023 unenforceable, citing Loper Bright.

Primary source Underreported

A one-page notice (FR 2026-21685, read in full) says the departments will not rely on these documents when enforcing Title IV and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, the Equal Educational Opportunities Act, the ADA or Section 504. It says they will be revisited for consistency with the “single best meaning” of the statutes. The list includes the January 2015 English-learner Dear Colleague letter and fact sheets, the 2021 guidance on COVID-related harassment, the May 2023 resource on racial discrimination in student discipline, and the August 2023 Q&A on the Supreme Court’s Students for Fair Admissions decision. Schools lose a federal reference point on English-learner services and discipline disparities. The statutes themselves do not change. No newsroom coverage was found in this run. Below the line because: withdrawing non-binding guidance changes enforcement posture, not law, and its effect will show only in cases DOJ and OCR do or do not bring. Sources: Federal Register 2026-21685.

Energy & grid · The NRC asks for comment on a petition to apply plutonium-accounting rules to spent-fuel reprocessing plants, which are currently excluded.

Primary source

Howard L. Hall, a nuclear and radiochemist petitioning in a personal capacity, asks the NRC to remove reprocessing plants’ exclusion in 10 CFR 74.51(a). He wants them held to the material-control-and-accounting rules for formula quantities of strategic special nuclear material, with provisions for bulk handling in a continuous chemical process (PRM-74-1, FR 2026-21690, read). He argues that the current framework, which treats them as moderate strategic significance, is inadequate. Comments close 23 November. Context: the NRC’s June “Modernizing Materials Licensing” proposal would open a one-step licensing route for reprocessing under Part 70, as part of the administration’s push to expand nuclear power. Below the line because: it is a docketed petition, not an NRC proposal, and there is no commercial US reprocessing plant to apply it to yet. It matters if reprocessing licensing goes forward. Sources: Federal Register 2026-21690; NRC June proposal.

Science & public health · Nigeria confirms its first Bundibugyo Ebola case, imported. Accounts differ over whether the patient was in Uganda or only passed through.

Independent corroboration

Nigeria confirmed an imported case in Abuja on 20 October (WHO Africa; AP). Premium Times, citing Africa CDC, says the patient is a diplomat’s wife who had been on holiday in Uganda. Uganda says she only transited and that it has no active cases. Divergence (facts): the two accounts come from parties with opposite interests. Uganda wants to avoid travel restrictions, and Africa CDC is tracing the source. WHO Africa’s notice, as summarised in search results this paper read, says she had recently travelled to Uganda. Treat the route as unresolved until the contact-tracing report. It matters to Americans because US entry rules are written country by country: non-citizens who were in Uganda or South Sudan in the past 21 days cannot enter, and returning citizens are routed through Dulles, Atlanta or JFK (CDC). Nigeria is not on that list. Below the line because: it is one imported case with no reported onward spread, and the US measure that would change, adding Nigeria, has not moved. Sources: WHO Africa (abstract); AP (abstract); CDC.
