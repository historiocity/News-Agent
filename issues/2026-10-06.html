FinCEN withdrew the unhosted-wallet reporting rule and the mixing measure, one day after invoking crypto addresses against a $179bn network

US financials AI & tech industry Underreported Primary source

Two FinCEN withdrawals published today. The first kills the December 2020 proposal (85 FR 83840) that would have required banks and money service businesses to report transactions over $10,000 and keep records and verify identity above $3,000 where the counterparty was an unhosted wallet — a wallet whose keys the holder controls, with no institution in between. FinCEN says it will “take no further action” on it. The second kills the October 2023 section 311 special measure on convertible virtual currency mixing (88 FR 72701), on the ground that the proposal’s “expansive definition” could chill legitimate activity and burden institutions. Both withdrawals cite the President’s Working Group on Digital Asset Markets.

Now set that against yesterday. On 5 October FinCEN proposed a special measure against the A7 Network written to cover any “account or CVC address administered by or on behalf of” a sub-agent, with findings recording $17bn of dollar transmittals and at least $179.1bn in A7A5 stablecoin volume. This brief argued that a measure operating on transmittals by covered institutions cannot reach value moving between foreign wallets.

Today answers that in the worst way. The unhosted-wallet rule is precisely the instrument that would have given a US institution a record of a customer sending value to a self-custodied wallet. Withdrawn. The mixing measure is the instrument for the service that breaks the chain between sender and recipient. Withdrawn.

What remains is enforcement one named entity at a time, against a technology whose point is that entities are cheap to recreate. FinCEN says it will keep monitoring mixers and “may take appropriate steps in the future”.

What to watch: whether any designated A7A5 address is actually frozen, which is the only measure of whether entity-by-entity works; whether a replacement unhosted-wallet proposal appears with a narrower definition; and the 4 November A7 comment file.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not between sources — no secondary coverage of either withdrawal was found, as with yesterday’s measure — but between two readings of the same two-day sequence. The coherent reading: these are different problems. A7 is a specific, documented laundering network and deserves a targeted measure; the 2020 and 2023 proposals were drafted so broadly that they would have swept in ordinary users, and the mixing withdrawal says exactly that, citing lawful privacy uses on public blockchains. The incoherent reading: the agency has conceded that general visibility into self-custody is unobtainable and is now pursuing a channel it cannot see, one shell at a time.

Cause. Both readings rest on real facts, and the honest resolution is that they describe different timescales. The breadth objection to the 2020 rule is genuine — a $3,000 recordkeeping threshold on unhosted-wallet transfers would have covered a very large number of entirely lawful transactions, and the comment file on it was hostile for six years across two administrations. But withdrawal with “no further action” is not the same as replacement, and the gap it leaves is not theoretical: it is the exact gap the A7 findings describe. Note what the withdrawals do not contain: any alternative, any timetable, or any estimate of what visibility is lost. Both cite a policy document rather than an analysis.

What it means for you. The practical consequence for an ordinary holder is that the compliance burden you were going to face on self-custody transfers has been lifted, and that is a real benefit if you use crypto lawfully — no $3,000 identity verification when you move coins to your own wallet. The consequence for everyone, holder or not, is that the dollar-denominated sanctions regime now has a documented blind spot that its own enforcement filings describe in nine-figure terms, and the tool chosen to cover it is a list of names. If you want one observation that distinguishes the coherent reading from the incoherent one, it is whether a narrower unhosted-wallet proposal appears within six months. A withdrawal followed by a better rule is regulatory hygiene. A withdrawal followed by nothing is a decision not to look, and it will be legible in the next enforcement action’s findings rather than in any announcement.

Four SEC proposals in two days, and the threshold to be charged a performance fee falls from $2.7m to $1m

US financials Underreported Primary source

Two more SEC proposals today, both closing 7 December. Investment Adviser Performance-Based Compensation Modernization (file S7-2026-28) would replace the “qualified client” tests — currently $2.7m net worth or $1.4m under management — with the accredited-investor definition, which is $1m net worth excluding the home or $200,000 of income. It would also allow performance fees on registered funds and BDCs up to 20% of net gains, with board approval by a majority of independent directors.

Adviser and Regulated Fund Custody Rules; Crypto Custody (file S7-2026-35) would let an adviser hold a client’s crypto in self-custody — the adviser holding the private keys — where it determines in writing, quarterly, that no qualified custodian is available, subject to joint transaction authorisation, cybersecurity review, annual internal-control audits and quarterly statements showing addresses. It would also admit state-chartered trust companies as qualified custodians. The release cites 9.2% of US adults holding crypto and a $2.7 trillion global market cap as of May 2026.

Add yesterday’s two — professional credentials as accredited-investor status, and interval funds released from the 100% liquid-asset requirement — and four proposals in two days move the same perimeter. Who counts as sophisticated, what a retail-available fund may hold, who may be charged incentive fees, and who may hold the assets.

The performance-fee change is the one with the cleanest arithmetic. A qualified-client test exists because performance fees give a manager a one-sided bet: upside participation without symmetrical downside. Dropping the bar to $1m is not a marginal adjustment.

What to watch: whether the Commission acknowledges the four proposals as one programme; whether the performance-fee rule keeps any inflation indexing, since Dodd-Frank requires five-yearly adjustment of the current figures; and whether a single commissioner dissents, given last week’s quorum change.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Again no wire carried either proposal, so the disagreement is between two readings this brief is setting out and labelling as such. The harmonisation reading: the SEC is running two incompatible investor tests — accredited investor for buying private offerings, qualified client for being charged performance fees — and collapsing them into one is tidying, not loosening. Someone already permitted to buy a private fund is hardly protected by being forbidden to pay it a performance fee. The exposure reading: the two tests do different jobs. Eligibility asks whether you can evaluate a risk; the qualified-client test asks whether you can absorb a fee structure that pays the manager for volatility.

Cause. The harmonisation argument is stronger than it first looks, and it is worth saying so plainly: two thresholds for overlapping populations really is incoherent, and the lower one has a documented rationale. But the direction of harmonisation is a choice, and both proposals resolve downward. Nothing required collapsing the qualified-client test into the accredited test rather than the reverse. The custody proposal has the same shape — a genuine problem, that crypto often has no qualified custodian willing to hold it, answered by permitting the adviser to hold the keys rather than by building custodians. In both cases the obstacle is treated as the rule rather than the market.

What it means for you. If your net worth is between $1m and $2.7m excluding your home — which in 2026 includes a large number of people with an ordinary house and a full retirement account — you are the population this moves, and the practical effect is that products carrying performance fees become marketable to you next year. The question to ask of any such product is not whether you qualify but what the fee does in a bad year: a 20%-of-gains fee with no clawback means the manager keeps the good years and you keep the bad ones, which is why the high-water mark and any catch-up provision matter more than the headline rate. On the crypto half, the thing to read is the quarterly written determination: an adviser self-custodying your crypto must record each quarter that no qualified custodian was available, and that record is the document a court would look at if the keys were lost. The observation that would tell you how seriously the Commission takes its own safeguards is whether the final rule keeps the joint-authorisation requirement, which is the only one of them that prevents a single person moving the assets.

The ten-year went to 5.31% on a day with nothing to react to — the highest this brief has recorded

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Primary source

Treasury’s par yield curve for Monday 5 October: the ten-year at 5.31%, up 3 basis points from Friday and above the 5.29% of 30 September that had been the month’s high. The thirty-year is 5.66%, the twenty-year 5.70%, the two-year 4.84%, the three-month bill 4.22%.

The notable thing is the absence of a cause. Monday brought no economic release, no auction, no Federal Reserve communication and no fiscal event. Friday’s payroll print — 29,000 jobs with July revised into a loss — was three sessions old, and it was weak. Yields rose anyway, and the whole curve moved: the front end up 1 basis point, the ten-year up 3, the twenty-year up 3, the thirty up 3.

A parallel shift with no news is the signature of supply and term premium rather than expectations. If this were about the Federal Reserve, the two-year would be doing the work; it moved least. If it were about growth, a weak jobs report would pull yields down, not up.

Two structural facts persist. The twenty-year still out-yields the thirty-year by 4 basis points, as it has every day checked since 4 September — a kink about issuance and demand at particular maturities, not a forecast. And the ten-year is 12 basis points above the October 2023 peak of 5.19%, verified directly in Treasury’s 2023 table.

What is not available, and this brief has withdrawn it twice: “highest since” any date before November 2023. The thirty-four months in between have not been checked month by month.

What to watch: the 4 November refunding, which is where a genuine buyer problem would appear as weak bid-to-cover rather than as a daily level; whether the twenty-thirty inversion closes, which would say the kink was technical; and the next CPI print.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. What a rising long end with a soft labour market means, and the two readings are genuinely opposed. The fiscal reading: investors are demanding more to hold duration because of issuance volume and debt service, and the weak economy is irrelevant to that calculation. The inflation reading: the market expects price pressure the Federal Reserve will have to meet, which is consistent with a hiking cycle still pricing a December increase. Both predict rising long yields and they imply opposite things about whether the Fed should act.

Cause. The curve’s own shape is the best available discriminator, and on 5 October it points fiscal. A pure inflation-expectations move lifts the belly and the front end together, because higher expected prices mean higher expected policy rates; here the two-year moved a single basis point while the long end moved three, and the two-year is below where it was on 30 September. A parallel long-end shift with a static front end is what term premium looks like. The twenty-thirty inversion is corroborating: it is a statement about which maturities buyers want, not about the economy. The honest caveat is that one session is not evidence of a regime, and this brief has twice been wrong about this exact number, so a three-basis-point move is being reported as a level rather than a turn.

What it means for you. Treat 5.31% as the number your borrowing costs track and stop waiting for the weak economy to lower it — that has not worked for three weeks and the mechanism above explains why it may not. Concretely: thirty-year mortgage rates follow the ten-year with a spread that has been unusually wide all year, so a 5.31% ten-year is consistent with a mortgage in the low sevens, and refinancing arithmetic does not improve on a weak payroll report in a term-premium regime. If you hold bonds, the twenty-year is the maturity being punished and the thirty is relatively favoured, which is the opposite of the usual ordering. The event that would settle the fiscal reading is the 4 November refunding: watch the bid-to-cover and the dealer takedown rather than the yield, because a buyer problem shows up in who is left holding the auction, not in the screen.

The biggest drug-pricing reform in years is already law and almost nobody noticed — it delinks what PBMs earn from what drugs cost

US politics & policy Science & public health New thread Independent corroboration

Pharmacy benefit managers are the intermediaries that decide which drugs an insurance plan covers, at what tier, and what a pharmacy is paid to dispense them. Three companies control most of the market and each sits inside a larger health conglomerate. The structural complaint is that PBM compensation has been tied to a drug’s list price and to the rebate negotiated off it — which gives the intermediary an interest in a higher list price.

That link is now being cut by statute. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (H.R. 7148), enacted in February, delinks PBM compensation in Medicare Part D from a drug’s price and from rebate arrangements; requires PBMs to pass 100% of rebates through to employer health plans; and adds transparency and data-reporting obligations for Part D and employer plans. It is the most comprehensive federal regulation of the industry to date, and it arrived inside a spending bill.

It is a story in October because the coalition is bipartisan in a way almost nothing in health policy is, and the follow-on bills show where it goes. Crapo and Wyden, the Finance Committee chair and ranking member, have the PBM Price Transparency and Accountability Act (S. 3345). Warren and Hawley have the Break Up Big Medicine Act (S. 3822), barring a PBM from common ownership with pharmacies or provider groups — a structural remedy rather than a disclosure one.

Delinking and divestiture are different theories of the problem: the first assumes a conflicted incentive can be neutralised by changing how the intermediary is paid, the second that it cannot, because the conflict is ownership.

What to watch: the Part D plan bids for 2027, the first set priced under delinked compensation, and whether premiums or formularies move; whether employer plans actually receive 100% of rebates, which is an auditable claim; and whether S. 3822 gets a hearing, since an ownership bill is the test of how far the coalition goes.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether delinking reaches the problem. The reform reading: compensation tied to list price is the mechanism of the conflict, so severing it removes the incentive to prefer expensive drugs, and 100% rebate pass-through removes the retained spread. The structural reading, which is what the Warren–Hawley bill asserts: a PBM owned by the same company as an insurer, a specialty pharmacy and a provider group has many other ways to profit from steering, and fee reform addresses only the one that was legislated against.

Cause. The two readings weight vertical integration differently, and the structural case is stronger on the mechanics than the delinking case admits. If a PBM, an insurer and a mail-order pharmacy share a parent, the group’s margin can be taken at whichever entity is least regulated; delinking the PBM’s fee changes where the margin is booked, not whether it exists. That is not a reason to think delinking does nothing — Part D bids are a real constraint and rebate pass-through is auditable — but it is why the same senators who passed it are now writing an ownership bill. Note also that both readings come from interested parties: independent pharmacies and drug manufacturers fund much of the PBM critique, and manufacturers set the list prices that PBMs are accused of inflating.

What it means for you. The honest expectation is modest and specific: delinking changes what the plan pays the intermediary, and it is not a mechanism for lowering the price at your pharmacy counter. The place to look for an effect is your 2027 Part D plan documents if you are on Medicare — specifically the formulary tier of any drug you take, because a plan no longer rewarded for high list prices has less reason to prefer an expensive brand over a cheaper equivalent, and tier changes are where that shows up. If you have employer coverage, the 100% rebate pass-through is money owed to your plan, not to you, and whether it reaches your premium depends entirely on your employer. The single thing worth watching across both is whether independent pharmacy closures slow: reimbursement below acquisition cost is what has been closing them, and it is the most measurable consequence of any of this.

The FDA has exempted fourteen drug-screening test systems from premarket review, effective immediately

Science & public health New thread Underreported Primary source

A final rule published today, effective on publication, exempts fourteen Class II clinical toxicology test systems from premarket notification under section 510(m)(2) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The list is the standard drug panel: amphetamine, barbiturate, benzodiazepine, cocaine metabolite, codeine, LSD, methamphetamine, methadone, methaqualone, morphine, opiate, propoxyphene, cannabinoid and tricyclic antidepressant test systems.

Exemption means a manufacturer no longer files a 510(k) demonstrating substantial equivalence to a cleared device before marketing. The FDA’s basis: these devices have well-established characteristics, users can detect changes by visual examination or routine testing, and changes are unlikely to affect safety or effectiveness. The stated purpose is to decrease regulatory burden.

Process was followed properly, which is worth saying because this brief has spent two weeks on rules that skipped it. The FDA proposed this on 1 May 2026, took comments to 30 June, received about 70, and responded to 21 comment categories before finalising.

What makes it consequential is where these assays are used, which is almost never in a hospital making a treatment decision. Drug screens of exactly this kind decide employment, probation and parole conditions, pain-management contracts, child-custody and child-welfare proceedings, and participation in treatment programmes. A false positive in that setting is not a medical error; it is a legal event. These assays are also the ones with documented cross-reactivity problems — the amphetamine and opiate immunoassays in particular.

Four further FDA rules published today classify a DNA sequencing test, a hyperoxia monitoring device and a focused ultrasound system, three of them also effective on publication.

What to watch: whether the comment responses addressed confirmatory testing, since an exempt screen is only as safe as the requirement to confirm it by mass spectrometry; whether any state forensic or employment-testing programme references 510(k) status; and the next 510(m) exemption list.

A pilot programme would let 256 truckers stop the 14-hour clock for up to three hours. Fatigue is in 30% of fatal truck crashes

US politics & policy World economy New thread Primary source

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration has proposed a pilot programme to test whether a driver may pause the 14-hour driving window. Today a driver has 14 consecutive hours to complete driving after 10 hours off duty, and the clock runs whatever happens. The pilot would allow a single pause of 30 minutes to 3 hours, taken at a cargo pickup or delivery location, that does not count against the window. 256 drivers, carriers representative of the overall distribution of carrier sizes, 16 weeks. Comments close 7 December.

The agency’s own figure for the stakes: driver fatigue “may be involved in over 30 percent of fatal” commercial motor vehicle crashes.

The monitoring is unusually serious and is the reason to take the pilot seriously rather than as cover. Participants would be measured with psychomotor vigilance tests, subjective sleepiness ratings, wrist actigraphy, driving records and telematics. That is a sleep-science protocol, not a survey, and actigraphy in particular measures whether a driver actually slept rather than whether the logbook says so.

The practical argument for the pause is detention time: a driver held three hours at a shipper’s dock loses three hours of his window and is then pressed to drive the remainder fast or park short of a paid delivery. The pause converts someone else’s inefficiency into neutral time.

The argument against is that a 14-hour window plus a 3-hour pause is a 17-hour day, and fatigue tracks time awake rather than time driving. Whether the pause restores anything depends on what the driver does in it — which is what the actigraphy would reveal.

What to watch: whether the pilot requires the pause to be sleeper-berth rest rather than any off-duty time; the 7 December comment file, where the Teamsters and the safety-advocacy groups will appear; and whether the 256-driver sample is powered to detect a crash-rate effect, which it almost certainly is not.

The Nobel in medicine went to optogenetics — switching single neurons on with light, from a protein in pond algae

Science & public health New thread Independent corroboration

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded on 5 October to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford, Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University in Berlin and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg, for light-gated ion channels and optogenetics. The prize is 12 million Swedish kronor, about $1.2m, shared.

The chain of discovery is the part worth knowing. Hegemann was studying how a single-celled green alga swims toward light. He and Nagel identified a protein that, illuminated by blue light, opens a channel and creates an electrical impulse. Deisseroth’s contribution was to put that protein into neurons, so that a pulse of light switches a chosen cell on or off.

That turns neuroscience from a correlational science into an experimental one. Before optogenetics you could record which neurons fired during a behaviour; afterwards you could activate a specific population and see whether the behaviour followed. Almost everything claimed about circuits for fear, reward, appetite, sleep and movement in the past fifteen years rests on it.

It is also a case study in basic research. Nobody funded pond-algae phototaxis in order to map the brain, and no plausible research-priority exercise would have selected it.

The caveat worth carrying: optogenetics requires genetically modifying the target cells, so it is a laboratory tool rather than a therapy. Its clinical descendants are narrow — retinal work is the furthest along — and a reader should not expect this prize to be about treatments.

What to watch: the physics prize today and chemistry tomorrow; and whether the citation’s emphasis on a tool rather than a disease mechanism signals how the committee is weighting methods.

US politics & policy The Supreme Court heard the Boulder climate case yesterday. This brief could not obtain a transcript, so it cannot tell you how it went.

The posture matters. Boulder sued Suncor and ExxonMobil on 17 April 2018, alleging deception about their products’ role in climate change. The jurisdictional question is the one that could make the case anticlimactic: the Court can decide it lacks jurisdiction over an interlocutory state-court ruling and never reach preemption, which would leave two dozen similar suits exactly where they are.

US politics & policy A rule called Transparency in Coverage adds phone price quotes — and cuts out-of-network rate reporting from monthly to quarterly.

A joint Treasury, Labor and HHS final rule published today, effective 7 December 2026 for plan years beginning on or after 1 January 2027, revises the Transparency in Coverage regime. Added: plans must provide cost-sharing estimates by telephone on request, satisfying both the ACA and the No Surprises Act, with the number of providers per request capped at no fewer than 20 a day. Clarified: estimates must say they do not account for out-of-network balance billing where state and federal law permits it.

On the machine-readable files — the bulk price data researchers and employers actually use — in-network rate files move from plan level to provider-network level, cutting duplication and file size, and rates for “unlikely provider-to-service mappings” are dropped. Out-of-network files get a market-level structure, a longer claims window, and a cadence moving from monthly to quarterly.

That last change is the one to notice. Reducing a disclosure’s frequency by a factor of three, inside a rule named Transparency in Coverage, is a real trade: smaller and cleaner files against less timely out-of-network data. Whether it nets out as more transparency depends entirely on whether anyone was using the monthly cadence.

Below the line because it is a technical revision to an existing regime with a 2027 compliance date, and the cost figures are in charts this brief could not parse. It leads if researchers show the quarterly cadence breaks an existing use.

Science & public health The EPA opened comment on which areas fail the 9.0 microgram soot standard — without publishing the list.

The EPA proposed initial air quality designations for the 2024 revised primary annual PM2.5 standard, which 40 CFR 50.20 sets at 9.0 micrograms per cubic metre, down from 12.0. Science & public health The FDA is asking how to design clinical trials for ibogaine — a psychedelic that treats opioid dependence and can stop the heart.

The FDA published a request for information on clinical trial design for ibogaine drug products. An RFI rather than a guidance means the agency is asking the field how a trial should be built before it says what it will accept.

Ibogaine is the hardest case in psychedelic medicine. It has the strongest anecdotal record of any agent for interrupting opioid dependence — often from a single administration — and it is cardiotoxic, prolonging the QT interval and causing fatal arrhythmias; deaths in unsupervised clinics are documented. It is Schedule I in the United States and legal under supervision in a few jurisdictions. Texas appropriated state money for ibogaine trials in 2025.

So the design questions are genuinely hard and that is why the FDA is asking: a dose that works may be near a dose that kills, the experience cannot be blinded, and a single-administration treatment does not fit the usual trial architecture. An RFI is the appropriate first step rather than a delay.

This brief has not read the RFI and does not have the comment deadline, the specific questions asked, or whether any sponsor has an open IND.

Energy & grid The EPA has set 2027 allowances for the hydrofluorocarbon phasedown — the one climate programme running on a statutory schedule.

The EPA published 2027 allowance allocations for the hydrofluorocarbon phasedown. HFCs are the refrigerants that replaced ozone-depleting CFCs and are themselves potent greenhouse gases, hundreds to thousands of times more warming than carbon dioxide per unit mass.

The programme is distinctive for being statutory rather than discretionary: the AIM Act of 2020 directs an 85% reduction in HFC production and consumption by 2036 against a baseline, on a schedule Congress wrote. The EPA issues allowances each year, and allowance holders trade them. That makes it a cap-and-trade system enacted with bipartisan support and almost never described as one.

Its practical reach is appliances. HFC availability sets the cost of servicing existing air-conditioning and refrigeration equipment, and an annual allocation is what determines whether a refrigerant price spike is coming — which lands on anyone repairing rather than replacing a system.

This brief has not read the allocation notice and has no allowance quantities, no comparison to 2026, and no indication of whether the schedule step applies this year.

Science & public health NHTSA is asking about blood transfusions in ambulances — a change that would move a hospital procedure to the roadside.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, which runs the federal office of emergency medical services, published a request for information on prehospital blood transfusion. Separately it published a driver monitoring systems assessment notice.

Prehospital transfusion is a live question with a real evidence base behind it. Haemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death after injury, most of those deaths occur before hospital arrival, and military experience with whole blood forward of a hospital is what drives civilian interest. A handful of US systems carry blood on ambulances or helicopters; most do not, because of cold-chain logistics, wastage of a scarce donated product, and the cost of stocking units that may expire unused.

An RFI from the federal EMS office is how a practice moves from a few well-resourced systems toward a national standard — or how the agency establishes that it should not.

This brief has not read either notice and has no comment deadline, no questions list, and no figures.

US politics & policy Two agencies jointly deleted the regulations for a 911 grant programme, effective on publication — the kind of housekeeping worth one line.

Commerce’s NTIA and Transportation’s NHTSA jointly published a rule “Eliminating Obsolete Regulations Related to the 911 Grant Program”, effective on publication. The grant programme those regulations implemented has ended, so the rules govern nothing.

It belongs here for one reason: it is the cleanest example this brief has seen of what an immediate-effect rule should look like. Removing regulations for a programme that no longer exists affects nobody’s rights, nobody could usefully comment, and an effective date on publication is exactly right.

That is worth stating because this brief has run three items in two weeks on rules taking effect immediately where the characterisation was doing real work — the SEC quorum change, several FDA classifications, and the immediate-effect rulemaking thread generally. The comparison is the point: the test is not whether notice and comment was skipped but whether anything turned on skipping it. Here nothing does.

The underlying policy question — that 911 systems still need capital to move to next-generation IP-based call handling, and the grant programme that funded it has lapsed — is not addressed by the rule and is not established here.
