The sanctions law was due to bite today. On Friday night the President waived all of it.

US politics & policy Geopolitics Continuation Primary source

This brief has carried the line "the Graham Act implementation deadline is 18 October and nothing has been filed" for five consecutive issues. Something was filed, late on 16 October, and it is an executive order that imposes the Act's measures and suspends them in the same document.

The order is titled Implementing the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026; the Act is at 22 U.S.C. 10802, signed 18 September. Section 2 does the implementing. The duty on Russian goods rises 500 percent. The duty on goods from the five largest importers of Russian crude and gas rises 100 percent if they knowingly buy more on or after today. The duty on goods from the top five facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion rises 100 percent. All of it stacks on existing duties, anti-dumping and Sections 232 and 301 included.

Then section 4 waives it. Under section 115(a) of the Act the President may waive any sanction, restriction or duty on submitting to Congress a certification that the waiver is in the national interests plus a report explaining the basis. He has waived the duties in sections 112 and 113; the sanctions and restrictions in section 102(d); and then, in subsection (c), sections 102(a), 102(e), 103, 104, 105, 106, 107, 108, 109, 110, 111 and 117 — which is substantially the whole statute.

Two provisions matter more than the list. Section 4(d): the waivers hold "until such time as the President or the Secretary of State… determine that further action is appropriate." No expiry. And section 5 delegates section 115 itself — the waiver power — to the Secretary of State. What survives is narrow: sanctions under the Russia emergency continued on 24 March 2026, as in effect the day before enactment.

What to watch: whether the certification and the report are published, because they are the entire stated justification and they went to Congress, not to the public; whether any committee demands them; and the USTR monitoring reports section 3 requires, which are the only machinery the order leaves running.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a dispute about facts — the order is public and says what it says — but about what kind of act this is. The administration's framing is in the title: this is implementing the Act, using an authority Congress itself wrote into section 115. The critics' framing, from members of both parties including a co-author, is that a law passed with bipartisan majorities and signed a month ago has been nullified before it ever operated.

The cause. Both readings are available because Congress built the escape hatch. Section 115 is real, and the order follows its procedure: certification, report, both submitted. What the drafters plainly did not anticipate is the hatch being used across every operative section at once, on the eve of commencement, with no end date and the power then handed to a cabinet secretary. The administration's vantage is a negotiation it says is live — the order follows a diesel arrangement with Moscow that this brief has reported since 10 October. The critics' vantage is institutional: they are measuring what a statute is worth.

What it means for you. Treat the Graham Act as law in force and of no present effect, and do not expect the tariffs. The thing that would change it is not a court — section 115 is a lawful authority and a challenge would be uphill — but either publication of the certification, which would let the reasoning be argued, or the statutory termination route, which this brief could not verify today and which is reported to require certifying that Russia has signed a peace agreement Ukraine accepts and ceased hostilities. No such certification exists. The observable to watch is whether Congress gets the report and makes it public.

Oil through Hormuz fell from 20.8 million barrels to 2.9 million in three days

Geopolitics Energy & grid Continuation Independent corroboration

For twenty-nine consecutive issues this brief has recorded that it could not get a transit count for the Strait of Hormuz, and has refused to characterise the chokepoint without one. The number exists and it is severe. According to Kpler data reported this weekend: nearly 20.8 million barrels of oil passed through on 8 October, and only 2.9 million on 11 October. That is an 86 percent fall in three days.

Around it, a count of incidents rather than claims. Last Sunday six vessels were attacked in or near the strait — the most in a single day since the war began — attributed jointly to the security firm Vanguard, the International Maritime Organization and UKMTO, which is three independent monitors rather than a belligerent. Two tankers were struck in Gulf waters off Saudi Arabia on Friday. Iran's army says two more caught fire on Thursday and Friday in what it calls its prohibited zone in the Gulf of Oman.

Saturday's incident is an IRGC claim and is reported as one. The Guard says two tankers, each carrying about two million barrels of crude, hit naval mines and caught fire. No ship names, no flags, no cargo origin. IRNA says both had switched off positioning and radar and used an unauthorised route; the IRGC also claims one crossed "with US support". None of it is checkable here.

What is checkable is the regime Iran has imposed: ships must seek Iranian authorisation to transit, and its naval commander Rear Admiral Ali Azmaei warned this week that any vessel deactivating its navigation systems will be treated as hostile. The US runs a counter-blockade on Iranian oil exports. About a fifth of world oil consumption used to pass through here.

What to watch: the Kpler series, which is now this brief's instrument for the thread and should be read rather than inferred; whether any named vessel is confirmed by an owner, insurer or flag state, which no mine claim has yet produced; and the pass-through into diesel, which is the fifth story in this issue.

Single-sourced and unconfirmed for Saturday's two tankers, which is an IRGC claim with no vessel named. Kenya is up to seven Ebola cases and three of them are the nurses who treated the first

Science & public health US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief reported Kenya's third case. The Health Ministry said on Saturday that there are four more, for a total of seven. Three of the four are health workers who treated the country's index patient. The fourth is another relative of his, in quarantine since 6 October.

The health-worker infections are the change, and they are a different category from what came before. Cases one through four were a family cluster, all of them people already in quarantine. Infections among the clinicians who treated the index patient are transmission inside a hospital — which is how this disease has historically got out of the family and into the health system, and which is the point at which contact tracing stops being sufficient on its own. Al Jazeera notes these are the first infections known to have been passed on inside Kenya.

The containment numbers remain good and should be said. Of the index patient's 130 contacts, the ministry says 128, or 98.5 percent, are quarantined, against a global benchmark of 85 percent to break a transmission chain. It maintains there is no evidence of community spread. But observers are asking how the index patient passed airport screening and reached so many people — he had been ill in the DRC for a month, crossed into Uganda by road and flew to Nairobi on 3 October.

The American instrument has not moved. The CDC order under 42 U.S.C. 265 suspends entry from the DRC, Uganda and South Sudan. Kenya is still not on it, and Kenya now has in-country transmission rather than an imported case. The Bundibugyo strain has no vaccine and no treatment. The DRC epidemic has passed 9,000 cases and 4,300 deaths.

What to watch: whether any case appears outside the traced contact group, which is what "no community spread" rests on; the health workers' own contacts, a second ring nobody has published a number for; and the order's 10 November expiry, when the country list is decided again.

550,000 in France, and cost-of-living protests in four countries on one Saturday

World economy Geopolitics New thread Independent corroboration

France had its largest demonstrations in decades on Saturday: 550,000 nationwide by the Interior Ministry's count, 110,000 in Paris. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said 1,200 were arrested and 150 police officers injured, and called it a miracle the toll was not higher. Shops were looted in nine cities. A fire in Limoges largely destroyed the 140-year-old town hall; the city halls of Marseille and Lille were broken into and Lyon's tourist office burned.

The composition is the part worth knowing. This began more than a month ago as a student movement over education reform and has widened into something cross-class: nurses, firefighters, teachers, parents and human-rights lawyers marched, the CGT backed it demanding higher wages and pensions and lower prices, and remnants of the gilets jaunes — the 2018 movement that began over fuel taxes — joined. A statue of Marianne at Place de la République was dressed in a yellow vest.

The same Saturday, separate and uncoordinated: tear gas and water cannon in Brussels and water cannon in Liège, after weeks of Belgian student protests over registration fees; tens of thousands in Lisbon over living costs, wages and housing, where house prices have more than doubled since 2017; and clashes at Piazza Duomo in Milan.

This brief's bar requires a US channel, and the obvious one is weaker than it looks. Fuel is a strand here, not the cause — the reporting puts education, wages, housing and public services ahead of it. The firmer channel: four allied governments are absorbing street pressure over prices in the same month the United States waived its Russia sanctions and is negotiating over the energy trade that sets those prices. That is the first story in this issue, seen from the other end.

What to watch: whether the French government concedes on wages or pensions, which is what the CGT's involvement is for; whether the gilets jaunes strand grows as diesel stays high; and whether any of these governments links its own position on Russian energy to domestic prices, which is the point at which this becomes a US story outright.

Turnout and arrest figures are attributed to the French Interior Ministry and the Prime Minister respectively; the looted cities list is AFP's; the Belgian, Portuguese and Italian accounts are Al Jazeera's. No primary document, and no official source read directly — these are wire figures. Sources: Al Jazeera on France · Al Jazeera on the four countries · Wikipedia current events

The diesel run broke after five days and the gap to petrol widened anyway

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

AAA's national averages this morning: diesel $6.3224, down 0.52 cents and the first fall after five consecutive daily rises. Regular petrol $4.2988, down 0.97 cents on the day and 5.97 cents on the week.

Both fell, and the gap between them got wider anyway, because petrol fell almost twice as fast. The premium is $2.0236, against $2.0191 yesterday and $1.999 the day before. It is the widest reading of the entire move, and it widened on a day diesel went down.

That is worth separating out, because it changes what the number is telling you. For a week the premium has been widening because diesel was climbing — a distillate story, consistent with the chokepoint above. Today it widened because petrol is falling faster than diesel, which is a different mechanism: petrol tracks crude and seasonal demand, diesel tracks refining capacity and distillate stocks, and the two have come apart. Over the month, diesel is down 12.5 cents from $6.4476 while petrol is down 17 cents from $4.4687.

Year on year the divergence is the whole picture: diesel +73.9%, regular +41.4%, and a premium that was 59.6 cents a year ago. The chart below plots both fuels rather than the spread, because the spread hides which side is moving.

What to watch: Tuesday's EIA weekly reading, the first containing this week's rises and the instrument that settles direction; whether petrol's fall continues, since a widening premium driven by cheap petrol is a demand signal rather than a supply one; and distillate inventories, still the number nobody reachable publishes promptly.

Confidence: Primary source. Both averages were read from AAA this morning; the premium, the percentage changes and the month-ago comparisons are this brief's arithmetic on AAA's published figures. Sources: AAA national averages · 17 October

US politics & policy The fourth round of No Kings protests ran this weekend, and this time it is aimed at early voting.

The fourth round of the No Kings demonstrations took place across the United States this weekend. NPR's framing of it is the thing that distinguishes this round from the last three: the focus is early voting, sixteen days before the midterms.

That is a movement converting turnout into an electoral mechanic, a different and more consequential act than protest. Prior rounds drew about 5 million (June 2025), 7 million (October 2025) and 8 million (March 2026) — organiser estimates, to be read as claims rather than counts.

No turnout figure for this round was available to this brief, and without one there is no way to say whether the movement is growing, flat or shrinking at the moment it tries to convert. That is precisely the fact a lead would need.

Below the line because the number that would make it a story does not exist yet, and because the configuration weighs campaign tactics negatively — though a mass movement redirecting itself at a ballot deadline is closer to substance than to tactics, and it will be a lead if the turnout holds up.
