Energy Geopolitics Macro New fact

The refinery pause is over. Ukrainian drones hit a refinery in Russia’s Ryazan region early Thursday, starting a fire, according to Russia’s Defence Ministry and the regional governor (AP). Hours earlier Zelenskyy told the Financial Times that Ukraine had honoured the “energy ceasefire” Trump announced on Sunday for three days while Russia launched roughly 40 ballistic missiles at Ukrainian energy targets: “The block is out. We’re done” (CNBC). Russia’s deputy prime minister Alexander Novak said refineries in Saratov and Ryazan were hit on Wednesday, per Interfax as cited by CNBC-TV18, which puts Ryazan at about 400,000 b/d. This closes the frame this brief carried since Sunday: the ceasefire never got a published text, Moscow never confirmed it, and it has now ended from the Ukrainian side too.

The Washington lever on supply. A US official said on Wednesday the administration is considering easing sanctions on some of Russia’s unregulated “shadow fleet” tankers so that “legal oil” can move on previously sanctioned vessels, which could lower freight rates and add flexibility to diesel shipments (Reuters via Investing.com). That would sit on top of General Licence 135, the Russian-diesel licence twelve senators asked the President to rescind on Tuesday. Brent fell $2.28 (2.2%) to $103.78 by 07:29 GMT on the report; WTI was $93.75.

The chokepoint. The Singapore-flagged container ship GFS Pride sank near Oman’s Musandam peninsula after a projectile strike on Tuesday; all 21 crew were evacuated (Maritime and Port Authority of Singapore via BBC). Its sister GFS Giselle was holed on Wednesday but reached anchorage, and the bulk carrier Cape Dao, hit on 23 Sep, was reported sinking. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said the ship hit mines on an “unauthorised” route through Omani waters. The IMO counts at least 24 seafarers killed in 106 confirmed incidents since February; Kpler put September Hormuz crude flows at about 80% of the pre-war 16m b/d (BBC). Separately, CNN reported — single-sourced, via Reuters — that US planners are weighing the GBU-43 “mother of all bombs” for possible future strikes on Iranian energy and nuclear sites if the President authorises renewed operations (lead 8).

The pump, on the right instrument. AAA’s national diesel average is $6.3173 this morning, up 1.76¢ from $6.2997 and 3.26¢ above a week ago; regular is $4.3344. That is the third straight daily rise. But the EIA weekly series — which yesterday’s edition said had not yet posted — did post on 14 Oct, and it reads $6.179 for the week of 12 Oct, down 2.0¢ from $6.199. The weekly path from the $6.529 peak (week of 21 Sep) is −14.7¢, −18.3¢, −2.0¢: still falling, but the fall has nearly stopped. Under this brief’s 22 Sep rule, that is a deceleration in the trend instrument, not a turn, and the three-day AAA rise is a level reading, not a direction call. API data cited by Reuters showed an 11.1m-barrel US crude draw for the week to 9 Oct; EIA’s official inventory report is at noon ET today, a day late for Columbus Day.

Why one chain. The refinery strikes, the shadow-fleet decision, the Hormuz sinkings and the diesel price are one supply shock seen from four places. What to watch: today’s EIA distillate stocks; whether the shadow-fleet easing is published as a licence; whether Ryazan’s run rate is reported cut; next Monday’s EIA weekly retail diesel.

September CPI: 3.7% headline, 2.6% core, gasoline more than half the monthly rise — it sets a 3.6% Social Security raise, lifts the odds of an October hike to about even, and flattened the curve rather than lifting the long end

Macro US debt & fiscal Print

The print (BLS primary). All-items CPI rose 0.6% in September, seasonally adjusted, after 0.4% in August, and 3.7% over twelve months (3.4% in August). Gasoline rose 9.1% on the month and, in BLS’s words, accounted for “over half” of the monthly increase; energy is +20.4% on the year, gasoline +34.2%, fuel oil +74.2%. Core (ex food and energy) rose 0.3% for a second month and 2.6% on the year, up from 2.4%. Food was flat. Shelter rose 0.4%; airline fares rose 1.2% on the month and 22.4% on the year — the most direct route by which jet fuel reaches a household that does not buy diesel. Edward Jones, quoted by Reuters, notes core is running about 3.4% annualised over three months. The October CPI is due 10 Nov — a week after the midterms.

What it set. CPI-W, the index the Social Security Act ties benefits to, rose 3.9% on the year. SSA announced Wednesday a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment for 2027: about $75 a month more for the average retiree, for nearly 72 million beneficiaries from January, and for 7.3 million SSI recipients from 31 Dec (SSA release). This is a direct fiscal cost that moves with the fuel shock.

What it did to the Fed. Reuters’ market wrap put the futures-implied probability around 49%, up from 19% before the release (its sentence wording is muddled between “hike” and “keep”; an Investing.com analysis put October hike odds at 45%). Read together: the market moved from about one-in-five to about even odds of a hike at the 27–28 Oct meeting. Several economists quoted by Reuters expect a hold in October and a December move. Under this brief’s Fed rule, the decision is the item; this is the print that sets it up.

What it did to the curve (issuer series). Reuters quoted the 10-year at 5.304% intraday after the release. Treasury’s own Daily Par Yield Curve for the 14 Oct close reads 5.19%, down 2bp from 5.21%, while the 2-year rose 3bp to 4.82% and the 30-year fell 4bp to 5.52%. This brief prints the issuer close (18 Sep rule). The 2s10s spread narrowed from 42bp to 37bp on the day: the market priced more tightening at the front and less term premium at the back. The 20-year at 5.57% still sits above the 30-year — the hump persists into next Wednesday’s 20-year reopening.

Why it matters. The headline-core gap (1.1 points) is energy. That makes the oil chain (lead 1) the swing factor for the Fed, for the COLA bill, and for what the long end has to absorb.

The Omar investigation is aimed at denaturalisation, WSJ reports: more than a dozen subpoenas to a Minnesota grand jury over whether her age was altered on 1990s papers — no suit has been filed, and the bar is high

US policy Courts New fact

What was reported. The Wall Street Journal reported late Wednesday that the federal investigation of Rep. Ilhan Omar (D-Minn.) aims to build a case to revoke her citizenship and eventually deport her, citing US officials and people familiar with it. Investigators have issued more than a dozen subpoenas, including to relatives, for testimony before a grand jury in Minnesota (WSJ via Al Jazeera). The question at issue is whether Omar or her late father altered her age on immigration paperwork to show 17 rather than 18, so that she derived citizenship through him rather than applying separately. Her father died in 2020. NBC reports agents seized her phone in Monday’s search; the Journal says they also took her passport, computers and notebooks.

What cuts the other way. The Journal reports Homeland Security Investigations looked at a similar allegation under the Biden administration and did not pursue it for lack of evidence of fraud; the Washington Post separately reported DHS declined to move forward years ago. Omar’s office calls the inquiry baseless. Trump said Monday he had “nothing to do” with the search.

The legal mechanism. No denaturalisation case has been filed. Civil denaturalisation requires the government to prove fraud or a willful material misrepresentation by clear and convincing evidence; the criminal route requires proof beyond reasonable doubt, and the Supreme Court held unanimously in Maslenjak (2017) that the misstatement must have played a role in obtaining citizenship (Time). The Migration Policy Institute counted an average of 11 cases a year from 1990 to 2017; DOJ has announced efforts against at least 108 people in 2026. Omar is on the 3 Nov ballot for a fifth term.

Why it is here. The target is a sitting member of Congress weeks before her election. The substance is the evidentiary standard and the grand-jury record, not the campaign.

The US channel. A US-controlled vehicle for Russian state money would put Washington, not Brussels, in charge of the largest bargaining chip in any settlement, and would put US policy across the EU’s legal framework for the freeze. The Ukraine balance here is held at the standing level: this is the policy story, the strikes are in lead 1.

Frozen-assets story:

Treasury proposes the rules that bar most non-citizens from ACA premium tax credits from 2027 and add pre-enrolment verification — implementing the 2025 tax law, comments by 30 November

US policy US debt & fiscal New fact

The document. Treasury and the IRS published proposed regulations today (REG-101576-26, 91 FR 65494) amending eligibility for the Premium Tax Credit — the refundable credit that subsidises Exchange coverage — its computation, and liability for excess advance payments. Comments close 30 Nov; a telephone hearing is set for 8 Dec (FR API).

What it does. Per the IRS release (IR-2026-121), from 2027 the credit is restricted to US citizens, US nationals and “certain lawfully present noncitizens,” and new verification criteria apply before any advance payment. A tax-practitioner analysis of the text adds that coverage obtained through an income-based special enrolment period is excluded for plan years after 2025 and that the cap on repaying excess advance credits is removed; that reading is secondary and not checked against the preamble here. HHS is expected to issue parallel Exchange rules.

The framing and the number. Bessent calls it closing “eligibility loopholes.” The release cites CMS’s removal of more than 760,000 “unverified” enrollees and $2.2bn in savings — an administration figure, not an independent estimate. The mechanism is the story: a statute written in 2025 becomes, through this rule, who loses a subsidy in 2027, and uncapped repayment moves the risk of an income misestimate from the Treasury to the household.
