Fed’s Cook: AI buildout is a top 2027 inflation risk — electricity and water already up ~5%

AI & tech industry Macro & markets New subject Primary source

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, in a 28 September Oakland speech now carrying the live policy conversation into this morning’s wires, put the physical AI buildout inside the inflation debate rather than outside it. Prices for AI-related goods — chips, computers, software — have surged. Some of that, she argues, is a relative-price shift that monetary policy should look through. The part that worries her for 2027 is economy-wide pressure from shared inputs: data-centre demand for construction labour and energy can lift costs in sectors that have nothing to do with models. Electricity and water costs are each up around 5% over the past year, “potentially attributable in part to AI”; core goods are running above a 3% annual pace. Firms have spent only a small fraction of roughly $2 trillion in announced data-centre plans.

On Thursday she told New York Fed President John Williams the same thesis in sharper form: the AI buildout may create inflationary pressures “that may not resolve very quickly,” and it is “one of the main things that concerns me right now for 2027.” She still expects longer-run productivity gains and modest disinflation later — but not in time to offset broadening pressure this year. Geopolitical supply shocks, including the Middle East conflict, remain in her near-term inflation stack alongside the buildout. She voted with the unanimous September 25 bp hike.

What to watch: whether electricity and water components keep feeding core; whether the $2 trillion announcement pipeline converts into measured investment fast enough to keep the shared-input channel live; and whether any FOMC speaker contests Cook’s timing on when productivity would offset the buildout.

Instrument note — speech versus decision

Per the reader’s Fed cadence this is a print-adjacent primary (a named governor’s inflation risk ranking), not a quiet state-of-play item. The decision remains the September hike; Cook’s speech is the new fact about how a voter weights AI capex against the 2% path.

Fed will publish stress-test models and average two years of losses into the capital buffer

US financials New subject Primary source Underreported

Two Federal Reserve final rules published in today’s Federal Register (effective dates staggered) change how large-bank capital requirements are set. The transparency rule (FR Doc 2026-20247, effective 2 November) requires the Board to invite public input annually on stress-test scenarios and any material model changes, publish descriptions of the models used each year by 15 May, and adopt the models for the 2027 supervisory stress test. Trading-book firms will face two global market shock components each year; the Board will use the shock that produces the largest losses for each firm.

The companion capital-plan rule (FR Doc 2026-20246) requires the Board, when setting a firm’s stress capital buffer, to average the CET1 declines from the two most recent annual supervisory stress tests for firms tested in both years. Averaging begins with requirements effective 1 January 2029 so that only models that have gone through the public-input process feed the buffer. The Board’s 30 September press release says the package should cut year-over-year volatility in capital requirements by roughly half without a material change in aggregate capital. A parallel request for comment (FR Doc 2026-20245) asks whether the noninterest-income model should better capture fee-business diversity.

What to watch: the first May 15 model publication; whether “material” model changes become a lobbying venue that softens shocks; and how JPMorgan and its peers describe the two-year average in 2028 capital plans.

Divergence — transparency versus “studying for the exam”

What is disputed. Supervisors say public models improve accountability. Critics say banks will optimise to the published equations.

Why they differ. Disclosure and risk-sensitivity trade off; the Board is betting granularity plus a second market shock preserves bite.

What it means for you. Large-bank capital requirements should jump around less year to year. Whether that is safer or cosier depends on whether the published models still bite in a real recession — which only the next severely adverse scenario will show.

Sources: Fed Board press release, 30 Sep (read) · FR 2026-20247 · FR 2026-20246

Medicare’s GLOBE model benchmarks Part B rebates against nineteen peer countries

US politics & policy New subject Primary source

CMS’s final rule for the Global Benchmark for Efficient Drug Pricing (GLOBE) Model published today (FR Doc 2026-20281, effective 30 November). It is a mandatory section 1115A payment model that tests an alternative way to calculate Medicare Part B drug inflation rebates for certain clinician-administered single-source drugs and sole-source biologics, using international prices from a set of economically comparable countries as the benchmark. CMS’s 30 September press release: the model runs 1 January 2027 through 31 March 2032 (rebate invoicing through March 2034), covers a randomly selected geography holding about 25% of Original Medicare beneficiaries, and may cut those beneficiaries’ out-of-pocket costs beginning 1 April 2027.

Exclusions tightened after comment: biosimilars and their reference biologics once a US biosimilar launches; orphan-only drugs; plasma-derived products; certain cell and gene therapies. ASPE comparisons cited in the rule put US brand prices multiples above non-US OECD averages — the political premise for importing a peer-country benchmark into the inflation-rebate formula. Manufacturers in the selected geography do not get an opt-out from the model’s rebate math.

What to watch: which HCPCS codes clear the >$100 m spending screen; litigation over the international benchmark; and whether April 2027 OOP prints move for the 25% cohort.

A judge strips “integration” from Section 504 rules after HHS joined the states that sued it

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

In Texas v. Kennedy, Judge James W. Hendrix granted a joint request — plaintiff states plus HHS — to remove “most integrated setting” / community-integration language from HHS’s Section 504 implementing regulations. Stateline’s 2 October account: the suit began as a challenge to Biden-era inclusion of gender dysphoria under 504, then narrowed to five Republican-led states (Alaska, Florida, Louisiana, Montana, Texas) asking to vacate the integration provisions; in late August HHS joined them. A June DOJ OLC opinion had already argued that Section 504 and ADA Title II do not impose a freestanding state integration mandate, even while unjustified isolation can still be discrimination.

What the order does not do, advocates and DOJ both say in different tones: it does not overrule Olmstead v. L.C. (1999) or repeal the ADA. What it does do is remove the regulatory hook HHS and DOJ have used to push states toward community-based services, and it lands after DOJ stepped back from enforcement cases in Florida and Texas. Florida’s AG office told Stateline the state joined “to serve as a check on federal power, not to deny care.” Disability advocates say the right without a federal enforcer is a right you sue for yourself.

What to watch: whether private Olmstead suits fill the enforcement gap; any congressional resolution reaffirming Olmstead; and Florida’s own HCBS / institutional census under the new posture.

Sources: Stateline, 2 Oct (read) · NPR member-station / ACB summaries of the judgment · Florida named among remaining plaintiffs

Treasury publishes temporary regulations for the $1,700 federal scholarship tax credit

US debt & fiscal US politics & policy New subject Primary source Underreported

Treasury/IRS published temporary regulations (FR Doc 2026-20264, effective 1 December) and a companion proposed rule (2026-20277) implementing the nonrefundable federal income-tax credit under section 25F — added by the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act — for qualified cash contributions to state-certified scholarship-granting organisations. The credit is capped at $1,700 per taxpayer per year, applies to taxable years ending after 31 December 2026 (so first contributions in 2027, claimed on 2028 returns), and requires participating states to elect in and list eligible SGOs. IRS’s 1 October newsroom release frames it as the first nationwide federal school-choice tax-credit programme.

Claude Issue 28 recorded that an earlier full-text fetch of 2026-20264 returned a GovInfo “Page Not Found”; today’s API abstract and HTML full-text endpoint both resolved, so the operative dates and §25F hook are primary-sourced here. Dollar mechanics and state-election procedures are in the temporary regs; the proposed rule carries the fuller SGO qualification text for comment.

What to watch: which states elect in before the 2027 contribution year; whether the $1,700 cap is stacked with state credits or reduced by them (the statute reduces the federal credit by related state credits); and take-up versus deficit score once Joint Tax updates.

US financials SEC quorum rule now lets one commissioner act when everyone else is disqualified — effective today

FR Doc 2026-20262 (effective 2 October) amends 17 CFR §200.41. Three commissioners remain a quorum; if fewer than three are in office, those in office suffice; and if disqualifications leave only one commissioner able to participate in a matter, that one is a quorum for that matter. Adopted as an agency-management rule without notice and comment. Trade press ties the timing to Commissioner Peirce’s exit and a short-handed Commission — the rule text itself does not name her.
