The diesel question has an answer, and it was never about American refineries

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source Underreported

Yesterday this brief withdrew its second explanation for the diesel price in nine days and named three series it had never looked at: distillate demand, distillate exports, and the crack spread. All three were pulled from the EIA this morning, with the same-week comparison across five years that the standing rule now requires. Two of them close the question.

The crack spread — what a refiner gets for a barrel of diesel over what the crude cost — is $111.27. Taking New York Harbor ultra-low-sulphur spot at $5.201 a gallon, multiplying by 42 gallons, and subtracting West Texas Intermediate at $107.17. The average across the same late-August-to-October weeks was $19.35 in 2024, $36.53 in 2025, $48.64 in 2023, $75.23 in 2022 — and $108.13 this year.

The other two series rule themselves out. Demand is unremarkable: distillate product supplied averaged 3,823 thousand barrels a day across this year's window, which is lower than 2024 (3,919), 2023 (3,894) and 2022 (3,859), and barely above 2025 (3,784). Exports are not: 2026 has averaged 1,511 thousand barrels a day, the highest of the five years and about 11 per cent above the next highest, 2022's 1,366.

Put together with yesterday's finding, the picture is coherent for the first time. American refineries are running at a four-year high for the date. Inventories are flat. Domestic demand is slightly below normal. Exports are at a five-year high, and the margin on diesel is three times last year's. That is not a US supply constraint. It is an American refining industry running hard and selling into a world market that is paying extraordinarily well — and the domestic price has to match what a barrel can earn abroad.

One honest limit. The crack spread is itself a price, so "diesel is dear because the margin on diesel is wide" is close to circular. What the crack establishes is where in the chain the price is being set — not at the wellhead, and not by a bottleneck in American refining — and the export figure says which way the product is moving. Why the world is paying this much is a further question this brief has not answered, though the war in Iran is the obvious candidate and appeared in the cruise-ship story on Wednesday.

At the pump, both fuels fell again: diesel $6.3101, down 1.03 cents; regular $4.1837, down 2.09. The spread is $2.1264, a seventh consecutive record.

Confidence: Primary, and the strongest-evidenced story this brief has run on this thread. All four series were parsed from the EIA this morning and the five-year windows computed programmatically; the crack is this brief's own arithmetic on two published spot prices, using the standard 42-gallon conversion and no product yield weighting, which is a simplification a refiner would not use. The cause of the international margin has not been established. Sources: EIA NYH ULSD spot · EIA WTI spot · product supplied · exports · 30 October

Los Angeles election workers have been subpoenaed to appear the day after the election

Courts US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

The Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder's office has confirmed that its employees received grand jury subpoenas last week. At least two were served, with instructions to appear on 4 November — the day after the midterms.

This is the fourth day this brief has led on the Justice Department and election certification, and each day the instrument has escalated. A civil suit in California on 15 October. A suit in Alaska to withhold certification if the Republican loses. A request to Washington state. On Wednesday Judge David Carter refused the California request in terms — no legal basis, an unreliable list, a risk of disenfranchisement. The Department is appealing to the Ninth Circuit. And it has gone to a grand jury.

The letters that preceded the subpoenas are on the record and are the part worth reading closely. The Department told local election officials in California that they "risk significant federal scrutiny and legal exposure" if they refuse to set aside ballots belonging to people federal officials have flagged as likely noncitizens, and that "if persons knowingly tabulate ballots cast by individuals identified by federal agencies as noncitizens, they risk becoming aiders and abettors to a federal crime." That is a warning to county staff that counting a ballot could make them criminally liable, sent while they are counting ballots.

Whether the subpoenaed employees are targets is unclear; the Department says it generally does not subpoena targets. Its spokesperson, Kiersten Pels, declined to comment on "alleged grand jury subpoenas" but added that the judge himself had said unlawful voting carries criminal exposure, and that California's own filing said the remedy for unlawful voting is criminal prosecution.

That last point is fair as far as it goes and it is not the issue. Prosecuting a person who voted unlawfully is the ordinary remedy, and the judge said so. Subpoenaing the clerks who processed the ballots is a different act with a different effect, and the effect does not depend on anyone being charged.

What to watch: whether the Ninth Circuit rules before Tuesday; whether the 4 November appearances happen; and whether subpoenas issue in Alaska or Washington state.

Pharmacy benefit managers will have to tell employers what they are paid

Health Regulatory Continuation Primary document Underreported

Pharmacy benefit managers sit between drug makers, pharmacies and employers, and are paid from several directions at once — fees, spread, rebates retained. A Labor Department final rule loaded into Monday's Federal Register requires them to say so. Providers of pharmacy benefit management services, and affiliated brokerage and consulting firms, must disclose their direct and indirect compensation to the fiduciaries of large self-insured group health plans covered by ERISA.

The legal hook is the useful part. The disclosures are attached to ERISA section 408(b)(2), the statutory exemption that lets a plan pay a service provider at all without the arrangement being a prohibited transaction. That exemption is conditional on reasonable compensation, and a fiduciary cannot assess reasonableness without knowing the number. Tying disclosure to 408(b)(2) means a PBM that does not disclose is not merely non-compliant with a reporting rule — it risks the arrangement falling outside the exemption.

The Department's stated reason is cost: "Pharmacy spending is a major driver of overall healthcare costs for employers and is growing faster than medical and surgical spending." The rule implements section 12 of Executive Order 14273, "Lowering Drug Prices by Once Again Putting Americans First."

The limits are real and worth naming. It covers large, self-insured ERISA plans — so not small employers, not fully insured plans, not Medicaid managed care, and not the individual market. And it is a disclosure rule: it tells a plan sponsor what it is paying, and leaves the sponsor to do something about it. The theory is that sophisticated purchasers with information will negotiate; the counter-theory is that the three largest PBMs handle most of the market and there is nowhere else to go.

Timing: effective 4 January 2027, applicable to plan years beginning on or after 1 July 2027 — so most employers see the first disclosures in January 2028.

What to watch: whether PBMs challenge the 408(b)(2) linkage, which is the provision with teeth; whether any disclosure is made public rather than only to fiduciaries; and whether the pharmacy-benefit-manager rule in Medicare, whose comments close 15 December, adopts the same approach.

A second national security finding in four days, and again no tariff

Trade & supply chains Continuation Primary document Underreported

A proclamation signed on 28 October and loaded into Monday's edition finds that robotics, industrial machinery and their parts and components are being imported "in such quantities and under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security of the United States" under section 232. The reasoning is the conventional kind: nearly all modern defence systems and critical infrastructure depend on robotics or industrial machinery, which also underpin some $7.1 trillion of manufactured goods shipments in 2024.

And, as with wind turbines on Wednesday, it imposes nothing. The operative paragraphs direct the Commerce Secretary and the Trade Representative to pursue negotiations, to report within 180 days, and to keep monitoring. That is two section 232 threat findings in four days, neither carrying a duty.

This one is more explicit than the wind proclamation about what is being held in reserve. Depending on the negotiations, the President says he "may consider alternative remedies in the future, including imposing or modifying tariffs, duties, quotas, and fees; and establishing a program to incentivize new investment in United States production". The subsidy option named alongside the tariff option is new, and it rhymes with Wednesday's Defense Production Act determination on refining, which was a spending instrument rather than a trade one.

What the pattern looks like from outside. A completed section 232 investigation is a standing option: the finding is the slow part, and once made, the President can act without starting again. Two findings in four days, both parked, is an administration accumulating authority it has not yet used — which is a different posture from a tariff, and one that puts the counterparty on a clock rather than on a bill. The 180-day mark here falls around 26 April 2027.

What to watch: whether any agreement is announced before that date; whether the investment-incentive programme appears as a budget line or another DPA determination; and whether a third 232 finding lands without a duty, which would make this the standing method.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full — the absence of a tariff is a reading of the whole proclamation, not an inference from the summary. Scheduled for Monday 2 November and not yet published; read early from GovInfo. The Commerce Secretary's underlying report is not public. Sources: FR 2026-22385 · GovInfo PDF · 28 October

The FDA is reopening the nutrient standards for infant formula

Health Regulatory New subject Primary document

Infant formula is the only food that is, for many babies, the entire diet, and its composition is set by federal regulation rather than by the manufacturer. The FDA has proposed amending the minimum and maximum levels of four required nutrients, changing the unit of measure for certain nutrients, changing the permitted source for one nutrient requirement, and amending required labelling.

The proposal is part of what the agency calls a phased, comprehensive review of infant formula nutrients — the first systematic revisit of standards that have stood largely unchanged for decades. It also reports a comprehensive assessment of four further nutrients for which the FDA concludes the current required levels remain appropriate and reflect the best available evidence.

That second half deserves as much attention as the first, and will not get it. An agency publishing the finding that four standards it examined need no change is doing the same work as one proposing amendments, and it is the part that tells you the review is a review rather than a deregulatory exercise looking for things to loosen. Whether the four amended levels go up or down is the question this brief cannot answer, because the specifics sit in the regulatory text rather than the summary.

The comment period is long by the standards of the rules in this brief lately: comments close 2 March 2027, four months out, with the agency warning that late filings will not be considered. For comparison, USTR gave seven days on the Section 301 port fees and CMS gave 31 on the ACA verification rule.

What to watch: which four nutrients are being changed and in which direction; whether the permitted-source change favours a particular ingredient supplier, which is where this kind of rule usually has commercial consequences; and the later phases of the review.

Confidence: Primary document, summary and dates only. Scheduled for Monday 2 November and not yet published; read early from GovInfo. This brief did not read the regulatory text and therefore cannot name the four nutrients, the direction of any change, or the source substitution — which is most of what a parent or a manufacturer would want to know. The hump has broken out of the range it held all month

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Continuation Primary source

Friday's Treasury print: two-year 4.56%, down 5 basis points; ten-year 4.94, down 4; twenty-year 5.34, down 1; thirty-year 5.31, down 1. A second consecutive day of rallying, again led by the front end — the two-year has fallen 11 basis points in two sessions, from 4.67 on Wednesday.

The consequence is in the shape rather than the level. The twenty-minus-ten hump is +40 basis points. This brief has tracked it daily for a month and reported a range of +34 to +37 through a 17-point rally, a 7-point selloff and a 7-point rally; as recently as Thursday it was +37 and this brief called thirteen sessions inside three points the strongest case yet that the hump is structural. It is now three points outside that range, and the move came from the ten-year falling while the twenty-year barely moved.

Two readings, and this brief is not going to pick one on a single print — which is the lesson of the two withdrawn diesel mechanisms. Either the range was never as tight as thirteen sessions made it look, or something has changed in how the long end is being priced relative to the ten-year, four days before a quarterly refunding. A front end rallying this hard usually means expectations of lower policy rates; if the twenty-year will not follow, that is term premium being demanded at the very long end specifically.

What to watch: Wednesday's refunding size announcement, which is the event this whole month has been pointing at; whether the hump returns inside +37 on Monday, which would make Friday a print rather than a break; and what moved the two-year, which this brief still cannot source.

No commentary, no market participant and no macro release was consulted — the brief can see the curve move and cannot say why. Sources: Treasury Daily Par Yield Curve · 30 October

US politics ICE activity is surging before the midterms, and so is turnout.

Immigration enforcement activity is reported to be rising sharply in the final week before Tuesday's election, alongside rising early voting. The two facts are presented together because they are happening in the same places and, in the reporting, to overlapping communities.

The brief notes it without a causal claim in either direction. Enforcement surges could suppress turnout among people who fear contact with federal authorities, or provoke it among citizens in the same neighbourhoods; both effects are documented in the literature and they point opposite ways. Nothing available this morning distinguishes them, and turnout data by precinct will not exist until after Tuesday.

Below the line because this brief read a headline and a summary, not the reporting, and because the only honest version of the story is one that reports a correlation and declines to explain it — which, after this week, is a sentence this brief has earned the right to be careful about.

A federal judge has ordered an inquiry into potential Justice Department misconduct in the case over renovations at the Trump golf course in Washington — the matter this brief noted in passing on 26 October, when a judge halted the works and the President said they would start next month anyway.

An order of this kind is unusual. Courts generally resolve misconduct concerns through sanctions motions brought by the other side; a judge initiating an inquiry on the court's own motion means something in the record troubled the court enough to act without being asked. What that was is in the order.

Regulatory The DMCA exemption notice has been corrected by one word, and the word is "not".

On 28 October this brief reported the opening of the tenth triennial rulemaking on exemptions to the DMCA's anti-circumvention rules, noted that the Register intends to recommend all but one existing exemption for renewal, and said that identifying which one required reading past the summary.

Monday's edition carries a correction to that notice. The Copyright Office fixes "a typographical error within a subsection discussing a petition to renew a current exemption". The fix, in full: on page 67794, column 1, line 8, add the word "not" before the phrase "permitted by the exemption."

A single missing negative reversed the meaning of a sentence about the scope of an exemption someone has petitioned to renew. Whether that sentence is the one about the exemption being dropped, this brief cannot say without reading the passage, which it has still not done. But a one-word correction issued five days later, by the General Counsel, in a proceeding where the whole dispute is which conduct an exemption covers, is worth recording.

Below the line because it is a typographical correction, and because the thing that would make it a story is the same thing this brief failed to establish on Tuesday.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full — it is six sentences long. Sources: FR 2026-22274 · 28 October

Sanctions A sanctions power from this year's defence act has been handed to Treasury and State.

A presidential memorandum in Monday's edition delegates the President's authorities under section 8335 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2026, codified at 22 U.S.C. 10704. The structure: the Secretary of State identifies sanctionable foreign persons; the Secretary of the Treasury, consulting State, exercises the main authority under §8335(a); Treasury also takes §§8335(c)(1), (d)(1) and (f); State takes §8335(g); and agency heads implement within their own areas.

That is the standard architecture for a new sanctions programme — designation at State, blocking and licensing at Treasury — and delegation is the step that makes a statutory authority operable. Until a President delegates, a sanctions provision Congress passed is a power nobody is exercising.

Below the line because this brief did not read section 8335 and therefore cannot say who the programme targets, which is the entire point of it. The delegation tells you the machinery is being switched on; the statute tells you what it is aimed at, and that was not reached this morning. It goes on the owed list.

Confidence: Primary document, read in full; underlying statute not read. Sources: FR 2026-22386
