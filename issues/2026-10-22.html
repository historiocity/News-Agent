Distillate stocks went back up, and yesterday's tidy explanation for diesel does not hold

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

What this brief said yesterday. That diesel's weekly rise had an ordinary mechanism — refinery maintenance cut runs, distillate drew down to 102.6 million barrels, and the price of the product drawing hardest turned up. It was called "a complete and ordinary explanation" and the thing to watch was named as whether the draw continued.

It did not. US distillate stocks for the week ending 16 October are 104.3 million barrels, a build of 1.7 million on the week. The four-week slide from the 11 September peak of 107.9 million has stopped and partly reversed. Refinery utilisation is 93.4 per cent, unchanged from 93.5 — so runs did not recover to explain the build either.

Two honest readings remain and this brief cannot choose between them. The first is timing: the price week runs to 19 October and the stocks week to the 16th, so the series are three days out of step and a build at the end of one week is compatible with a price rise in the next. The second is that the stock draw was never the cause — that it coincided with the price turn without driving it, and that something this brief has not measured did.

What is not in doubt is the price. AAA this morning: diesel $6.3090, down 0.84 cents; regular petrol $4.2701, up 0.57 cents and rising for the first time in a week. The premium has narrowed to $2.0389 from $2.0530. On the weekly series, diesel at $6.276 is still 25 cents below the 21 September record.

The lesson is the one this brief keeps relearning about this thread. Two consecutive weekly observations are not a mechanism, and the sentence "a complete and ordinary explanation" should not have been written on a series that had been readable here for exactly one day.

What to watch: the week-ending-23-October stocks figure, which will say whether the build was a one-week restock or a turn; utilisation, which has been flat for three weeks when maintenance season should be ending; and whether the premium keeps narrowing now that petrol has stopped falling.

A New Mexico judge has ordered Meta to pay $25bn, five times the federal fine

US financials AI & tech industry New thread Independent corroboration

State District Judge Francis Mathew ordered Meta on Wednesday to pay New Mexico $25bn, after a jury found the company misled Facebook users about who could reach their personal data. The judgment covers more than 43 million violations of the state's consumer protection laws. "Facebook promised users that they controlled their data," the judge wrote. "This was false."

The scale is the first thing to hold. The Federal Trade Commission's 2019 Facebook settlement — at the time the largest privacy penalty in American history — was $5bn. This is five times that, awarded by one state court in a state of about 2.1 million people. Attorney General Raúl Torrez called it one of the largest state verdicts against a company in US history and said the money goes into a fund for New Mexico's education system.

The second thing is how it was reached, because it explains both the number and its fragility. The penalty is per violation under state consumer protection law: 43 million violations multiplied by a statutory amount. That arithmetic produces very large numbers from ordinary per-count penalties, and it is why Meta's lawyer Matt Nicholson told the court a figure as high as $40bn would be "shocking to the conscience".

Nicholson's other argument is the appellate one and it should be stated plainly rather than dismissed: "The jury didn't make findings that any New Mexican heard or saw these statements, or was harmed by them." A judgment of this size resting on counted violations rather than demonstrated harm is the kind that gets reduced on review — the question an appeal will put is whether a penalty untethered from proven injury is constitutionally excessive.

What to watch: whether Meta appeals and on what ground, which is near-certain and determines whether $25bn is ever paid; whether other state attorneys general file on the same theory, since the per-violation arithmetic travels; and whether the figure survives a remittitur in the trial court before it ever reaches an appellate bench.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Not a factual dispute — the jury's findings stand — but a disagreement about what a penalty is for. New Mexico's position, through counsel Randi McGinn, is that the point is to stand up to companies that believe they are untouchable; the deterrent has to be large enough to register against Meta's revenue. Meta's position is that a penalty must be proportionate to harm shown, and no New Mexican was shown to have seen the statements or been injured by them.

The cause. Both are reading the same statute from opposite ends. A per-violation consumer protection penalty is designed to work where individual harm is small and diffuse and would never be worth suing over — which is exactly privacy. The state is using the statute as written. Meta is invoking the constitutional backstop on excessive penalties, which exists because per-count arithmetic can reach numbers no legislature contemplated. Neither is being unreasonable about the law; they disagree about which safeguard controls.

What it means for you. Treat $25bn as the opening figure, not the outcome. The headline number is real and the judgment is entered, but very large per-violation awards are routinely reduced, and the absence of proven individual harm is the strongest reduction argument available. What would settle it is the appellate ruling, and the thing to watch before that is whether any other state files on this theory — because a second case would make the arithmetic a strategy rather than one state's verdict.

This brief did not read the judgment, the jury verdict form or the statutory per-violation amount, so the arithmetic above is described rather than checked, and the $5bn FTC comparison is context. Rubio says the satellite cutoff was Musk's call; Musk said it was Washington's

Geopolitics AI & tech industry Continuation Independent corroboration

This brief has run the Starshield throttling four times in eight days and each time said the same thing: everyone agrees it happened, nobody will own the decision, and no document exists. Yesterday the Secretary of State closed the loop.

Marco Rubio said on Wednesday that decisions about Ukraine's access to Starlink rest with SpaceX and Elon Musk, not the White House. On 14 October, Musk said the speed limits on Ukraine's Starshield terminals are set by the US military, including the President, and not by his company. Each of the two parties has now publicly pointed at the other.

The underlying facts are no longer in dispute and are worth restating with the names attached. Zelenskyy adviser Serhii Beskrestnov wrote on Telegram on 14 October that SpaceX had imposed a limit of 160 kilometres per hour — 100mph — on Starshield, which disabled guidance for the drones Ukraine uses to intercept faster Russian drones. "Our defence forces received no prior warning regarding this restriction, and it has led to the disruption of a number of missions."

Two newsrooms have now reported the decision chain and they differ in an interesting place. The Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday that Musk ordered the slowdown, with one source saying Musk, the President and White House officials discussed it beforehand and two saying Musk and the President discussed it afterwards. Reuters has two US officials and a Western official saying the decision came from the White House. Zelenskyy has endorsed the Reuters version; the administration denies it.

What to watch: whether any member of Congress compels an answer, since the Pentagon has paid for Starshield since 2024 and a contract exists somewhere; and whether Rubio's statement is repeated in a form that can be tested — a written assertion that no instruction was given would be a far more consequential document than anything produced so far.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. A clean factual dispute about who decided, with both candidate deciders denying it. Musk: the US military and the President set the limits. Rubio: the decision belongs to SpaceX and Musk. The WSJ's sourcing puts the order with Musk after consultation; Reuters' puts it with the White House. The two reports are closer than the two denials — both describe Musk and the President discussing it — and the gap between them is whether consultation amounted to instruction.

The cause. This is what a decision with no paper looks like when it becomes costly. A government that instructed a contractor to degrade an ally's defence would own a policy it has not announced; a company that did so on its own would own a commercial decision with battlefield consequences. Both have a clear interest in the other having decided, and that is sufficient to explain the pattern without anyone lying — a conversation in which a company is told what Washington would prefer can be honestly described from one side as the government's decision and from the other as the company's.

What it means for you. Take the throttling and its effect as established, and treat the attribution as unresolved in a way that now looks deliberate. The fact that two officials with every incentive to deny responsibility have each named the other is itself the finding: nobody is prepared to defend this decision as policy. What would settle it is a contract instruction, a congressional letter with an answer, or a written denial from either side — and after nine days, none has appeared.

The de minimis machinery for mail has slipped three months, to 20 January

World economy US politics & policy Continuation Underreported Primary source

A Customs and Border Protection rule published this morning carries a title about indefinite suspension and does something narrower. The compliance date for 19 CFR 145.12(a)(2)(v) and (vi) moves to 20 January 2027.

What those provisions require is formal customs entry for goods arriving through the international post that are either subject to another agency's requirements — food, drugs, devices, consumer products, anything FDA or CPSC or USDA regulates at the border — or dutiable under Chapters 98 or 99 of the tariff schedule. Formal entry means a filing, a bond and a broker. Applying it to parcels in the mail is the hard part of ending de minimis, and it is the part that has just been put off for three months.

The suspension of duty-free de minimis itself is untouched and stands. CBP grounds it in Executive Order 14324 of 30 July 2025 (90 FR 37775), as modified by Executive Order 14388 of 20 February 2026 (91 FR 9433), and independently in its own authority under section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930 as amended. Informal entry — the lighter procedure — remains available under 19 U.S.C. 1498(a)(1)(A) for shipments valued at $2,500 or less.

Read with the Postal Service rule this brief led on five days ago, the shape of the thing is now visible. USPS has already told mailers they owe destination customs charges "regardless of whether the item is delivered, lost, damaged, or returned", effective Monday. CBP has now given the inbound side until January. The obligations on American senders arrived first; the processing capacity for American receivers is three months behind.

What to watch: whether 20 January holds or slips again, which this is the second extension to suggest; what the "new postal informal entry process" in the title actually consists of, which the summary does not describe; and the 7 December close of comments on the wider de minimis proposal.

Confidence: Primary source. The compliance date, the regulatory citations, the executive orders and the statutory authorities were read from the rule itself on GovInfo. The rule runs to several pages this brief read in part: the new informal entry process named in the title was not read, and no estimate of affected volumes was extracted. An executive order creates a women's health division inside NIH and a task force above it

Science & public health US politics & policy New thread Primary source

The one presidential document in today's issue, Advancing Women's Health To Improve Outcomes in All Phases of Life, does two institutional things.

First, it directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services, through the NIH Director, to establish a Women's Health Division within an appropriate institute or centre within 90 days, working with the existing NIH Office of Research on Women's Health. Its list of under-researched conditions is long and specific: cardiovascular disease, cancer, stroke, Alzheimer's, autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, osteoporosis, mental health, chronic pain, endometriosis, fibroids, polycystic ovary syndrome, infertility, maternal health, menopause, neurological disorders and ageing.

Second, it creates a White House Women's Health Task Force, co-chaired by the HHS Secretary and the Assistant to the President and Chief of Staff, with eleven named members: the Secretaries of War, Agriculture, Labor, Education and Veterans Affairs; the EPA Administrator; the Commissioner of Food and Drugs; and the directors of the CDC, NIH, ARPA-H and CMS. The co-chairs may hold hearings and take outside input, and HHS pays the administrative costs.

The sceptical reading is about money. The order creates a division and a committee; it appropriates nothing. A new NIH division funded from an existing institute's budget competes with that institute's grants, and "HHS shall provide funding for the administrative costs of the Task Force" is a sentence about staff, not research. The instrument can reorganise attention and reporting lines; it cannot add a dollar.

One detail for a different reason. The member list names the Secretary of War — the second documented appearance of that title in three days, after the arms notifications of 19 October styled the department "the Department of War (DoW)". An executive order's roll of cabinet officers is about as settled a usage as the government produces.

What to watch: which institute or centre the division lands in, because that determines whose budget pays for it; whether the 90-day deadline is met, which falls around 20 January; and whether any appropriations request follows.

US politics & policy AIPAC has stopped endorsing candidates and making direct contributions.

AIPAC has halted endorsements and direct contributions to candidates, twelve days before the midterms. For an organisation whose influence has been exercised substantially through exactly those two instruments, that is a structural change rather than a tactical one.

What it does not necessarily mean is a withdrawal from politics. Endorsements and direct contributions are the visible, reportable half of political spending; independent expenditure through affiliated committees is the other half and is not covered by the phrase as reported. Whether this is a retreat or a shift from disclosed to less-disclosed channels is the question, and the reporting available here does not answer it.

No reason was reported, no duration was given, and this brief did not reach AIPAC's own statement or any Federal Election Commission filing.
