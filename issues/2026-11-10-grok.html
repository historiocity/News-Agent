Waller says he will vote to raise rates in December even if this morning's inflation print is good, and for half a point if it is not

Macro & markets New subject Primary source

Fed Governor Christopher Waller, speaking in London hours before the October consumer price index (8:30 a.m. ET today), set out his vote in advance. If October inflation improves on September, as he expects, he says he will still back a 25 basis-point increase at the 8–9 December FOMC meeting. If core CPI comes in at around 0.3 per cent or higher, like the previous two months, he says a 50 basis-point move should be considered, because hiking only a quarter point "would put our credibility at risk."

This is the speech text on the Board's own site, not a wire paraphrase. Its reasoning matters more than the headline. Waller says the Fed's earlier signalling of hikes already tightened markets, so the hikes now have to be delivered or rates will fall in a way that loosens policy. He calls September's inflation data plainly bad: PCE up 0.5 per cent on the month and 3.7 per cent on the year, core PCE 3.1 per cent. He says more than 70 per cent of core services components were rising faster than 3 per cent.

Two of his inputs run straight through this brief's other leads. He cites reports that Hormuz insurance and ship-to-ship transfers are adding $20–30 a barrel, which a settlement would remove. He also cites a Fed staff note published 9 November finding that oil shocks now pass into core inflation more persistently than before 2020. And he names high-tech goods prices tied to the AI build-out as something he is watching in today's print.

What to watch: core CPI month-on-month against Waller's 0.3 per cent line. BLS will publish no year-on-year rate this month, because October 2025 was never collected during the shutdown. Futures had about an 85 per cent chance of a December hike priced at Monday's close, per Reuters. PPI follows Friday.

Divergence — how much did Waller move the odds of a half-point hike?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. American Banker reads the speech as all but locking in a December hike. Reuters leads on the quarter point, with the half point as a conditional. FXStreet's desk says futures put no weight on 50 basis points before he spoke, and that he made today's CPI a choice between 25 and 50.

Why they differ. The audiences differ. Reuters writes for a general wire and leads on the base case. American Banker writes for bankers pricing deposits and loans, so a near-certain hike is their headline. FXStreet writes for traders, for whom the news is the change in the tail. They agree on every quoted sentence, and they published within the same hour.

What it means for you. Read it as one governor, a well-known hawk on this cycle, saying where he stands. It is not a committee forecast. American Banker notes two members already dissented for a hike in October. The odds that matter are the ones in futures after 8:30. If core prints 0.3 or higher, a 50 basis-point December move becomes a live question for the first time. At 0.2 it goes back to being one governor's conditional.

One Federal Register issue carries three rules tightening food aid and jobless benefits, and USDA's own estimate is 6.3 million people losing SNAP

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Primary source

Today's Federal Register carries three proposed rules that together tighten access to the two main federal income supports for people out of work or on low pay.

SNAP categorical eligibility (FR 2026-22936). USDA would end "broad-based" categorical eligibility. Today a household can qualify for SNAP automatically by receiving any TANF-funded benefit, including non-cash services. Under the proposal, only households receiving TANF assistance as defined at 45 CFR 260.31 would qualify that way. USDA's own regulatory impact analysis estimates about $44.6 billion less in federal SNAP benefits over fiscal 2027–31. It puts the effect at 3.2 million households containing 6.3 million people in fiscal 2029, the first full year. School districts would have to process about 197,000 more school-meal applications, because SNAP enrolment currently qualifies children automatically. USDA tried a narrower version of this in July 2019. Comments close 10 December.

SNAP work-requirement waivers (FR 2026-22944). This rule codifies the 2025 reconciliation act's changes to the three-month time limit for adults without dependants. Outside Alaska and Hawaii, an area now qualifies for a waiver only if unemployment is above 10 per cent. USDA would also tighten the data needed to request a waiver and rewrite the definition of a homeless individual. Comments close 10 December.

Unemployment insurance (FR 2026-22903). Labor would set a national floor of five qualifying work-search activities per benefit week. Claimants would have to report them before being paid. States could no longer issue a warning instead of finding a claimant ineligible. Comments close 11 January 2027.

What to watch: the comment dockets, the state response (House Agriculture Democrats say 46 states use broad-based eligibility), and whether the incoming Democratic House tries an appropriations rider. None of the three is final, and the rules would land after the new Congress is seated.

Divergence — "targeting" or "kicking off": the same 6.3 million

What is disputed. Significance, not the number. Both sides use USDA's figure. USDA says the rule targets benefits to households that actually qualified for TANF, and notes that SNAP reached a record of nearly 45 million participants as broad-based eligibility spread. Rep. Angie Craig, ranking Democrat on House Agriculture, calls it an effort to "kick an additional 6.3 million Americans off SNAP." She says 5.5 million have already left SNAP since the 2025 act.

Why they differ. Each is describing a different group inside the same 6.3 million. USDA's own text shows that many households qualifying only through broad-based eligibility have resources or incomes above the federal SNAP limits. Those are the households the rule is aimed at. But the same rule also removes automatic eligibility for people who would pass the federal test and now face new paperwork. USDA's burden section counts 73.9 per cent of affected households as facing that paperwork. Craig's 5.5 million figure is her committee's, and I could not check it against a USDA series.

What it means for you. Take the 6.3 million as the government's own estimate of people who stop receiving SNAP. It is not an opponent's projection. The open question is the split between people above the federal limits and eligible people lost to paperwork, and USDA does not publish that split. It would show up in the final rule's revised impact analysis, or in state-level estimates filed during the comment period.

Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow at $70–78 a share, a smaller deal than the draft it pulled back from bankers last night

AI & tech industry Follow-up Primary source

Anthropic announced this morning that it has started marketing its initial public offering. The deal is 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78: 801.35 million sold by the company and 23.65 million by existing holders. The underwriters get a 30-day option on 123.75 million more. It intends to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market as "ANT", with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley named for the prospectus. At the top of the range, the base deal raises about $64.4 billion, of which about $62.5 billion goes to the company. If the underwriters take the full option, the total is about $74 billion. Handelsblatt led its front page on the $64 billion figure.

The launch followed a mishap. Late Monday, Reuters reported that an Anthropic representative had sent banks a filing showing 866.7 million shares at $70–80, or $69.3 billion at the top. The representative then followed up to say the pricing was inaccurate and an updated filing would follow. The same draft described a $26 billion private placement of Class N shares alongside the IPO, from Alphabet, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC. Today's announcement does not mention the placement, and I have not confirmed it in the amended S-1, because sec.gov refused this run's fetch.

Why it matters here: this is the largest test yet of whether public markets will fund frontier-model losses at the scale the labs plan to spend. The Times of India's summary of the S-1 gives first-half 2026 revenue of $16.3 billion and a net loss of $80.7 billion. Most of the loss is fair-value accounting on financing instruments, not cash burn. Bloomberg reports investors discussing $1.1–1.3 trillion, below earlier talk of up to $2 trillion, after last week's AI sell-off.

What to watch: pricing, expected next week; whether the private placement survives in the final prospectus; and whether the range moves during the roadshow.

Divergence — $69.3 billion or $64.4 billion?

What is disputed. Facts, and they have been settled. Reuters' Monday-night figure came from 866.7 million shares at up to $80. Today's company announcement is 825 million at up to $78.

Why they differ. Timing, and nothing else. Reuters accurately reported a draft that was circulated and then retracted. Bloomberg Law calls the retracted number outdated. Anthropic's own release, published hours later, is the document of record. Reuters said it could not determine which details were wrong, and the gap now answers that: both the share count and the top of the range came down.

What it means for you. Use $64.4 billion at the top of the base deal. Treat any valuation figure as investor talk until the deal prices. A smaller deal at a lower top price fits the reporting that investors pushed back on valuation after the sell-off. The final price and share count next week will settle it.

Commerce investigators stepped back from the Megaspeed chip-smuggling case after Nvidia's top lawyer contacted them, and the FBI is now looking at Huang

AI & tech industry Follow-up Independent corroboration

The Nvidia story moved in three directions overnight.

The export-control case. The New York Times reported, with NBC independently confirming the core, that in August 2025 Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, contacted Bureau of Industry and Security agents investigating Megaspeed. Megaspeed is the Singapore data-centre company suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China. Teter told the agents that Under Secretary Jeff Kessler had approved the contact. The agents declined to meet and raised concerns internally. The Justice Department then asked Commerce to pause its part of the case, and DOJ is now running a criminal investigation. A Commerce official told the Times that Kessler told staff to handle the case by the book. Nothing reported so far shows Nvidia changed the outcome. But a politically appointed export-control chief whose name was invoked in a live enforcement case is now a possible subject himself.

The FBI. At a White House briefing Monday, Trump said "I know the FBI is looking into" Jensen Huang's relationship with Alice Huang, the former Megaspeed executive US officials believe worked for Beijing. He said Huang told him he had no idea she was a spy, and that he believes him. Beijing called the allegations groundless.

The lawsuit. Shareholder Mark Kistenmacher filed a proposed securities class action in federal court in San Jose on Monday under Rule 10b-5. It covers buyers from 9 October to 8 November. It alleges that Nvidia's October statements clearing Megaspeed were misleading given the CEO's relationship.

What to watch: the 18 November congressional date for Huang reported in yesterday's edition. Also whether he travels to APEC with Trump: SCMP reports his attendance is in doubt. Watch, too, any move to put Megaspeed on the Entity List, which the Times says other agencies proposed and Commerce did not act on.

Divergence — what Nvidia's lawyer asked for, and what Monday's fall cost

What is disputed. Two facts. First, the contact itself. The Times, relayed by Export Compliance Daily and Investing.com, describes an email to the agents asking for a meeting and citing Kessler's permission. Bloomberg describes a call to Kessler asking whether BIS had a hand in Singapore's detention of a Megaspeed executive. Second, the market loss. Courthouse News gives a 5 per cent fall and $192 billion. The BBC gives "more than 4 per cent" and about $250 billion.

Why they differ. The contact accounts probably describe two steps in one sequence, a call to the under secretary and then an email to his agents citing it, each sourced from a different side. Bloomberg's sources sound closer to Nvidia's account, the Times' closer to the agents'. On the stock, one outlet is using the close and the other an intraday or differently based market value. They cannot both be right: a 4–5 per cent move on a company of roughly $4.5 trillion is about $180–225 billion.

What it means for you. Treat the call and the email as both likely to be true. The email is the more consequential, because it is what the agents escalated. On the money, use "about $200 billion", not $250 billion. A congressional or inspector-general request for the Teter–Kessler records would settle the first question; Monday's official closing price settles the second.

A Republican has forced a House vote on impeaching the Defense Secretary over the Iran war, due within two legislative days

US politics & policy Follow-up Independent corroboration

Rep. Thomas Massie, a Kentucky Republican, introduced a privileged resolution on the House floor Monday with eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. CBS describes the text as 34 pages. Three articles charge him with waging war on Iran without congressional authorisation, in violation of the 1973 War Powers Resolution. Others cite disregard for civilian protections, including a strike on an Iranian school, the Caribbean boat strikes, the capture of Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro, and the Pentagon's pursuit of Sen. Mark Kelly over a video urging troops to refuse illegal orders.

The mechanism is what makes this news rather than noise. A privileged resolution forces the Speaker to schedule action within two legislative days. Roll Call says that puts a vote as early as Wednesday. Speaker Mike Johnson must first rule on whether it is privileged; precedent favours treating impeachment that way. The House can then vote on the articles, or on a motion to table them or refer them to committee. A tabling vote is the likeliest form, and it would put every member on record on the Iran war before the new Congress is seated. Massie first filed in September, and leadership recessed the House early rather than vote.

Trump said he would stand by Hegseth even if he is impeached. Hegseth scheduled a rare Pentagon briefing for this morning. Conviction would need two-thirds of the Senate.

Yesterday's edition listed impeachment as an agenda item, after Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said he was a "hard yes." What has changed is that a Republican filed it, and the clock has started.

What to watch: whether Johnson rules it privileged; whether the vote is on tabling or on the articles; how many Republicans cross; and whether outgoing members who lost last week turn up, which Roll Call flags as a factor in the count.

The Supreme Court restarted the Big Bend border wall and held there is no such thing as a "constitutional" major-questions claim

US politics & policy · courts New subject Primary source

In Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church (No. 26A560), decided Monday in an unsigned four-page order, the Court stayed a West Texas district court injunction. That injunction had halted border-barrier construction in what Customs and Border Protection calls the Big Bend Sector. Justice Jackson noted she would deny the stay. The stay lasts while the government seeks review and ends automatically if certiorari is denied.

The holding reaches further than one stretch of wall. Section 102 of the 1996 immigration act lets the Homeland Security secretary waive "all legal requirements" to build barriers quickly. It also bars courts from hearing anything except constitutional claims. Judge Kathleen Cardone got around that bar by treating the major-questions doctrine as a constitutional rule grounded in the separation of powers. The Court rejected the move outright. It says major questions is a canon of statutory interpretation, and "There is no such thing" as a constitutional major-questions claim. It said the government is very likely to win on jurisdiction.

The practical effect is that the waiver power is close to unreviewable again. Where Congress has limited review to constitutional claims, plaintiffs cannot recast a statutory-authority argument as a constitutional one to get into court. Expect that line to be cited well beyond immigration.

What to watch: the cert petition; whether construction resumes inside Big Bend National Park itself; and whether the new Congress tries to amend the Section 102 waiver.

Divergence — is the wall going into the national park?

What is disputed. Facts. Al Jazeera's headline says the Court allowed the wall "in Big Bend National Park." NPR and NBC say West Texas or "the Big Bend area." The order itself never mentions the park; it speaks of the Big Bend Sector, a Border Patrol unit.

Why they differ. The sector and the park are different geographies with the same name. SCOTUSblog, which is closest to the filings, says the waiver region "includes parts of" the national park. Headlines compress that to the park, which is the more vivid noun.

What it means for you. The accurate statement is that construction may resume across a Border Patrol sector that includes parts of the park. Whether barrier segments actually run through park land depends on the alignment in the waiver notices (91 FR 7298 and later amendments), not on this order. Those maps, or DHS's construction schedule, will settle it.

Pinellas has arrested a third deputy for misusing licence-plate cameras, and the State Attorney says Florida law may not reach what one of them did

Florida & Tampa Bay New subject Independent corroboration

Former Pinellas deputy Ronald Scott resigned and was arrested on 5 November on four felony counts, WTSP reports: three of unauthorised computer access and one of official misconduct. He is accused of running more than 100 searches on the Flock automated licence-plate-reader system and other law-enforcement databases. The searches, from February to about June, tracked his estranged wife, her boyfriend and others. He is the third Pinellas deputy charged over Flock misuse this autumn.

The local consequence is a gap in state law, not one officer. Pinellas–Pasco State Attorney Bruce Bartlett dropped charges against an earlier deputy, Karl Gwynne. Gwynne was accused of about 300 searches to track a woman he had arrested and hoped to date. Bartlett wrote in a court filing that Florida statutes "do not address the misconduct reported." A third former deputy, Travis Stanton, still faces a harassment charge for allegedly using the system to find a 17-year-old. Sheriff Bob Gualtieri acknowledged disagreements with Bartlett over the statute's elements. Bartlett's circuit covers both Pinellas and Pasco, so the gap applies to New Port Richey too.

How Scott was caught matters as well. Gualtieri said the misuse surfaced only through a new, more thorough audit tool from Flock. Gwynne's case surfaced after a TCPalm reporter's inquiry prompted an audit. The sheriff, who leads a national pro-ALPR campaign, says the technology is not the problem.

What to watch: a bill for the 2027 session that makes personal misuse of ALPR data a crime in its own right; whether Pasco's sheriff's office (now under Jeff Harrington, appointed after Chris Nocco resigned) publishes its own audit; and whether the Scott charges survive the same statutory challenge.

Divergence — is this a few bad deputies, or an oversight failure?

What is disputed. Significance. Gualtieri told reporters he is confident all previous misuse has now been caught, and that critics who blame ALPR are wrong. Creative Loafing and WUSF's reporting shows two of the three cases were found only after outside pressure or a new vendor tool, months after the searches.

Why they differ. Vantage and interest. The sheriff runs the system and campaigns for it nationally, so he has every reason to frame each case as individual misconduct the system caught. The reporters are counting how it was caught, and the answer is late and partly by accident. There is also a minor spelling conflict: WUSF has "Gwynee" in one place, and Creative Loafing has "Gwynne". I use the latter.

What it means for you. The evidence supports both a real deterrent, since arrests are happening, and a detection system that until recently could not find this. The confidence that "all" past misuse is caught rests on an audit tool only weeks old. Gualtieri said the earlier audits were less comprehensive. A re-run of the new tool over the full retention history, with the number of flagged queries published, would settle it.
