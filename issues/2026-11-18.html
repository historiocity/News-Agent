The NSA's own classified network is down, and the cause given is a security patch

US politics & policy AI & tech industry New subject Single source

NSANet, the classified network the National Security Agency uses for its own internal communications, is down, according to six people familiar with the matter. The agency closed its campus to most employees on Tuesday night and reverted to essential personnel only while it works to restore it — hours after telling staff they could return to work as normal, and after staggering arrivals earlier in the day. Sources say there is no evidence of a cyberattack.

The agency's public account and its director of national intelligence's account are not the same account. NSA said it “experienced temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance”, that mission-critical capabilities remain fully operational, and that its infrastructure has multiple layers of redundancy. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, on Fox Business, said the outage was the result of the agency applying a software patch for major vulnerabilities discovered last month, and that the patching caused systems not to come back up as expected.

The vulnerabilities are the part that reaches everybody. They are in PHP, the language that powers roughly 70% of the world's websites. AI tools helped researchers exploit them, which set off a global patching scramble by governments and companies over recent weeks — a scramble the NSA itself urged. So the chain runs: a widely used language, flaws made exploitable faster with AI assistance, an emergency patch cycle, and systems that would not restart.

This is the fifth significant outage in eight days, after Microsoft, the FAA ground stop, United Airlines and Chase. On Monday this brief declined to call that a trend because the four had nothing technical in common. Three of those four, and now this one, were caused by planned changes rather than by attackers.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What happened at Fort Meade. The NSA says temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance, with mission-critical capability intact. Six people say the network is down, the campus was emptied to essential staff, and restoration is incomplete. A dispute about facts, and the two accounts are hard to hold together: an agency does not send its workforce home over temporary degradation.

Cause. Reach for the mundane first, and here the mundane reading is the DNI's, not the agency's. A patch applied in a hurry to a classified estate that cannot be tested against the public internet is exactly the kind of change that breaks on restart, and the sequence — staggered arrivals, then all-clear, then reversal — is what an unsuccessful restoration looks like from the outside. The gap between the two statements is then about audience rather than truth: a signals intelligence agency has strong reasons to describe any interruption to its own communications as routine, whatever it is, and the DNI was being asked a technical question on television. Note that the two official accounts are not consistent with each other either: “scheduled routine maintenance” and “an emergency patch for vulnerabilities found last month” are different events.

What it means for you. The reassuring reading and the alarming reading point at the same remedy. If this is a patch gone wrong, then the agency that tells the country to patch has demonstrated how expensive patching is at scale, and every bank, hospital and utility now patching the same PHP flaws should expect their own restarts to fail. If it is something else, the public will learn that late. Believe that NSANet is down, that the cause offered is a patch, and that no evidence of an attack has emerged. What would settle it: how long restoration takes; whether CISA issues an advisory on the patch itself rather than the vulnerability; whether the congressional intelligence committees are briefed and say so; and whether any other agency reports the same failure mode. cisa.gov returned 403 to this brief this morning, so the one source that would answer the second question could not be read.

What to watch: when NSANet is restored and whether the campus reopens; any CISA advisory on patch-induced failures; the intelligence committees; whether other agencies report the same; and whether the no-cyberattack assessment holds.

The FAA has stopped installing the routers, and its administrator says its own technician was watching

US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

The FAA is pausing installation of new Juniper telecommunications routers while it investigates Friday's nationwide ground stop, an agency spokesperson confirmed after the Wall Street Journal reported it. Investigators are still working out whether the failure came from defective equipment or from an installation or configuration error.

The manufacturer's parent has already answered. A spokesperson for HPE said that “our equipment was working as configured” on what the company knows and has been told, and that the FAA is assessing the configuration and performance of all the infrastructure involved.

And the agency's own administrator has undercut the department's preferred account. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has spent four days pointing at the contractor L3Harris and at Congress. On Capitol Hill on Tuesday, FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the L3Harris engineer doing the work — a “highly trained certified engineer” — was “monitored by an FAA technician” during the installation. If an FAA technician was standing there, the agency cannot locate the error entirely outside itself.

This is the second consecutive day on which the FAA story has produced a hard fact that the funding argument did not want: on Monday it was the Senate majority leader saying about $8 billion of the last tranche is unspent, and today it is the agency's own presence at the failure.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whose hands were on the failure. Duffy: a contractor doing a copper-to-fibre cutover. HPE: the equipment worked as configured. Bedford: an FAA technician was monitoring the contractor's certified engineer. A dispute about facts, and the three statements are compatible in exactly one arrangement — a configuration decision, made or approved on site, with the agency present.

Cause. Interest explains the shape of each statement without anyone lying. A vendor facing a pause on its product line says the hardware did what it was told. A secretary seeking $50 billion needs the failure to be about an old system and somebody else's engineer. An administrator testifying under oath to a committee that will subpoena him in January has the narrowest incentive of the three, which is why his sentence is the most useful one. Note what the pause itself concedes: you do not stop installing a vendor's routers nationwide if you are confident the fault was purely human.

What it means for you. The pause is the fact with consequences, because it stops the modernisation that the $50 billion request is premised on. An agency asking for money to replace copper with fibre has just suspended the equipment programme that does the replacing, which means the schedule Duffy gave on Sunday — everything finished by the first part of 2029 — is already out of date. What would settle the blame question: the configuration file and the change record for the El Dorado installation, both of which exist; the FAA's preliminary incident report; and whether the Juniper pause is lifted, which is the agency telling you whether it blames the box.

What to watch: whether the Juniper pause is lifted and when; the preliminary incident report; whether the FBI's involvement produces anything; the effect of the pause on the 2029 schedule; and Thanksgiving week operations.

The strait is being kept open by smaller ships, and the empty ones are running out

Energy & grid Geopolitics Continuation Independent corroboration

US fighter jets struck an Iranian missile launch site on Qeshm Island on Tuesday with precision munitions, in what an American military official called a self-defence response to IRGC aggression against American personnel and assets. Iranian state media reported an enemy attack on the outskirts of a city on Qeshm; Hormozgan's deputy governor for political and security affairs, Ahmad Nafisi, said no vital infrastructure, commercial or residential areas were damaged. Kuwait's air defences responded to hostile drone attacks early Wednesday, origin unstated.

A ship burned the same day and a man is missing. ADNOC L&S said one crew member is unaccounted for and eleven were injured after one of its vessels was attacked in the strait on Tuesday. Oman's emergency authorities say they rescued 24 crew from the Panama-flagged LPG tanker Tara Gas and were still fighting the fire.

The traffic numbers explain how exports have recovered anyway, and they are the finding. Volume moved through Hormuz by Suezmaxes and Aframaxes hit a wartime high of more than 2 million barrels a day in October, on Vortexa's figures, lifting total daily exports to 11.4 million barrels, the highest since the war began. Very large crude carriers are still 72% of transits on BRS Shipbrokers' count — and they are what Iran is hitting, so smaller hulls are taking up the work, with some ships refusing the strait entirely and loading from shuttle tankers in the Gulf of Oman.

The constraint is now arriving from the other direction. Tankertrackers.com says the collective capacity of tankers sailing empty toward the Gulf to collect cargo has fallen about 40% since the end of August. Co-founder Samir Madani's summary is that there are not enough vacant tankers to take the oil out. And at the American end the price keeps falling: EIA's weekly retail diesel average printed $5.937 on 16 November — down 19.0 cents in a week and the first print below $6 since 7 September, 59.2 cents below the 21 September record.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the Gulf export recovery is durable. The volume data says yes: 11.4 million barrels a day, a wartime high, achieved while attacks continued. The ballast data says no: the fleet sailing in empty to lift those barrels has shrunk 40% in eleven weeks. A dispute about facts, and both are measurements of the same trade taken at different points in the cycle.

Cause, and it resolves cleanly. Exports are a flow you can sustain for a while by emptying a buffer. The buffer here is ships already in the region; a recovery run on in-position tonnage shows up as record loadings now and as a shortage later, which is precisely what the two datasets are showing. That also revises what this brief wrote yesterday. Yesterday's lead explained the 64% collapse in supertanker rates as falling tonne-miles from shorter voyages, and that is still right — but it is incomplete, because part of the freed capacity was VLCCs taken out of service by attacks and replaced by two Suezmaxes doing the same work. A rate can fall because voyages shortened and simultaneously because the ships doing them got smaller and more numerous.

What it means for you. Expect the pump relief to continue for several more weeks and then to become conditional on tonnage rather than on diplomacy. If the ballast fleet keeps shrinking, Gulf loadings fall regardless of how the war goes, and the crude price turns back up without a single new attack. Believe the $5.937 print — it is the series of record and it is the first sub-$6 federal diesel number in ten weeks. What would settle the durability question: the ballast count over the next month, the October export figure holding in November, and today's EIA release, which lands hours after this brief and carries the crack spread — still $97.99 on 6 November, and the number that decides whether the fall is reaching the driver or the refiner.

What to watch: today's EIA release and the crack spread; the ballast tanker count; whether the Tara Gas crew member is found; whether US self-defence strikes continue at this tempo; and whether Kuwait names the drones' origin.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — a US military official and Iranian state media on the strike, ADNOC L&S and Omani authorities on the tanker, Bloomberg via gCaptain for the Vortexa, BRS and Tankertrackers figures, and EIA's own weekly series read this morning. A rule published tomorrow rewrites who may see your medical record without asking you

Science & public health New subject Underreported Primary source

The Office for Civil Rights has finalised a 363-page rewrite of the HIPAA Privacy Rule, publishing tomorrow, effective sixty days later, with a compliance date 240 days after publication. It finalises a proposal first made in 2021 and it moves in two directions at once: it gives you faster and cheaper access to your own record, and it widens who else may receive it without your authorisation.

What you gain. The deadline for a covered entity to answer a request for your own records falls from 30 calendar days to 15, keeping the existing 30-day extension. The rule defines electronic health record and personal health application for the first time, reduces the identity-verification burden on people exercising access rights, amends the fee structure to specify when electronic records must be provided at no charge, and requires covered entities to post estimated fee schedules on their websites — for access requests and for authorised disclosures alike. Where a provider offers a summary instead of a copy, it must now tell you that you keep the right to the copy.

What you lose, and what others gain. The right to direct your records to a third party of your choosing is narrowed to electronic copies held in an electronic health record, which the rule says follows a binding court decision. Meanwhile the definition of health care operations is amended to cover individual-level care coordination and case management, and the rule clarifies that covered entities may disclose protected health information to social services agencies, community-based organisations and home and community-based services providers to coordinate care — a class of recipient that is not a doctor, not an insurer, and not bound by HIPAA itself.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether permitting disclosure to social-services organisations without authorisation serves patients or exposes them. The department's case is that value-based care fails when a case manager cannot tell a housing agency what a patient needs. The objection is that a social services agency is not a covered entity, so once the record arrives it is outside HIPAA's enforcement entirely. A dispute about significance, and both are right about different patients.

Cause. The two positions come from different parts of the same system, and the ordinary explanation is sufficient. Care coordinators see people whose medical problems are housing, food and transport problems, and every consent form is a point at which coordination fails. Privacy advocates see categories of information — substance use, mental health, reproductive care, immigration-adjacent detail — whose disclosure to a local agency has consequences a clinician never sees. Note the drafting tell: the access provisions are specific and measurable (15 days, fee schedules posted, no charge for ePHI), while the disclosure provisions are framed as clarifying an existing permission rather than creating a new one. A clarification is how a regulator widens a permission without having to defend it as new.

What it means for you. Two things to do and one to watch. After the compliance date you can demand your own electronic record in fifteen days and check the posted fee schedule before anyone quotes you a price — both are enforceable and both are new. The thing to watch is that no consent step is added anywhere in this rule; the coordination disclosures are permitted, not required, so the question becomes which providers choose to make them and under what agreements. What would settle the dispute: OCR's enforcement posture on downstream misuse, whether any state writes a stricter consent rule on top (HIPAA is a floor), and the severability section, which this brief notes the rule contains and has not read in full.

What to watch: the effective date sixty days out and the compliance date at 240; whether states legislate above the floor; litigation and how the severability clause is used; whether providers publish fee schedules on time; and OCR's first enforcement action under the new access deadline.

Confidence: Primary source — the final rule as filed for public inspection, read in the original through its summary of major provisions. This brief has not read all 363 pages, and has not read the response to comments or the severability analysis. No outside reporting on it was found this morning. Sources: HHS OCR, HIPAA Privacy Rule: Changes to Support Coordinated Care and Individual Engagement (RIN 0945-AA00)

The president has confirmed the dirty-bomb intelligence by threatening Iran over it

Geopolitics Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief ran the dirty-bomb intelligence as a lead and labelled it single-sourced on seven anonymous officials, with the White House saying there were no credible threats to the homeland. That labelling is now out of date, and not because a second newsroom matched it.

The president answered the reporting on the record. Speaking to reporters in the Philippines, he said Iran would “pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid before” if it targeted members of his administration with a dirty bomb or chemical weapon in Washington. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said on CNBC that carrying out the plan would mean an all-out war — going after one of our cities or Washington, he said, would mean using all resources to destroy you — while also saying the threat is not new and that Iran is “very aspirational”.

Neither man denied the existence of the plan. Twenty-four hours earlier the administration's position was that there were no credible threats to the homeland; today two of its most senior officials are describing consequences for a specific attack by a specific country on a specific city.

A related data point landed the same day: a man was sentenced to more than 27 years for an assassination plot against the president that he said was directed from Iran, which is the previous generation of this threat reaching a courtroom.

Divergence

The point in dispute. How serious this is. Mullin says it is not new and Iran is aspirational — a minimising frame. He also says it would cause all-out war, which is not. The reporting says the plotting is specific, including locations and targets. A dispute about significance conducted within a single official's answer.

Cause. The tension is structural rather than evasive, and it is worth naming because it will recur. A homeland security secretary has two jobs that pull opposite ways in the same sentence: reassure a public that must not panic, and deter an adversary that must not test him. “Aspirational” does the first; “all-out war” does the second. The vantage point is also different from yesterday's spokesperson, who was answering a narrow question about whether a credible threat — a term of art requiring imminence — existed. Reach for that before reading a contradiction: the spokesperson and the secretary were answering different questions, and only the secretary was asked what the United States would do about it.

What it means for you. The confirmation that matters is behavioural, not verbal. No one has produced a document, and no second newsroom has matched the intelligence — but an administration that responds to a leak with a presidential threat and a cabinet secretary's war warning has told you the underlying reporting is substantially right. Upgrade the story from single-sourced to corroborated by official response, and leave the capability question exactly where the sources left it: debated. What would still settle it: an NTAS bulletin, a public FBI or DHS advisory, congressional briefings disclosed in January, or a prosecution. None has appeared, and this brief has still seen no document.

What to watch: any NTAS bulletin or DHS advisory; whether a second newsroom matches; further protective-detail changes; the effect on ceasefire talks; and whether the tempo of US self-defence strikes changes.

Alaska flipped, and the Senate will be 56 seats

US politics & policy New subject Single source

Mary Peltola will defeat Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska, CNN's decision desk projects, taking a Republican seat in a state the president won by 13 points in 2024. With it, Democrats are projected to hold 56 seats in January. Peltola, who is of Yup'ik descent, will be the first Native American senator from Alaska and shares with Minnesota's Peggy Flanagan the distinction of being among the first Native American women in the chamber.

The race was not ordinary and the irregularity is the part with consequences. It was upended by a spoiler candidate carrying the same name as the incumbent, which drew legal action from the administration and cast doubt on whether the result would be certified if Peltola won. She has won.

Fifty-six is a number with mechanics attached. It is not sixty, so the legislative filibuster still binds. It is comfortably past fifty, which decides committee ratios, subpoena authority and the confirmation calendar. For this brief's purposes the relevant consequence is the one running through four other stories in this issue: the committees that will investigate the Iran war, the FAA ground stop and the chip export decisions will be chaired by the party that now holds the chamber by six.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this result gets certified. The administration brought litigation over the same-name spoiler candidate before the count concluded, and the reporting says that cast uncertainty over certification if Peltola won. A dispute about facts that has not resolved: a projection is a newsroom's arithmetic, and certification is a state process.

Cause. Two mundane readings, and they are not exclusive. A same-name candidate is a real ballot problem, and a party that loses a seat by a margin smaller than the spoiler's vote has an ordinary legal grievance about voter confusion. A certification challenge is also the cheapest available instrument for delaying a seating, and the incentive to use it rises exactly when a chamber margin is in play. The vantage difference is that nobody outside Alaska's division of elections yet knows the margin against the spoiler's vote count, which is the single number that decides which reading is right. This brief does not have that number and has not seen the complaint.

What it means for you. Treat the 56 as provisional until Alaska certifies, and watch the certification rather than the projection. This file has carried a thread on election certification and the Justice Department since late October, and this is the first post-election test of it with a Senate seat attached. The practical consequence either way is already fixed: the chamber turns over in January and the investigative agenda in this issue does not depend on the sixth seat. What would settle it: the certified margin against the spoiler candidate's vote total; the disposition of the administration's suit; and whether the Senate seats her on schedule.

What to watch: Alaska's certification deadline and the certified margin; the administration's litigation; whether Sullivan concedes; the committee ratios that follow from 56; and the January swearing-in.

Nvidia's board said no to Huang hours after he said he would be honoured to return

AI & tech industry Continuation Independent corroboration

Nvidia's board unanimously accepted Jensen Huang's resignation and does not intend to reinstate him, chair Harvey Jones said at the start of Tuesday night's earnings call. Huang had said earlier the same day that he would be “honored” to return if the board allowed it. Jones said the board has every confidence in chief financial officer Colette Kress, now interim chief executive, while a permanent search runs.

The numbers were not the problem. Nvidia more than doubled quarterly revenue and profit against a year earlier, and both beat expectations. Kress told investors the roadmap is on track and the company's ambition has never been greater.

The context those results landed in is the one this brief has been tracking all week. The shares closed Monday at $156.14, their lowest since July 2025, down about 35% from the 6 October record with roughly $2 trillion of market value erased; Handelsblatt reported on Monday that Silicon Valley was lobbying for Huang's return. And on Monday the Commerce Department filed the rule that makes remote access to Nvidia's chips an export-control question, naming Singapore among five higher-risk destinations.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether the company's problem is its results or its governance. The earnings say results are excellent — revenue and profit more than doubled and beat forecasts. The share price says the market repriced the company by a third anyway. A dispute about significance: everyone agrees on both sets of numbers.

Cause, and the week supplies it. A semiconductor company's value is a claim on future demand, and two things happened to that claim in eleven days that no quarter can answer. The founder who personally held the relationships with the administration and with every major customer is gone, and the board has now foreclosed his return in public, which removes the optionality the market was partly pricing. And the regulatory regime changed underneath the product: a licence requirement attached to compute delivered from data centres anywhere narrows the addressable market in a way that shows up in future quarters, not this one. Note the sequencing, which is unusual and tells you the board's priority: Huang's offer to return came first and the rejection came hours later, at the top of an earnings call, before any financial detail. A board that wanted to keep the option open would have said nothing.

What it means for you. Stop reading this as a scandal story and start reading it as a demand-visibility story. The results confirm that nothing has yet changed in what customers are buying; the price confirms that investors no longer believe they can see what customers will buy in a year. Believe that the board has chosen stability over the founder and that the quarter was strong. What would settle the forward question: guidance on China exposure in the light of the new rule; whether a permanent chief executive is hired from outside, as the Treasury Secretary urged; and whether any customer in the five newly licensed countries discloses a delayed order.

What to watch: the chief executive search and whether it goes outside; China guidance against the new rule; whether Huang resurfaces elsewhere; the Trump–Xi meeting; and whether the shares stabilise.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — the board chair and the interim chief executive on the company's own earnings call, reported by CNN, with Monday's market data from the same outlet. This brief has not read the quarterly filing. Sources: CNN · 17 November

California has grounded Waymo on its freeways for imminent hazard

AI & tech industry New subject Single source

California's Department of Motor Vehicles told Waymo on Tuesday that its driverless vehicles may not use state freeways for now, finding that they pose an “imminent hazard to public safety”. Waymo responded by pausing freeway operations in every US market, not only California. It runs freeway operations in five of its thirteen service areas: the San Francisco Bay Area, Los Angeles County, Phoenix, Miami and Austin.

The DMV named two incidents and one pattern. Three Waymo vehicles entered an active construction zone on US Route 101. Another vehicle attempted to pass California Highway Patrol cars conducting a traffic break on Highway 280 earlier this month. And the department says Waymo vehicles have made “recurrent incursions” into active construction zones, putting workers, other vehicles and equipment at risk. Waymo says it is working with the DMV and other state officials to resume.

The regulatory shape here is new and is the reason this is a lead rather than a short. Federal vehicle safety standards are NHTSA's; roadworthiness and licensing are a state's. A state motor vehicle department has just used its licensing authority to stop a specific driving behaviour nationwide, because the operator chose to pause everywhere rather than run two policies. Three weeks ago this brief was reading NHTSA's proposal to let automated vehicles drop turn-signal controls and add cyan marker lamps; that is federal equipment rulemaking proceeding on one track while a state grounds the fleet on another.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether a handful of incidents justifies removing a fleet from freeways. The DMV's standard is imminent hazard, and it cites recurrent construction-zone incursions as a pattern. The implicit industry answer is that human drivers enter construction zones and pass stopped patrol cars constantly, at rates no regulator counts. A dispute about facts with a missing denominator.

Cause. The denominator is missing for a structural reason, not a convenient one. Autonomous vehicle incidents are reported, logged and attributable to a single operator; the equivalent human events are not reported at all unless someone is hurt. That asymmetry makes an AV fleet look worse than the traffic around it on any raw count, and it also makes it the only part of traffic a regulator can actually act against. Reach for that before reading either bad faith or capture. Note too what the nationwide pause reveals about the company's own view: an operator confident the incidents were anomalous would have paused in California and kept running in Phoenix. It did not.

What it means for you. The precedent is the product here, not the inconvenience. A state has demonstrated that a licensing authority can halt an automated driving capability in days, with no rulemaking, on a finding it writes itself — which is faster than any federal instrument and is now the fastest-acting constraint on automated driving in the United States. Expect other state DMVs to be asked why they have not done the same, and expect the industry to argue for federal preemption on exactly this ground. What would settle the safety question: Waymo's own construction-zone incursion rate per million miles against a human baseline, which the company has and the DMV has not published; and whether the department conditions resumption on a specific capability rather than on time.

What to watch: what the DMV requires for resumption; whether other states act; whether NHTSA or Congress raises preemption; Waymo's own incident data; and whether the pause extends beyond freeways.

Macro · The ten-year's real yield has kept climbing while the nominal has stalled, so the market's inflation expectation has fallen back to 2.20% — the pump showing up in the bond market.

The implied breakeven therefore went 2.20, 2.24, 2.24 and back to 2.20, while the real yield rose fourteen basis points across the same four sessions. It is the same story as federal diesel printing below $6, read off a different instrument.

Below the line because four sessions is not a trend and because the ten-year moving three basis points is not an event. It runs because the breakeven is the cleanest available measure of whether the public expects this inflation to persist, and it has now turned.

Cybersecurity · After a fortnight of outages, the honest observation from security practitioners is that distinguishing a glitch from an attack is getting harder, not easier.

The week's failures have been attributed to a router, a software update, a patch and a cloud problem, and in each case the first public question was whether it was an attack. The question is hard because the symptoms of a botched change and of a successful intrusion converge: services degrade, systems do not restart, and the operator's own telemetry is part of what broke.

That ambiguity is exploitable. An adversary who knows a patch cycle produces unexplained failures has cover; a defender who assumes every failure is a patch has a blind spot. The NSA case in the first lead is the sharpest version: the agency says routine maintenance, the intelligence director says an emergency patch, and the only published assessment of whether anyone was inside is “no evidence” — which is not “no”.

Marine safety · The NTSB warned about exactly this class of Mississippi River passenger-vessel collision nearly thirty years ago.

The board that is now investigating the capsizing of the paddlewheeler American Heritage at New Orleans issued warnings about collision risk to passenger vessels on the Mississippi almost three decades ago. The board's mechanical finding, briefed on Sunday and reported here on Monday, was that the asphalt tanker Atlantic Asphalt suffered a steering casualty with its rudder stuck to starboard two minutes before impact; the paddlewheeler with 190 aboard capsized at the dock in about 22 minutes. A thirty-year-old safety recommendation that was not adopted is a different kind of fact from a rudder failure.
