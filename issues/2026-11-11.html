A Fed governor floated a half-point rise, and named the AI buildout as a reason

Macro & markets Continuation Independent corroboration

Core consumer prices rose 0.3 per cent in the month, against an expected 0.2. Speaking in London, Fed Governor Chris Waller said he would consider backing a 50 basis point rise at the December meeting if core inflation keeps printing at 0.3 or above. "Then, a stronger policy move may be needed," he said. "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take."

The bond market moved hard. By this brief's own read of Treasury's series, the two-year par yield rose 17 basis points on 10 November to 4.73 per cent — the largest single-day rise in the two-year of 2026. The five-year rose 14, the ten-year 10 and the thirty-year 9. CNN reports it as the biggest two-year jump since April 2025, a window longer than this brief has checked.

What makes this more than a rates story is what Waller said he is watching. He named two things: how the AI buildout is affecting the prices of high-technology goods, and whether this year's rise in energy prices is still adding to inflation. Those are the two subjects this brief has led on most often in the past fortnight, and a Fed governor has just attached them to the December decision.

The two-year has now travelled 4.46 on 5 November, in the flight to quality during the \$2.5 trillion equity rout, to 4.73 five sessions later. It has not merely retraced that move; it is 27 basis points above where it started.

Divergence. A dispute about cause, between two readings of the same 17 basis points. Read one: the data did it. A 0.3 core print against a 0.2 expectation is a genuine surprise, and bond markets reprice on surprises; Waller's remarks were a consequence of the same number, not an independent shock. Read two: Waller did it. A sitting governor naming a specific threshold — 0.3 or higher — and a specific response — 50 rather than 25 — converts a data point into a rule, and it is the rule that moves a two-year yield, because it tells the market what the next three prints mean.

Cause of the divergence. The two are separable by timing and this brief cannot separate them, because it has an end-of-day yield and not an intraday series. CNN's account says the move started after Waller's remarks and extended after the CPI release, which if right means both mattered and the speech came first. What is not in dispute is the direction of surprise: the brief spent last week reporting a market pricing a slowdown in the AI buildout, and a Fed governor has now cited that same buildout as a possible source of inflation requiring faster tightening. Those are opposite readings of one phenomenon, held simultaneously by the equity market and a central banker.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the 17 basis points and the 0.3 print as fact and the attribution as unsettled. The settling observations are dated and close: the next core CPI print, the December meeting itself, and whether other governors adopt Waller's threshold language. One governor floating a number in London is not a committee. For this brief the more useful consequence is that its energy and AI threads now have a monetary transmission channel it had not been tracking — and that the hyperscaler capital expenditure guidance named repeatedly as the discriminator for the AI story is now also an input to the December decision.

What to watch: the next core CPI; whether any other governor uses the 0.3 threshold; the December meeting; whether the two-year holds above 4.70; and hyperscaler capex guidance, which now matters in two directions.

The two diesel instruments disagree for the first time, and the weekly one says the turn has come

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

AAA this morning: regular $3.9429, diesel $6.1306, a premium of $2.1877 — another record, the fifth in seven days.

EIA's weekly survey says the opposite. For the week to 9 November it puts retail diesel at $6.127 and regular at $3.995 — a premium of $2.132, down from $2.139 the week before, which was the widest weekly reading of 2026. The weekly series has turned. The daily card average has not.

This brief's standing rule, adopted on 22 September after it called a turn in diesel off three shrinking daily AAA increases and was wrong the next morning, is that a daily card average measures today and a weekly official survey measures the trend, and direction comes from the weekly one. On that rule, the turn has come.

Two other things in today's numbers. Regular in the official weekly series is $3.995 — below four dollars for the first time since 13 July, seventeen weeks. And the stocks picture behind all of it is the tightest in years.

Divergence. Between two instruments measuring the same quantity, which is the cleanest kind this brief gets. AAA: a new record premium today. EIA: the premium narrowed in the week to 9 November. Both are correct about what they measure. AAA is a daily average of credit-card transactions settling today; EIA is a Monday survey of retail outlets covering a week. The AAA record is the arithmetic of regular falling faster than diesel on one day; the EIA narrowing is diesel falling faster across a week.

Cause of the divergence. They disagree because they are measuring different windows at a moment when the two prices are converging unevenly, and the daily series will cross the weekly one within days if the weekly trend holds. What makes the weekly reading the more informative is not authority but construction: a survey of outlet prices across a week cannot be moved by one day's mix of transactions, and the thing in dispute is a trend. The stocks chart is the reason to expect the weekly series to be right: at 99.3 million barrels, distillate inventories are below anything in the two prior years, which is a tight market, not a loosening one — and yet the premium is falling, which means the relief is coming from the refining margin rather than from supply.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Take the weekly series: the premium has probably peaked, at $2.139 in the week to 2 November. The settling data arrives today at 10:30 Eastern, after this brief publishes — the EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report, with refinery utilisation and distillate stocks for the week to 6 November. Utilisation was 94.5 per cent and stocks 99.3 million at 30 October. If stocks build and utilisation holds, the weekly premium reading is confirmed and the record daily figures of this week will read as the last of the old move. If stocks fall again with the premium, the two are decoupling and the explanation this brief has been running since Sunday is incomplete.

What to watch: today's 10:30 release — utilisation and distillate stocks for the week to 6 November; whether the daily AAA premium crosses below the weekly reading; whether regular stays under four dollars; and the cold snap forecast for the eastern United States, which is a demand input this brief has not been tracking.

Confidence: Primary source throughout — AAA for the daily figures, EIA's own weekly series for the retail prices, the stocks chart and the "lowest of 2026" claim, all computed here across 44 weekly observations for 2026 and 52 for each prior year. The EIA retail survey updates earlier in the week than the Weekly Petroleum Status Report, which is why this brief has the price half and not the inventory half of today's picture. A Chinese defence university posted its receipt for two banned Nvidia chips

AI & tech industry Continuation Single source

Northwestern Polytechnical University — a member of China's "Seven Sons of National Defense" group of elite defence universities, and on a US export blacklist — bought two Nvidia H200 AI chips last month for 874,700 yuan, about $123,000. The evidence is a procurement record dated 21 October on the university's own website, reported by Reuters.

This brief has written about Nvidia and export controls twice this week. On Monday it said the intelligence allegation against the chief executive rested on three layers of attribution with no named source, no agency, no charge and no filing, and that the settling evidence would be a BIS action, an Entity List addition, an indictment or a licence revocation. None of those has appeared. What has appeared is something different and in some ways better: a dated, priced, self-published document showing restricted hardware in the hands of a blacklisted defence institution.

It is also small. Two chips for $123,000 is a rounding error against a company whose market value is counted in trillions, and nothing connects this purchase to the allegations about Megaspeed or to anyone at Nvidia.

Divergence. A dispute about significance: whether two chips is a leak or a system. Read one: an isolated procurement. Two units, bought through some intermediary, posted on a procurement page by an administrator following a routine disclosure rule — the quantity is trivial and the publication suggests nobody involved thought they were doing anything that needed hiding. Read two: the quantity is the point. A blacklisted defence university does not need thousands of chips to do what it does, and the fact that it can buy two and publish the receipt says the controls are not binding at the retail end at all.

Cause of the divergence. The readings differ on what export controls are for, and that difference is resolvable. If the purpose is to deny China AI capability at scale, two chips is noise. If the purpose is to deny specific listed entities access, this is a straightforward failure, documented by the entity itself. The second is what the Entity List is: a named-party prohibition, not a volume cap. On that reading the significance does not scale with the number, and the procurement record is evidence about enforcement rather than about capability.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the purchase as documented and the route as unknown, because the route is the whole enforcement question. What would settle it is who sold them: a distributor, a grey-market reseller, or a third-country intermediary of the kind the suspended BIS Affiliates Rule was written to reach. That rule — extending controls to affiliates of listed entities — has been suspended for all fourteen months of its existence, most recently to 10 January 2027, as this brief reported on 7 November. A procurement record naming the supplier would connect those two facts, and this brief has not seen the record itself.

What to watch: whether the procurement record names a supplier; any BIS response; whether other Seven Sons institutions have comparable records; and whether the Affiliates Rule stay is extended again in January.

Confidence: Single source — a Reuters report carried by Al Jazeera. This brief did not see the procurement record, cannot read the university's website, and has no confirmation from Nvidia, BIS or the university. The connection drawn to the Affiliates Rule is this brief's own. Sources: Reuters via Al Jazeera · 10 November · 7 November

The Senate advanced a bill making data centres pay for the grid they need

Energy & grid New Single source

The Senate has voted to advance a bipartisan bill that would speed up permitting for new data centres and energy infrastructure and require data centres to pay for the infrastructure they need. Environmental groups say it does not go far enough.

The timing places it between three things this brief has been tracking separately. Last Thursday the market took \$2.5 trillion off US equities on the theory that the midterms would slow the data-centre buildout, with Texas's governor-elect's moratorium pledge as the proximate cause. Yesterday a Fed governor named that same buildout as a possible source of inflation. And now a bipartisan Senate majority is moving a bill that speeds the buildout while shifting its grid costs onto the companies doing it.

Who pays for grid connection has been the live fight in state utility commissions all year: a data centre's load is large, lumpy and arrives faster than transmission can be built, and the default rules spread the cost across all ratepayers. A federal cost-allocation rule would settle in one instrument what has been litigated state by state.

Divergence. A dispute about what the bill does on net, and the two readings disagree about which half dominates. Read one: it is a build-faster bill. The operative provisions accelerate permitting; the cost-allocation requirement is the price of bipartisan support, and faster approval is worth more to a developer than the connection charge costs. Read two: it is a make-them-pay bill. Forcing data centres to fund the infrastructure removes the subsidy that made the siting economics work in cheap-power states, and that changes where they get built even if nothing changes about how fast.

Cause of the divergence. Both readings are guesses, because the thing that decides it is a number nobody has quoted: how much of the interconnection and transmission cost the bill actually assigns, and whether it is a full-cost rule or a contribution. "Require data centres to pay for infrastructure" covers everything from a connection deposit to full system upgrade costs, and the difference between those is the difference between the two readings. This brief has read a wire account of a procedural vote and not one line of the bill.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the vote as real and the content as unread. What would settle it is the bill text and the cost-allocation section, which exist and are public. This is today's negative check and the honest statement of it is that this is a lead written off a headline, which is the thing this brief criticises in its own shorts most mornings. It runs as a lead anyway because the subject connects three threads and a reader is better served knowing the bill is moving than not — but the text is owed tomorrow.

What to watch: the bill number and text; the cost-allocation provision in particular; whether the environmental groups' objection is about the permitting speed or the emissions; and whether any state commission holds off pending federal action.

Confidence: Single source — an Associated Press account via Al Jazeera, describing a procedural vote. This brief has no bill number, no text, no vote count and no named sponsors, and everything about cost allocation above is this brief's description of the general problem rather than of this bill. Sources: AP via Al Jazeera · 6 November

Nine Kennedys sued to stop the arch

US politics Continuation Single source

Nine members of the Kennedy family filed a motion in federal court in Washington on Tuesday to block construction of the 250-foot triumphal arch. Their argument is about sightlines: that the structure "will permanently cast shadows over and damage historically significant sightlines to the existing gravesites and memorials established in the Kennedy family's honor at Arlington National Cemetery, as well as to the memorials of hundreds of thousands of fallen American heroes." They name specifically the view of the John F. Kennedy Eternal Flame from the Arlington Memorial Bridge.

This brief has carried the arch since 6 November, when it ran below the line that the planning commission would vote that afternoon and that Judge Chutkan had ordered she be told within the hour. The commission approved it 8–4 at 10:29 p.m. after more than eight hours of testimony from over 120 citizens, and Chutkan blocked construction within the hour for fourteen days — to about 19 November.

Divergence. A dispute about which argument is doing the work, and the answer matters for whether the arch gets built. Read one: the Commemorative Works Act is the case. The original suit by veterans and preservationists argues the monument is unlawful without congressional authorisation — a clean statutory question that, if won, stops the project outright regardless of sightlines. Read two: the sightlines are the case. Aesthetic and historic-preservation harms are what courts weigh when deciding irreparable injury for an injunction, and the Kennedy filing is addressed to that question rather than to the merits.

Cause of the divergence. The two are not competing theories so much as different stages, and the filing's form tells you which stage this is. It is a motion, not a new complaint: nine family members intervening to strengthen the irreparable-harm showing before the fourteen-day order expires. Statutory authority wins or loses a case; a shadow over the Eternal Flame is what persuades a judge to hold the ground while the case is decided. The Kennedys are supplying the second, and the timing — eight days before the order lapses — is consistent with nothing else.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Treat the suit as an intervention on the injunction question and the Commemorative Works Act argument as the thing that decides the project. The settling event is dated: the preliminary-injunction decision before or at the expiry of the restraining order, about 19 November. This brief has now written about this case four times and has still not read Chutkan's order, her memorandum, or any complaint — which it has said each time, and which has stopped being a disclosure and become a habit.

What to watch: the preliminary-injunction ruling by about 19 November; whether the administration appeals; whether any site preparation happens outside the order's geographic scope; and whether the new Congress legislates in January.

This brief did not read the motion, the underlying complaint, Chutkan's order or her memorandum, on the fourth occasion of writing about this case. The characterisation of the motion's legal function is this brief's inference from its timing and content, not something the reporting states. Sources: CNN · 6 November

Congo's Ebola outbreak has passed 11,000 cases and 5,387 deaths

Science & public health Continuation Single source

Government figures released on Tuesday put confirmed Ebola cases in the Democratic Republic of the Congo at 11,042, with at least 5,387 deaths. It is the largest and deadliest Ebola outbreak in the country's history. Authorities attribute their difficulty containing it to delayed detection, conflict, and strained health services.

The arithmetic is the thing to sit with. A case fatality ratio of 48.8 per cent, across more than eleven thousand confirmed cases, is a death toll approaching that of the 2014–16 West African epidemic, which was the largest in the disease's history. This brief has carried DR Congo Ebola as a thread since the summer and last gave it a lead on 24 October, when a vaccine trial began in the outbreak's epicentre testing a strain deliberately different from the circulating one.

Divergence. A dispute about what the confirmed-case count represents, and it changes the fatality ratio in opposite directions. Read one: confirmed cases understate the outbreak. Delayed detection and conflict-restricted access mean mild and unreached cases are missed, so the true denominator is larger and the real fatality ratio lower than 48.8 per cent. Read two: the deaths are undercounted too, and by more. People who die outside health facilities in conflict zones are not tested; in previous Congolese outbreaks community deaths were the hardest category to capture.

Cause of the divergence. Both effects are real and they pull the ratio opposite ways, which is why a case fatality ratio from an ongoing outbreak in an insecure area is the least reliable number in the release. What is not in doubt is the absolute death count, because 5,387 is a floor: these are confirmed. That is the figure to carry. This brief has repeatedly used "largest in the country's history" without establishing the comparison, and the 2018–20 Kivu outbreak, the previous largest, recorded about 3,470 cases and 2,280 deaths — a comparison this brief is stating from memory and has not verified today, and which should therefore be treated as indicative only.

How to read it, and what would settle it. Use 11,042 and 5,387 as confirmed floors and treat the fatality ratio as unreliable. What would settle the scale is the WHO situation report, which publishes case and death counts with a breakdown of confirmed against probable, and an estimate of community deaths. This brief has never fetched one, across a thread it has carried for months, and that is the gap.

What to watch: the WHO situation report and its probable-case breakdown; the vaccine trial begun on 24 October; whether case growth is accelerating or flattening; and whether the conflict in the affected provinces changes access.

Confidence: Single source — Reuters via Al Jazeera, citing DRC government data. This brief did not read the government release or any WHO document, and the comparison with the 2018–20 outbreak is stated from memory and flagged as unverified, which is the error that produced the Ebola correction of 24 October. Sources: Reuters via Al Jazeera
