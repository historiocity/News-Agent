Anthropic listed at a record and the market sold everything else: the Dow lost 1,101 points, Asia fell 5–6%, and Anthropic is down 6% before its second session

Macro & markets / AI & tech Continuing · Anthropic IPO Independent corroboration

Anthropic opened on Nasdaq on Thursday at $111.50, 34% above its $83 offer price, and closed at $97.17, up 17% (Yahoo Finance, Nikkei, BBC). Everything around it fell. The Dow closed down 1,101 points, or 2.2%, at 49,097; the S&P 500 lost 2% and the Nasdaq 2.3%, with more than 90% of S&P stocks lower, CBS reported. Analysts quoted by CBS named no single catalyst.

Overnight the selling moved to the markets that make AI hardware. Al Jazeera reports Japan’s Nikkei 225 down 5.1%, Korea’s Kospi down 5.2% and Taiwan’s Taiex down 6.2% on Friday; Hong Kong lost 1.9%. Before New York opened, Anthropic itself was trading 6% lower, according to the Guardian’s markets live page.

The money went to bonds. On Treasury’s own par curve the 10-year closed Thursday at 5.04%, its fifth straight lower close from 5.15% on 13 November (see Also today).

What it means for you: the listing did not do what bulls hoped, which was to prove that public investors will pay up for AI and lift the rest of the trade. If you hold broad index funds, you hold a large share of the companies that fell. What to watch: Anthropic’s second-day close, and whether Asia’s chip names keep falling when Tokyo and Taipei reopen on Monday.

Divergence — how big was the IPO, and what is Anthropic worth?

What is disputed. Mostly a significance dispute about which number to print. The raise is reported as $81.3bn (Yahoo Finance, CNN) and as $107.3bn (CoinDesk, Techmeme summaries). The value is reported as $1.27tn (Yahoo), roughly $1.3tn (Guardian), $1.5tn (Yahoo, at the offer price), $1.76tn (BBC) and “$2tn” or more (several headlines at the open).

Why they differ. Definitions explain all of it. A Techmeme summary of the deal splits the raise: 980m shares at $83 is $81.3bn in the IPO, plus a separate $26bn private placement, which makes $107.3bn. On value, the Guardian gives both readings at the close: about $1.3tn on shares outstanding and $1.75tn fully diluted, which counts options and other convertible claims. The $2tn-plus figures are fully diluted values at the $111.50 opening price.

What it means for you. Use $81.3bn as the IPO and $107.3bn as total new money. For the company’s value, use the fully diluted figure, about $1.75tn at Thursday’s close, because that is what a buyer of the whole company would pay for. Anything above $2tn was true for a few minutes on Thursday. The final prospectus filed with the SEC will fix the share counts.

Dig deeper — the safety promise and the lab’s own pace

At the opening bell Dario Amodei repeated that Anthropic would not bend to commercial or political pressure and that its AI would “remain under human control,” TechCrunch reported. Investors are now pricing that promise next to a finding about how fast the company is moving.

On 5 November METR, an outside evaluator that Anthropic gave unusual internal access, published its assessment. It estimated that Anthropic is improving its models on well-specified tasks at about twice its March 2024–October 2025 rate, with wide uncertainty: anything from 1.3 to 3 times would not surprise it. METR attributed most of the speed-up to Anthropic’s own use of AI in research, and said it saw no bottleneck likely to stop further acceleration within twelve months.

That finding is now feeding public forecasts (see Also today). For a shareholder it matters in two directions: it supports the revenue story and it raises the chance that Anthropic’s own safety policy obliges it to slow down. Contrary Research reports that the amended IPO filing says Anthropic “may imminently meet” that policy’s threshold for AI-driven acceleration. This run did not read the filing, so treat that sentence as reported, not checked.

The White House’s cyber chief is out after a week of outages; the Pentagon’s CIO takes over, and officials still say the outages were not attacks

US politics & policy / AI & tech Continuing · outages Independent corroboration

Sean Cairncross is leaving as national cyber director, the post that coordinates federal cybersecurity policy. A White House official confirmed it to CBS, CNN and NBC on Thursday. Kirsten Davies, the Defense Department’s Senate-confirmed chief information officer, takes over in an acting capacity, according to sources quoted by CNN, NBC, Defense One and Reuters.

He goes after a week in which a Microsoft network upgrade, an FAA router installation, the NSA’s internal network and bank payment systems all failed. The administration’s explanation has not changed. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told lawmakers he is “98% confident” the outages were not foreign attacks, CBS reported, and the Director of National Intelligence called them separate maintenance failures, according to Reuters via the Guardian.

The reason given for the change is about the man, not the outages. Unnamed sources told CNN, Reuters and Defense One that Trump backed the firing because Cairncross “lacked the strong cyber credentials” for the job. CBS adds that Stephen Miller, chief of staff since earlier this month, had long wanted him out. Cairncross, a lawyer and former RNC chief operating officer, was confirmed 59–35 in August 2025 with no cybersecurity background (TechCrunch).

What it means for you: nothing here says the outages were attacks. The practical question is who sets cyber policy in January. What to watch: a nomination sent to the Senate before the Republican majority ends on 3 January.

Divergence — fired, or resigned?

What is disputed. A facts dispute about how he left. Defense One, citing a White House official and two others, says he was fired. CNN’s and Reuters’ source says Trump “fully supported firing” him. Semafor found a senior White House official who strongly denies he was fired and says he resigned without being asked. Axios, as relayed by TechCrunch, calls the split amicable and timed so a successor can be confirmed. Fox has both: fired, plus an official saying he did not want another two years.

Why they differ. Every version is anonymous and comes from inside one White House. CNN and Reuters use word-for-word the same quote, which suggests one source talking to both. The “fired” version suits people who want to show the outages had consequences. The “resigned” version suits people who do not want the departure read as an admission that the outages were a security failure. Cairncross’s own statement says only “I leave my role” and praises the president.

What it means for you. On the evidence, he was pushed. Two newsrooms have the White House saying the president backed his firing, and Fox confirmed it independently. Whether he also wanted to go cannot be settled and makes no difference to policy. What would settle it: a formal letter of resignation, or Cairncross saying more.

Dig deeper — what the office does, and the confirmation clock

Congress created the office in 2021. Cairncross was its third Senate-confirmed director. It coordinates the national cyber strategy, which this administration published in March, and it has worked on AI security with the Office of Science and Technology Policy. NBC reports that Cairncross was in the White House discussions after Commerce ordered Anthropic to take down its two most capable models over the summer.

Davies can act in the job, but the post needs a confirmed director. If the White House wants its own pick confirmed by this Senate, the nomination has to move during the lame-duck weeks, which also have to carry appropriations and the permitting bill. A nomination that slips into January goes to a Democratic Senate.

California subpoenas Nvidia, and House papers show Commerce approved 879,000 AI chips for Chinese buyers

AI & tech / US policy Continuing · Nvidia / chips Independent corroboration

Two new official actions on Thursday. California Attorney General Rob Bonta served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena. He wants to know whether its advanced chips or confidential information reached China and whether the company misled investors, the LA Times and Guardian report. Bonta said the conduct of Nvidia and its former chief executive “raises bright red flags.”

In Washington, the House Foreign Affairs Committee voted to release Commerce Department licensing records. CNBC reports they show the Bureau of Industry and Security approved sales of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies, including Alibaba and Tencent, as of May. Some went to firms on the Pentagon’s list of Chinese military companies. Companies had requested 3.4 million chip sales between December and May. Nvidia told CNBC it followed the rules in force at the time and has since stopped shipping H200s to China.

This comes on top of the Jensen Huang scandal. Huang resigned after admitting an affair; reports say it was with Alice Huang, an executive linked to Megaspeed, a Singapore buyer of Nvidia hardware under investigation for diverting chips. Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott has asked Huang to testify on 3 December and Commerce Secretary Lutnick to appear too; Commerce says Lutnick will (ABC).

What it means for you: Nvidia now faces a state securities inquiry as well as federal ones, and the licensing record turns the political fight toward Commerce. What to watch: Huang’s answer on 3 December and any Justice Department response to the experts’ letter below.

Divergence — is 879,000 a scandal or a rounding error?

What is disputed. A significance dispute with a number in it. Democrats on the committee say the record shows export rules were not enforced. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon told CNBC it is “not that many”: about one approval per four requests, which looks like a working process. Handelsblatt, writing from the same records, gives a much smaller figure: 272,000 older processors approved by May for Tencent, Alibaba, Baidu and BYD, including 150,000 for Tencent.

Why they differ. The numbers measure different things. 879,000 is every approval to Chinese companies; 272,000 is the subtotal for four named groups. The judgments split by interest. The committee minority wants oversight of Commerce; a sell-side analyst is weighing Nvidia’s revenue at risk. Timing matters for the military-list point: Handelsblatt notes Alibaba, Baidu and BYD were put on the Pentagon list in February, taken off, and added again in June. Tencent has been on it since January 2025.

What it means for you. Both are partly right. The volume is small next to Nvidia’s sales. The approvals to Tencent, a company already on the Pentagon list, are the part that needs explaining. The question is no longer only what Nvidia knew. It is what Commerce approved and why. The licence-by-licence record, if the committee publishes it, would settle how many approvals went to listed firms while they were listed.

Dig deeper — the Justice Department letter and the chip revenue at stake

NBC reports that 15 national-security and AI-policy figures, including a former CIA officer, wrote to Attorney General Todd Blanche on Tuesday. They ask whether Nvidia kept selling to customers or middlemen after it had evidence that chips were being routed to China, or “deliberately blinded itself” to violations. No Justice Department response has been reported.

On the money: CNBC says Nvidia reported $2.4bn of quarterly revenue from H200 and related China sales on Tuesday, and that China and Hong Kong customers were about 9.5% of revenue. An earlier statement that H200 shipments were under 1% of data-centre revenue covered the quarter ended July, so the two figures are not in conflict. On Monday Washington also issued a rule to curb Chinese access to AI chips through data centres in Southeast Asia (Nikkei Asia), which this brief covered on 17 November.

The Senate passes the permitting overhaul 62–33, including a rule that big data centres pay for their own grid hookups; the House takes it up after Thanksgiving

US politics & policy / Energy & grid New Independent corroboration

The Senate on Thursday night passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) by 62–33 and sent it to the House, CBS, NBC and Roll Call report. Fifteen members of the Democratic caucus voted yes with almost all Republicans. Thirty-one Democrats voted no, along with Republicans Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds. President Trump backs it.

The bill, written by Shelley Moore Capito, Sheldon Whitehouse, Mike Lee and Martin Heinrich, sets tighter deadlines for environmental reviews, speeds up approval of transmission lines and limits agencies’ power to revoke permits already issued. It covers power lines, wind and solar, hard-rock mining and oil drilling. The Senate voted on the authors’ revised text and took no further amendments, Roll Call says.

One clause lands directly on household power bills. NBC, citing the Bipartisan Policy Center, reports that new data centres of 20 megawatts or more would have to pay the full extra cost of the infrastructure built to serve them, rather than spreading it across ratepayers.

What it means for you: it is the first federal attempt to make AI’s power build-out pay its own way at the grid connection. It also makes new pipelines and power lines easier to build. What to watch: House Majority Leader Steve Scalise says “the details matter.” If the House changes the text, it goes back to a Senate that turns Democratic in January.

Dig deeper — who objected, and why the window is short

Most Senate Democrats who voted no said the bill weakens environmental protection and helps fossil-fuel projects. Some tribal organisations objected to looser consultation rules (NBC). Supporters on the left argue that the current permitting system blocked the clean-energy projects the Inflation Reduction Act paid for.

Majority Leader Thune told reporters the fastest route to law is for the House to pass the Senate bill unchanged. The House returns after Thanksgiving. Any change means another Senate vote in a short lame-duck session that also has to deal with defence spending.

JPMorgan, Visa and Mastercard confirm hackers reached their vendors; the banks say names and emails, the extortion crew says more

US financials New Independent corroboration

JPMorgan Chase said on Thursday that outside companies it uses were hit by a cyberattack, and that the attackers probably got marketing data that may include customer names and email addresses. Mastercard said a vendor exposed email addresses of a small group of cardholders in one benefit programme. Visa said a vendor in Asia-Pacific was breached and no payment data was involved. ABC, Reuters (via Claims Journal) and The Register have the statements; American Banker, carrying Bloomberg, reports JPMorgan Reserve cardholders’ emails were exposed.

All three say their own systems and payment networks were not touched. All three say this is unrelated to this week’s outages, including Monday’s Chase payment failure, which the bank blamed on a software bug. None would name the vendors or say when the breach happened. Chase says it will contact affected customers and offer free credit monitoring.

What it means for you: if you hold a Chase card, especially a Sapphire Reserve, or a Visa or Mastercard benefit product, expect convincing phishing that uses your real name. Card numbers are not reported stolen. What to watch: a state data-breach filing, which would put a number on affected customers.

Divergence — vendor marketing lists, or the banks themselves?

What is disputed. A facts dispute about scope. The Register reports that a Russia-linked extortion crew called n0n posted all three firms to its leak site on Monday, presenting them as compromised. The companies say only vendors were breached and only contact data was exposed.

Why they differ. Timing and interest. The crew’s claim came first and is meant to pressure the firms into paying. The companies’ statements came after investigation, and they have their own interest in keeping it narrow. But a bank or card network that makes a breach disclosure in writing faces regulators if it is wrong. Reuters adds that the OCC and Treasury were calling banks within minutes of Monday’s outage, so supervisors are already watching.

What it means for you. Believe the firms on what was taken, for now. Their written statements are specific and legally exposed; the gang has shown nothing. Leak sites overstate as a matter of business. This would change if n0n publishes samples that include account or card data, or if a state attorney-general filing shows a breach count larger than a marketing list.

Sources: ABC News · Claims Journal (Reuters) · The Register · American Banker (Bloomberg)

Washington holds its first seabed-mining lease sale, off American Samoa, at the minimum price, and a US group wins Kenya’s biggest critical-minerals tender

US politics & policy / Geopolitics Continuing · deep-seabed mining Independent corroboration

Interior completed the first US offshore critical-minerals lease sale on Thursday, gCaptain (carrying Reuters) and ABC (carrying AP) report. Two lease areas covering more than 31.5 million acres of seabed, 150–240 miles east of Pago Pago and 4,630–20,100 feet deep, drew $6m in high bids. That is exactly the $3m minimum for each area. Interior did not name the winners. Secretary Doug Burgum called it “a historic step.”

The sale went ahead three days after a federal judge in Hawaii ruled that the National Marine Fisheries Service broke the Endangered Species Act in its review of the sale. American Samoa’s governor opposes mining in the territory’s waters, and Oceana says most of the more than 76,000 public comments were against it.

Separately, the BBC reports that Frontier Minerals, a group of three US companies (Bunker Hill Mining, American Resources and ReElement Technologies), won the tender for Kenya’s Mrima Hill deposit. It beat seven bidders, including Chinese and Australian firms. The site is estimated to hold more than $60bn of minerals, including niobium, which is used in defence and aerospace alloys. Two losing bidders plan to challenge the award, Reuters reports via the BBC.

What it means for you: these are two concrete steps in the push to source critical minerals outside China. Both are options to explore, not mines. What to watch: Interior naming the winning bidders, and the court’s remedy order in Hawaii.

Divergence — “historic” sale, or a floor-price sale?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. Interior calls it historic. The bids landed exactly on the floor: $3m each for two areas together larger than South Carolina, with no winner named.

Why they differ. Interior wants the programme to look as if it has momentum. Bids at the minimum usually mean little or no competition. Interior has not said how many bidders took part, which is the number that would show demand. Opponents (Oceana, the territorial government) have their own interest in making the sale look unwanted. The court ruling adds legal risk that a bidder would price in.

What it means for you. Read it as the start of a process with thin demand so far, not a rush. The leases are cheap options on exploring nodules and crusts that nobody yet mines commercially in US waters. The bidder count and names will settle it. So will whether the court’s ESA ruling forces the review to be redone before any permits are issued.

Sources: gCaptain (Reuters) · ABC News (AP) · BBC on Kenya

Florida’s emergency-management watchdog says she was fired for trying to oversee Alligator Alcatraz; the governor’s office says she just refused to resign

Florida & Tampa Bay New Primary source

Mandi Mitchell, the inspector general of the Florida Division of Emergency Management (FDEM) for four years, said on LinkedIn that she was fired on Wednesday “for political reasons.” She wrote that her office had been blocked from independent oversight of Alligator Alcatraz and other immigration enforcement, and that the chief of investigations who led that oversight was fired the same day.

Governor DeSantis’s press secretary, Alex Lanfranconi, replied that every employee of the governor’s office had been asked for a resignation letter as part of the transition to Governor-elect Byron Donalds, and that when Mitchell refused, “she was fired,” the Miami Herald/Tampa Bay Times reports.

The money behind it is the state’s disaster fund. The Herald reports that more than 70% of the emergency fund, over $1.2bn, has gone to immigration enforcement since summer 2025, under a standing emergency declaration. FDEM’s report on the fund due on 15 July arrived nearly two months late, and the 15 October quarterly report had not been delivered when she was fired, according to Herald/Times reporting relayed by CiberCuba.

What it means for you: this is the agency that pays for hurricane response, and its watchdog is gone with the fund’s reports overdue. What to watch: whether the Legislature asks for the overdue report, and whom Donalds appoints after 5 January.

Divergence — a normal transition, or the removal of oversight?

What is disputed. A facts dispute about motive; the sequence is agreed. Both sides say she was asked to resign, refused and was fired. She says the reason was her oversight of immigration spending. The governor’s office says it was a routine request made to everyone.

Why they differ. Both sides are the interested parties, and neither has published documents. The transition explanation is plausible for political appointees. It explains less well why her chief of investigations, who was leading the Alligator Alcatraz review, was fired the same day, if her account of that is accurate. Mitchell has not said what oversight was blocked or by whom.

What it means for you. Hold judgement on motive, but the fact that matters is not disputed: FDEM’s oversight office has been emptied while its largest spending programme is behind on reporting. Three things would settle it: whether other agency inspectors general who refused were also fired, whether the chief of investigations was a political appointee, and Mitchell’s promised next steps, which may include a whistleblower filing.

US financials The FDIC has proposed how deposits placed through custodians would be treated under the new housing law, with comments due 19 January.

In today’s Federal Register, the FDIC proposes changes to its brokered-deposit rules to carry out section 901 of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which deals with custodial deposits. Whether a deposit counts as brokered affects how banks can fund themselves and what they pay for deposit insurance.

Custodial accounts are how fintech apps and stablecoin issuers keep customer money at banks, so the definition decides how cheaply those arrangements can be funded.

Below the line because it is a proposal with a 60-day comment period; the final definition is what matters.

Primary source Federal Register 2026-23669 (primary)

US financials A federal appeals court has ruled that Maryland can police Kalshi’s sports contracts, deepening a split that now looks headed for the Supreme Court.

Reuters, via Insurance Journal, reports a 2–1 Fourth Circuit decision on Tuesday that Kalshi cannot stop Maryland’s gaming regulator from enforcing state law against its unlicensed sports wagers. Two other appeals courts have also ruled against Kalshi; a third held that New Jersey could not regulate it, and New Jersey has taken that case to the Supreme Court.

On the federal side, the CFTC has sued Louisiana and Arkansas to defend its exclusive jurisdiction (CFTC releases, 13 November; covered here on 19 November). The CFTC also asked Kalshi to pause its contracts on what Trump will say.

Below the line because it is one more ruling in a split already visible; the Supreme Court’s decision on whether to take a case is the event.
