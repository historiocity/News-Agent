The oil chain on Thursday: Houthi strikes kill four at a Saudi airport and oilfield, the US orders diplomats out, and Brent tops $111 on reports of post-midterm strikes on Iran

Energy & gridGeopoliticsMacro & marketsNew factIndependent corroboration

What happened. On Wednesday evening, an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, in Saudi Arabia’s oil-producing Eastern Province, killed two people and injured eight, according to the Saudi civil aviation authority (BBC, Guardian). Earlier the same day, debris from a drone intercepted over the Shaybah oilfield killed two more. The energy ministry says the drone was aimed at an Aramco processing plant and operations were not affected. The Houthis have not claimed either attack. Saudi authorities count at least 21 people killed in attacks on the kingdom’s airports since 1 October (BBC). State has ordered non-emergency staff and their families out of Saudi Arabia, and spokesperson Tommy Pigott says the order covers Saudi Arabia only (CBS, Guardian, CNBC). The New York Times reports US and Israeli plans for a “short but intensive” operation against Iran after the 3 November midterms, which Trump has not approved (relayed by CNBC and the Guardian). The IRGC claims seven vessels were hit or struck mines in the past day. UKMTO has confirmed two tanker strikes, on 20 and 21 October, with crews safe (gCaptain).

Where it lands. Brent was $111.74 at 6:30 a.m. ET, up 2.7%, and WTI was $99.30 (CNBC). AAA this morning: diesel $6.3090, regular $4.2701 (up 0.57¢), a premium of $2.04 (AAA, read). Airlines are pricing it in. American now expects a deeper 2026 loss on fuel despite record revenue, and Southwest cut its profit forecast (CNBC). The first Russian diesel cargo under the Trump–Putin deal, about 300,000 barrels, sails for the US today (Novak via Bloomberg/gCaptain). That is about a third of one day of US distillate demand.

Why it matters. The barrels are still moving, but the cost of moving them keeps rising, and now so does the risk at the Saudi end, the supplier that has done most to refill the market. Aramco says it is asking foreign governments for access to their strategic storage and wants to double its overseas capacity (Nasser via Bloomberg/gCaptain).

What to watch: whether a Houthi strike hits an export terminal rather than a field perimeter; whether the drawdown spreads beyond Saudi Arabia; and EIA’s weekly diesel print on Monday.

Dig deeper

Freight is where the shock is. The IEA says moving crude from the Gulf to Asia cost more than $37 a barrel by mid-October. For a 2-million-barrel VLCC that is more than $74m a cargo, against under $15m before the war (IEA via gCaptain). The system keeping the oil moving is improvised: pipelines to the Red Sea, short shuttles through Hormuz under US protection, and ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman. UKMTO counts 32 attacks on ships around the Arabian peninsula this month, against 16 in all of September (Bloomberg via gCaptain).

Saudi output. JODI data show Saudi crude production at a record-low 6.24m b/d in August while exports rose to 4.45m b/d, the highest since March, because it drew on stocks and loaded on both coasts (Reuters via OilPrice). Bloomberg says output recovered by a third in September to more than 8m b/d. Riyadh has moved government staff to remote work, and the Future Investment Initiative conference due on 26–29 October has been postponed to 2027 (AFP via OilPrice). Aramco is studying raising its east–west pipeline capacity to 10m b/d from 7m.

The Russian diesel offsets little. Moscow pledges 500,000 tonnes in a first phase, with no timetable. Analysts call the deal too small to move prices before the vote, and Senator Tillis has said it may be illegal (Bloomberg via gCaptain).

Divergence: are Gulf exports above pre-war or not?

Substance (facts, about definitions). Bloomberg, citing Kpler, says Persian Gulf crude and condensate flows hit 24.6m b/d in the week to 18 October, against a pre-war level of “at least 18.5m b/d.” Vortexa puts the same week at 18m b/d. The WSJ, also citing Kpler, says Middle East shipments averaged more than 24m b/d that week, against a pre-war average of 23.3m b/d (via gCaptain). For October to date, Kpler says 16.7m and Vortexa 15.4m.

Cause. Mundane. The two firms count different things: Kpler includes Red Sea and Gulf of Oman loadings and ship-to-ship transfers, while Vortexa sums shipments from seven producers. Most tankers are sailing with their transponders off, which makes every count partly an estimate. Loadings also come in convoy bunches, so one strong week overstates the trend. The two pre-war baselines almost certainly cover different products: crude plus condensate, against all oil.

What it means. Use the month-to-date figures, 15–17m b/d, and treat any “above pre-war” headline as a one-week spike measured against whichever baseline flatters it. On either count, supply is short of normal and transport is the bottleneck. This would be settled by a full month of matched Kpler and Vortexa data on the same definition, which October’s close will give.

Divergence: how many died at Dammam, and in Riyadh on 10 October?

Substance (facts, minor). CNBC and The Hill say the Dammam strike killed “at least one” and injured eight. The BBC and the Guardian, citing GACA’s statement, say two were killed. For the 10 October Riyadh airport strike, the BBC says 13 killed and 308 injured, and CBS says 12 killed and more than 300 injured, including one American.

Cause. Timing. The “at least one” reports went out overnight, before the civil aviation authority’s Thursday statement named two dead, a Saudi citizen and an Indian resident. The Riyadh gap looks like a later death or a revision that one outlet carried and the other did not.

What it means. Go with two at Dammam, since that is the official figure and the later one. For Riyadh, the 13/308 figure is the more recent and specific count, but this paper has not seen the Saudi statement behind either. Nothing here changes the point that the attacks are getting more lethal.

Divergence: is the US pulling out of Saudi Arabia only, or the wider Gulf?

Substance (facts). NBC and AFP reported that families were also being withdrawn from the UAE, Jordan, Qatar and Bahrain, and Reuters said Washington planned to pull family members from other missions (Guardian, CNBC). State’s spokesperson called reports of wider withdrawals “inaccurate” and said the order covers Saudi Arabia only. CBS reports several other embassies are on “restricted operations,” which reduces staff without an ordered departure.

Cause. Both can be true. An ordered departure is a formal, mandatory step. Restricted operations lets posts send people home quietly. Reporters hearing about staff leaving Doha or Abu Dhabi may be hearing about restricted operations, and State is denying only the formal step. Officials also told NBC and CBS that the US military is quietly reducing its footprint in the region.

What it means. Believe State on the formal order, and believe the reporting that the regional footprint is shrinking. The two together are consistent with preparation for the post-midterm operation the Times describes, which is the thing that matters for oil. This would be settled by an ordered departure for a second Gulf post, or a Pentagon statement on force posture.

Inside the story, unconfirmed: the IRGC’s claim of seven vessels hit is Unconfirmed. Only two strikes are on UKMTO’s record, and no hull has been named.

AI found two remote-takeover flaws in PHP, which runs most of the web; the NSA says patch or pull your site offline, and attacks began before the disclosure

AI & tech industryUS politics & policyNew subjectIndependent corroboration

What happened. On Wednesday, the PHP project shipped emergency fixes for two flaws, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. Both let an attacker run their own code on a web server without logging in (The Verge, quoting the PHP Group). PHP is the server language behind about 69% of websites whose language is known, WordPress among them (W3Techs via The Verge). The NSA told organisations that cannot patch immediately to take their systems offline, and said federal agencies must patch at once (BBC, Guardian). Britain’s NCSC said the same and told operators to check for backdoors. Japan’s prime minister asked the public to act “tonight.” Wikipedia, 4chan, DeviantArt, several Dutch and Finnish government sites, the Paris police prefecture and UK councils went offline as a precaution (Verge, Guardian).

How it was found. The security firm Calif found one flaw using OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol under OpenAI’s “Patch the Planet” programme, and Google-owned Wiz found them independently with its own agent, Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol (TechCrunch, Guardian). Wiz says exploiting them used to take a chain of steps “at the edge of what human researchers could practically achieve,” and that language models remove that barrier.

Why it matters. This is the first internet-wide emergency in which AI both found the hole and makes it cheap to exploit. Now that the patch is public, the gap between disclosure and mass exploitation is measured in hours. The long tail of unmaintained sites will be exposed for weeks.

What to watch: whether CISA adds the CVEs to its known-exploited list; Cloudflare’s count of attacks; and whether any big US breach is traced to it.

Dig deeper

What the bugs are. In the first, PHP can be interrupted while running out of memory, leaving half-built request data that cleanup then treats as valid. In the second, internal state from one request survives after its memory is freed, and the next request handled by the same worker uses it. Both lead to remote code execution: an attacker can get passwords, API keys, certificates and databases, and use the server to attack others (The Verge). PHP 8.1 and older are out of support and will get no fix, so those sites must upgrade to a current release, which TechCrunch gives as 8.5.12. Hosted builders such as Squarespace and Wix do not run PHP. GoDaddy says any PHP site should assume it is affected.

The policy angle. The White House says it learned of the flaw on Friday (Guardian). The NSA’s “take it offline” advice is unusual for a commodity web stack, and it lands 12 days before an election run on county websites. Lead 6’s DOJ monitors and the Florida voter-lookup outage below are both downstream.

A conflict to state plainly. OpenAI, a competitor of this paper’s maker, is the main source for the “frontier AI found it” framing. The assessment of severity rests on the PHP Group, Wiz, the NSA and the NCSC, not on OpenAI.

Divergence: was it being exploited before disclosure?

Substance (facts). TechCrunch, following OpenAI, says the flaws were found before hackers and that there were “no reports of exploits in the wild.” Amazon’s security chief says a decoy server caught an exploit attempt on 17 October, four days before disclosure (Guardian). Cloudflare’s chief executive says it saw “thousands” of attempts in the 10 hours before the announcement (BBC), and its CTO says early attacks are emerging (Verge). The BBC reports that details leaked online this week and forced the announcement early.

Cause. Timing and vantage. OpenAI’s post and TechCrunch’s story reflect the disclosure-day position of the researchers. Amazon and Cloudflare are looking at their own network traffic and published later. A leak during the embargo explains both: the researchers had no evidence of exploitation when they wrote, and the attackers already had the details.

What it means. Assume exploitation began before patches were public. Amazon’s attempt from an anonymous address is direct evidence. Any PHP server that was unpatched on 17–21 October should be checked for compromise, not just patched, which is the NCSC’s advice. This would be settled by a CISA known-exploited listing or an incident report naming a victim.

Divergence: did it take frontier AI, and how old are the bugs?

Substance (significance, plus a minor fact). OpenAI says “frontier AI” was key. The researcher who found the flaw told the Guardian they used models available to the public. On age, TechCrunch says every version since PHP 5 in 2004, The Verge says “roughly 20 years,” and the BBC and the Guardian say versions from the past decade, or “more than a decade.”

Cause. OpenAI has a commercial interest in crediting its newest model. The researcher has none. GPT-5.6 Sol is OpenAI’s current public model, so both statements can be literally true. On age, the outlets are probably counting two things: when the code pattern first appeared, and which supported versions are affected.

What it means. For the reader, the second reading matters more. If public models can find and weaponise this class of bug, so can attackers, and that is Wiz’s point. The exact age doesn’t change the action needed. This would be settled by Calif’s and Wiz’s technical write-ups, which name the first affected commit.

Sources: TechCrunch (Bort); The Verge (O’Brien, Peters); Guardian; BBC (three pieces); CNBC; FT (headline); Suncoast Searchlight via WUSF. Independent accounts compared: 7.

AI money gets dearer: CoreWeave’s bonds yield about 15%, junk issuance is paused until after the midterms, and Meta creates a non-voting stock to fund its build-out

AI & tech industryUS financialsMacro & marketsNew factIndependent corroboration

The credit side. CoreWeave’s unsecured debt now yields about 15%. Its five-year credit default swaps are near 1,067 basis points, up 30% in October, and the stock is down 29% this month, to a value of about $34bn against a $90bn peak (CNBC). It has $20.7bn of debt due by 2030. Nebius is down 27% this month and IREN 32%. Australia’s NextDC fell 40% after cutting guidance. Credit-desk sources told CNBC the high-yield market for data-centre debt is effectively on hold until after the midterms, because investors want permitting and regulatory fights settled first. This morning, Nvidia’s Jensen Huang said demand for these “neocloud” firms is “off the charts” but “there’s a scarcity of capital” (CNBC).

The equity side. Meta reported Q3 revenue of $64bn, up 26%, with costs up 68% (TechCrunch). It raised the bottom of its 2026 capex range to $135–145bn (The Verge). It will create a new class of non-voting stock, distributed to all shareholders as a dividend and tradable from 2027, “usable for capital raising, employee compensation, acquisitions” while preserving the founder’s voting control (CFO Susan Li, on the call).

Why it matters. Both stories are about the same thing: how the build-out gets paid for when debt is expensive. Firms with only debt markets are being repriced. Meta is building a way to raise equity without diluting control.

What to watch: whether Nscale goes ahead with its NYSE listing; the first high-yield data-centre deal after 3 November; and how big Meta’s first issue of the new class is.

Divergence: demand problem or money problem?

Substance (significance). Huang says the fundamentals are strong and only working capital is missing, and that it is “going to get solved.” Barclays’ credit analyst calls sentiment “decidedly negative.” CNBC’s credit sources say lenders are waiting on politics, not demand. Nebius said on Tuesday that AI computing prices are rising (CNBC).

Cause. Vantage and interest. Nvidia sells the chips, owns about 9% of CoreWeave, and benefits when neoclouds raise money. Credit analysts price the chance of default on borrowers with about two-thirds of revenue from one customer, Microsoft. Both can be right: rising compute prices show demand, and 15% yields show that lenders doubt the borrowers can hold their margins through refinancing.

What it means. Read it as a financing problem, not a demand collapse. The weak link is leveraged middlemen, not the hyperscalers, which can fund from cash flow or, like Meta, design new instruments. This would be settled by whether the high-yield market reopens after 3 November at lower yields, or whether a neocloud has to raise money on distressed terms.

Divergence: profit down 43% or 29%, and capex $4.7bn or $3.73bn?

Substance (facts). Ars Technica says net profit fell 43% to $787m and capex rose 110% to $4.7bn. CNBC says net income attributable to common shareholders fell 29%, from $1.11bn to $787m, and capex “more than doubled” to $3.73bn.

On capex, one outlet may be using cash capex and the other capex including finance leases or accruals. Neither outlet has an interest in the difference. Ars was writing quickly after the release.

What it means. Use 29%: it is the figure whose base is published and whose arithmetic holds. Treat capex as “roughly doubled, $3.7–4.7bn” until the line is checked. This would be settled by the cash-flow statement in Tesla’s 10-Q, due within weeks, which this paper did not read.

The US embassy in Ottawa publishes a fact sheet accusing Canadian ministers of planning to influence the midterms; Carney, Joly and Ford deny it

US politics & policyWorld economyContinuationSingle-sourced

What happened. On Wednesday, the US Embassy in Ottawa released a fact sheet saying the Canadian government “brushes off concerns about potential interference in U.S. elections,” while its ministers and premiers “openly describe plans to influence American politics in official documents and public appearances” (BBC, quoting the document). The evidence it cites is trade politics, not covert action. It quotes Industry Minister Mélanie Joly, asked about tariffs if Democrats win the midterms, saying “that’s exactly why I’m meeting so many governors and potential presidential candidates.” It also cites Ontario Premier Doug Ford urging retaliatory tariffs aimed at states in “the current administration’s base of support.” Earlier this month, Ambassador Pete Hoekstra said US officials were “closely watching” Canadian politicians.

The answer. Prime Minister Mark Carney said the US started the trade actions and Canada’s responses protect Canadian jobs. He was speaking at the launch of a nearly C$9bn aid package for industries hit by US tariffs, with Joly and Ford beside him. Ford said he has “no interest at all” in interfering in US elections (BBC).

Why it matters. It is an official US document, issued 12 days before the election, that treats targeted retaliatory tariffs and meetings with opposition politicians as election interference. That stretches the term to cover what allies do in any trade war, and it gives Washington a frame to answer Canadian retaliation politically rather than at the WTO or under USMCA.

What to watch: whether State or the White House repeats the charge, and whether any measure follows (sanctions, visa limits, or a trade action citing interference).

Divergence: interference, or trade retaliation?

Substance (significance). The facts are not in dispute: Joly met US governors and candidates, and Ford urged tariffs aimed at politically significant states. The embassy calls this a plan to influence American politics. Canada calls it a response to US tariffs and an effort to attract investment.

Cause. Interest and timing. The embassy is speaking for an administration that has made foreign interference a midterm theme. Canada’s leaders are defending a retaliation strategy that is openly designed to hurt politically, the way tariffs targeted at bourbon or soybeans have always been. Joly’s quote is the strongest single piece of evidence. It links her meetings to a midterm outcome, but what she describes is lobbying, not covert action.

What it means. On the evidence in the fact sheet as reported, this is political trade retaliation of a kind allies have long used, not interference in the sense of covert or illegal influence. Nothing reported alleges spending, disinformation or contact with election systems. It would become something more if the US published evidence of Canadian money, advertising or FARA (foreign-agent registration) breaches. Until then, read it as trade-war escalation.

DOJ starts telling counties it will monitor them on Election Day, in Florida among four other states, and didn’t tell the states first

US politics & policyNew subjectUnderreportedSingle-sourced

What happened. The Justice Department began notifying counties this week that it wants to monitor them on 3 November. So far they are in Colorado, Florida, Minnesota, Texas and Washington, and the list is still growing (NPR). Civil Rights Division head Harmeet Dhillon has said DOJ will send 1,000 observers, “the largest monitoring effort” in its history. In 2022, the Biden DOJ monitored 64 jurisdictions in 24 states. A DOJ spokesperson says the full list will be shared “closer to the election.”

The departure from practice. In past cycles, states were notified at the same time as their counties. This time they were not, according to officials in three states, who say they have been scrambling since Tuesday to find out where DOJ will be. After NPR asked about it, several states then received notice (NPR). In Wyoming’s August primary, the Republican governor complained of “aggressive” and “irregular” behaviour by monitors, and the Republican Laramie County clerk said they showed “complete disregard” for state law. DOJ disputes that.

Why it matters. Monitoring is routine and lawful. The scale, the skipped state notice, and the administration’s stated aim of taking more control of elections are not routine. Florida is on the list, and early voting there began this week.

What to watch: which Florida counties were notified; any state that refuses monitors access to counting rooms; and the final list.

Divergence: why Adams County and not Weld?

Substance (significance). Adams County, Colorado, which includes part of the toss-up 8th District, was notified. Weld County, which covers much of the same district and has a Republican clerk, was not. Adams’ Democratic clerk says his first thought was that it is “politically motivated” and possibly intimidation. DOJ says Adams is covered by Section 203 of the Voting Rights Act, which requires election materials in voters’ own languages, and Weld is not.

Cause. Both explanations fit the facts. Section 203 coverage is a published, objective list, and it is a standard basis for monitoring. The clerk is a Democrat in a contested district who is reacting to an administration that has questioned mail ballots.

What it means. DOJ’s explanation can be checked, and on this pair it holds: Section 203 coverage is real and does differ between the two counties. The test is the full list. If the notified counties track Section 203 coverage and past practice, the clerk’s reading weakens. If they cluster in Democratic-run counties in competitive districts regardless of coverage, it strengthens.

A federal judge rules the EPA cannot dismiss a citizen suit over the gas turbines powering Musk’s Memphis data centre

Energy & gridAI & tech industryUS politics & policyNew factSingle-sourced

What happened. On Tuesday, US District Judge Debra Brown ruled that “nothing” in the Clean Air Act lets the EPA Administrator “unilaterally dismiss a citizen suit” (Ars Technica, quoting the order). The case is the NAACP’s suit, backed by Earthjustice, over unpermitted gas turbines powering the Colossus data centre in Memphis, which belongs to xAI, now SpaceXAI after its merger. The suit seeks the best available pollution controls and penalties of up to $124,426 per day per violation. The administration had argued that the EPA can end such suits whenever the President decides not to enforce, and that big penalties against AI firms racing to build would “not serve the public interest.” The judge called it an “extraordinary position.” She noted that the narrow exception the government relied on applies only when the EPA itself is enforcing, and the government has said it will not sue SpaceXAI.

Why it matters. Building off-grid gas generation and permitting it later has become a common way to get data centres running fast, and Colossus was the first case to expose it. Had the government won, it could have blocked every citizen suit over data-centre pollution, or any Clean Air Act suit at all. The ruling keeps private enforcement alive, so building first and permitting later now carries financial risk. That connects to lead 3: credit desks say lenders want permitting risk settled before they lend.

What to watch: whether DOJ appeals to the Fifth Circuit; and whether other suits over data-centre turbines cite the ruling.

The big bond holders step back: Norway’s $2.3tn fund lost $29.7bn on bonds and plans to cut its Treasuries, France’s 10-year touched 5%, and the US 10-year sits at 5.13%

US debt & fiscalMacro & marketsNew factPrimary source

The US curve (Treasury, read). Wednesday’s par yields: 2-year 4.81%, 10-year 5.13%, 20-year 5.47%, 30-year 5.42%. The 10-year is up one basis point on the day and four on the week. Its gap below the 20-year, +34 basis points, is the second straight narrowing after weeks at +38.

The holders. Norway’s sovereign fund, the world’s largest single investor, reported a third-quarter loss of 278bn kroner ($29.7bn). Its fixed income lost 3.5% and its equities returned 0.5% (CNBC). Last month it said it wants to cut its US Treasury holdings as part of a lower allocation to government debt. In Paris, the 10-year yield on French government bonds (OATs) crossed 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2002, then eased to about 4.98–4.99%, as riots and a stalled budget weigh on it (CNBC, Guardian). The Guardian reports that some Japanese fund managers have stopped lending to France altogether. France’s deficit was 5.1% of GDP last year and its debt 115.6%.

Why it matters. The marginal buyer of long-dated government debt is getting choosier everywhere. France shows what happens when a fiscal story and a political one coincide. Norway shows a giant holder deliberately trimming Treasuries. For a US curve already at 5.13% on the 10-year and 5.4% beyond 20 years, foreign appetite matters more at each auction.

What to watch: the bid-to-cover and indirect (foreign) share at next week’s 2-, 5- and 7-year auctions; and whether France forces its budget through without a vote under Article 49.3, as its finance minister says it might.

Dig deeper

What this does not show. One fund’s plan to trim is not a buyers’ strike, and the US curve did not move on the Norwegian news. On Thursday the WSJ headlined “French government-bond yields jump, spread widens as Treasury yields fall,” which suggests that money leaving euro debt has so far been going into Treasuries, not out of them. The case for watching is about structure: Norway, Japanese managers and others are saying out loud that they want less sovereign duration. On Treasury’s numbers, the US now pays more than 5% for 10-year money while that is happening.

A claim this paper is not repeating. CNBC’s NBIM story says major central banks “including the U.S. Federal Reserve began rate-hiking cycles” in the third quarter. This paper has not verified a Fed hike in that period against a Fed statement, so it is not repeated as fact.
