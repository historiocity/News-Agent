Futures jump about 3% after the worst week since 2020, but traders now price a December Fed hike as near-certain, and Bessent is warning the Fed off it

Macro & markets Follow-up Independent corroboration

US stock futures rose sharply before Monday’s open. At 5:17am ET, S&P 500 futures were up 2.7%, Dow futures 1.9% and Nasdaq-100 futures 3.5%, Reuters reported. Chips led. Nvidia was up about 8.7% before the bell and Anthropic about 4.5% in its third day of trading. Friday left the S&P 500 and the Dow more than 10% below their records, which is a correction. The Nasdaq-100 was 19.8% off its 6 October high, just short of a bear market.

Asia did not get the memo. South Korea’s Kospi fell more than 5% intraday and closed down 3.3%, Handelsblatt reported; Tokyo was shut for a holiday after Friday’s 5% drop. Taiwan, home to the chipmakers, rose more than 2%.

The bigger change is in rates. CME futures now price about a 96% chance that the Fed raises its rate by a quarter point at its December meeting, up from about 92% on Friday (CNBC, Reuters). Treasury’s own series had the 10-year at 4.92% on Friday, down from 5.04% on Thursday as money ran to bonds. It was rising again early Monday. On Sunday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that the Fed risks hurting the economy by tightening too far, Semafor reported.

Why it matters: this reader’s borrowing costs are set by the Fed and the bond market, not by Monday’s bounce. The Treasury Secretary is now publicly at odds with a central bank that is expected to hike into a falling market.

What to watch: New York Fed President John Williams speaks today. On Wednesday come October core PCE (forecast +0.3%) and revised third-quarter GDP (forecast 3.4%). Those are the last big prints before the decision.

Divergence — is the market betting on a half-point or a quarter-point?

What is disputed. A facts question that turns out to be a timing one. Semafor wrote on Sunday that traders “pared back” bets on a half-point hike on 9 December. CNBC and Reuters on Monday say the odds of a quarter-point hike went up, to 96%. Read quickly, those look like opposite calls on the Fed.

Why they differ. They measure different things. Semafor was describing the tail: the chance of a bigger, 50-point move faded as stocks fell. CNBC and Reuters quote CME FedWatch’s headline probability for the most likely outcome, a 25-point move. Both can be true at once, and they are. Semafor was writing from Sunday’s Bessent remarks; the wires were writing from Monday’s futures screen.

What it means for you. Believe both. The market has stopped betting on a bigger hike, but it still expects a hike. Bessent’s warning has not moved that pricing yet. What would settle it: if Williams sounds dovish today or core PCE comes in soft on Wednesday, the 96% should fall. If it does not fall, the Fed is about to tighten against the Treasury’s stated wishes.

Nvidia’s server makers halt shipments to Southeast Asia under the new US rules, as reporting ties the woman in the Huang affair to China’s spy agency and a chip-smuggling network

AI & tech industry Follow-up Independent corroboration

At least two of Nvidia’s server partners have stopped shipping to customers in Southeast Asia, Nikkei Asia reported on Monday. The trade outlet Digitimes separately reported the same thing, and said Nvidia’s stricter checks on who ends up with its chips are making shipments to the region harder. The pause follows the new US export rules. Southeast Asia has been one of the fastest-growing places to build AI data centres, much of it serving Chinese demand.

The scandal behind the rules moved too. The New York Times reported that Alice Huang stayed in contact with China’s main spy agency during her affair with Jensen Huang, who quit as Nvidia’s chief executive. The FT reported on Sunday that US investigators are looking at whether she was linked to a network that moved billions of dollars of Nvidia chips to China around export controls. She was an executive at the cloud provider Megaspeed. Beijing calls the spy claim slander. Jensen Huang is to be questioned by three House committees.

Why it matters: Southeast Asia is where much of the chip demand that the export rules block was going. If server makers stop shipping there, it cuts into Nvidia sales just as the stock has lost more than 40% in a month. It also shows the rules are being enforced through the supply chain, not only at the border.

What to watch: whether Commerce issues guidance or licences for Malaysian, Thai and Singaporean buyers; the dates for Huang’s House testimony; and whether Nvidia’s board answers Trump’s call to rehire him.

Divergence — what did Jensen Huang know?

What is disputed. A facts dispute that is still open. Trump has publicly defended Huang, saying he knew nothing of any spying, and he wants the board to rehire him. The FT, citing more than a dozen people plus company records and trade data, says it is unknown what Huang knew. The NYT reports that her contacts with the spy agency continued into recent months.

Why they differ. Trump wants a US champion back in charge, and a falling Nvidia stock hurts his market. The newspapers’ sources are investigators and people near the case. They can see the contacts, but not necessarily what Huang was told. Nobody on the record has said Huang was briefed or took part. Huang left after admitting the affair, which is a much lower bar than complicity.

What it means for you. There is no public evidence that Huang knew. There is solid reporting that the person close to him was in contact with Chinese intelligence. Treat “he knew nothing” as a claim, not a finding. What would settle it: his House testimony under oath, or charging documents in the Megaspeed inquiry.

OpenAI’s chief scientist proposes that AI labs agree to stop training past a shared limit until safety monitoring catches up, and Altman backs it; no limit or date is named

AI & tech industry New subject Primary source

On Sunday, OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, published an essay proposing that frontier AI developers agree on “common limits” to how far they push model training. Labs would commit not to go past a limit until the ability to understand and monitor AI behaviour reaches a set milestone. His example milestone is a successor to chain-of-thought monitors. The limit would be measured mainly by perplexity, a standard score of how well a model predicts text, where lower is stronger. Sam Altman called the essay “the most important thing OpenAI has published in awhile.”

OpenAI also published, for the first time, its internal perplexity scores by model, from GPT-3.5 (about 0.99) to GPT-6 Astra in September (0.783). Pachocki writes that AI-driven research could cause a sudden jump in training methods as soon as 2027. He also says progress on monitoring will probably be too slow to keep up.

On the same day, Meta’s chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, said superintelligence is close and that humans are unlikely to control AI that can improve itself without limits. He was reacting to the Anthropic Riemann-hypothesis result.

Why it matters: two of the biggest US labs are publicly saying the main danger is the speed of progress, just as a Democratic Congress arrives that is ready to regulate. A voluntary limit set by the industry itself is now on the table. Senator Booker has already called for Congress to rein in AI that improves itself.

What to watch: whether Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta sign on to any number; whether anyone proposes who would check compliance; and how this comes up at the first AI hearings of the new Congress.

Divergence — did OpenAI name a stopping point?

What is disputed. A facts dispute, settled by the document. One trade write-up (Silicon Report) says Pachocki suggested stopping before a log-perplexity of about 0.68–0.70. Bloomberg and TechCrunch say he named no limit and no date.

Why they differ. The essay does mention 0.68–0.70, but as a forecast: Pachocki says further “transformative capabilities” should be expected as models get there. Separately, he proposes that labs agree on a first limit. He never says the limit should be that range. The shaded band on OpenAI’s chart probably made the two look like one thing to a fast reader. Bloomberg quoted the actual text.

What it means for you. There is no committed number. Read 0.68–0.70 as OpenAI’s forecast of the next capability jump, about 0.1 below its September model. It is not a pledge. What would settle it: a joint statement from several labs that puts a figure on the limit and names who checks it.

Dig deeper — what the published numbers show

OpenAI’s chart puts its released models on one line: GPT-4 at 0.913 (March 2023), GPT-5 at 0.893 (August 2025), GPT-5.5 at 0.848 (April 2026), GPT-5.6 at 0.828 (July 2026) and GPT-6 Astra at 0.783 (September 2026). The score fell more in the last ten months than in the three years before. That is the speed-up the essay is about.

Pachocki admits the disclosure has a cost: competitors can now see where OpenAI stands. He argues the need for shared safety measures outweighs it. The score is measured on an internal OpenAI dataset, so other labs’ numbers cannot be compared directly yet. A shared limit would first need a shared test set, and the essay does not propose one.

For this brief’s lab-safety thread, the new fact is that a lab has proposed a pause mechanism. The essay is not an industry agreement, and nothing in it binds OpenAI.

CrowdStrike agrees to buy Zscaler for $43.9bn in stock, the largest cybersecurity deal yet, after weeks of outages and breaches

AI & tech industry Follow-up Primary source

CrowdStrike and Zscaler announced on Monday that CrowdStrike will buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal valuing it at about $43.9bn. Zscaler holders get 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each share. At Friday’s price that is $260.41, a premium of almost 34% over Zscaler’s $194.82 close, Dow Jones reported. CrowdStrike holders will own about 84% of the combined company. The deal is expected to close in the second half of CrowdStrike’s 2028 fiscal year, which starts on 1 February 2027. Before the open, Zscaler was up about 12% and CrowdStrike down 7%.

CrowdStrike sells endpoint security, the software on laptops and servers. Zscaler sells network security in the cloud, which replaces firewalls and VPNs. The companies say the combined business will have more than $10bn of annual recurring revenue. CrowdStrike gets a route to Zscaler’s roughly 8,000 customers. The talks were reported on Friday and ran as a short item here; Monday’s announcement makes it a lead.

Why it matters: banks, agencies and hospitals would buy more of their security from one vendor. That is simpler to run, but it means more depends on that vendor. CrowdStrike’s faulty update in July 2024 took down millions of Windows machines, and this month’s US outages have made concentration a live issue.

What to watch: antitrust review (FTC or DOJ, plus the EU); whether the outage findings due from the administration bear on vendor concentration; and the price CrowdStrike pays in its own falling stock.

PJM asks regulators to cut grid hookups for data centres and power plants from two years to one, with data centres billed for the upgrades; the first fast cycle would end in 2029

Energy & grid New subject Primary source

PJM runs the largest US grid, covering 13 states from Illinois to Virginia. On 16 November it filed a plan with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), docket ER27-531, which it calls the “Fast Energization Process.” New power plants and large electricity users would be studied together in one 12-month process. Today generation is studied separately in three rounds, and the full process takes about two years. A “large load” is defined as 50 megawatts or more at one site, which covers big data centres. They would have to stay connected through routine grid disturbances. In July, about 3.9 gigawatts of data-centre load dropped off at once.

Cost is the other half. PJM would publish the upgrade costs caused by each large load. In a companion filing (ER27-520), the transmission owners propose that large loads sign cost-recovery agreements of at least ten years and pay a minimum of 80–100% of a firm-service benchmark, with upgrade charges assigned to the zones where the data centres are, over 15 years.

Why it matters: this is where AI spending turns into household power bills. The plan aims to make data centres, not every ratepayer, pay for the wires they need, and to bring new power plants online sooner.

What to watch: PJM has asked for an extended 60-day comment period. Watch state regulators’ responses, data-centre developers’ objections to the minimum charges, and FERC’s ruling.

Divergence — how fast is “fast”?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. PJM’s own summary and DCD lead with the cut from two years to one. Load Factor News, reading the same filing, leads with the start date. The new process cannot begin until the current Cycle 01 clears its second decision point, which PJM expects in late November 2027. Applications close in August 2028, so the first fast cycle ends in 2029.

Why they differ. Both are accurate. PJM is describing how long the process takes once it runs; the specialist outlet is describing when anyone first benefits. PJM wants the headline about speed to reach states that are angry about prices. A trade reader who builds projects cares about the date they can plug in.

What it means for you. For bills over the next two years, this filing changes little. The cost-allocation half, in which data centres pay by zone, could matter sooner if FERC accepts the transmission owners’ filing on its own schedule. What would settle it: FERC’s order, and whether it lets the cost rules start before the new study process does.

The Labor Department finalises a rule that makes it easier to classify workers as independent contractors, replacing the 2024 test, effective in January

US politics & policy New subject Single-sourced

On Thursday, the Labor Department released a final rule replacing the Biden-era test for deciding whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor under federal wage law, Reuters reported. Under the 2024 rule, any one of several factors could tip a worker into employee status. The new test, favoured by business groups, puts the weight on core factors, starting with whether the company controls the work. It takes effect 60 days after formal publication, in January.

Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling said rules must keep up with how Americans now choose to work. The department called the 2024 rule legally flawed.

Why it matters: employees get the minimum wage and overtime; contractors do not. Trucking, home health care, retail sales, and app-based ride and delivery companies all rely on contractors. These firms face constant lawsuits over misclassification, and a looser federal test makes those suits harder to win. State laws with stricter tests, such as California’s, are untouched.

What to watch: lawsuits from labor groups once the rule is published; whether the new Democratic Congress tries to overturn it under the Congressional Review Act, which needs the president’s signature; and how many workers the department estimates will be reclassified. The label is Single-sourced: one wire account, because the department’s press page refused automated access.

The SEC proposes taking direct control of the Consolidated Audit Trail, the trade database Wall Street sued over, and says Congress should pay for it

US financials New subject Single-sourced

On Friday the SEC proposed changing how the Consolidated Audit Trail is run and paid for, Bloomberg reported. The CAT is the huge database that brokers and exchanges must report every US stock and options trade to. Under the plan, firms would report directly to the SEC or to an entity it names. Today an exchange-run operator holds the data. Chairman Paul Atkins said the aim is to put the CAT on a “sustainable footing.” Funding would have to be appropriated by Congress.

The background is cost and litigation. The CAT was built after the 2010 flash crash to help regulators trace trading abuse. Atkins put projected costs at about $250m a year before changes he says have cut operating costs by more than $100m. Under the Biden-era SEC, brokers were made to pay much of the bill. Citadel Securities and an industry group sued, and a 2025 court ruling struck down the funding model.

Why it matters: the SEC would be the direct keeper of a record of nearly all US trading. That raises privacy and security questions after this month’s breach of the FBI’s crime database. It also asks a new Democratic Congress to pay for a database the Republican SEC chair is reshaping.

What to watch: the comment period; how much of the cost brokers still carry while Congress decides; and whether appropriators take it up.

Iran vows “all available means” against the US blockade and may send a tanker convoy, but Brent slips under $92 and the US pump average falls to $3.64 before Thanksgiving

Geopolitics Follow-up Independent corroboration

This is one causal chain, from the strait to the pump. On Monday Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baqaei, said Iran will use all means at its disposal against the US naval blockade of its ports, AP reported via CBS. He called the sanctions and blockade military aggression that gives Iran a right of self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter. Iran says Hormuz could be open within two days if Washington meets its demands, including lifting the blockade. Fox News reported that Iran is preparing a convoy of more than a dozen tankers to run the blockade from Kharg Island. Trump said that if they try, they will end up “at the bottom of the sea.”

Over the weekend, UKMTO reported several attacks on shipping in the Gulf and near the strait. The Houthis said they hit a Saudi refinery, Reuters reported. Prices still fell. Brent was down 60 cents to $91.97 by 3:44am ET, and WTI was at $82.92. Brent has fallen three weeks running.

The pump: AAA’s national average for regular is $3.637 on Monday, down from $3.645 on Sunday and $3.72 a week ago. AAA says Thanksgiving Day could still come in under the $3.58 record from 2022, if the decline continues.

Why it matters: the reader’s fuel bill is falling even as the war at sea gets worse. That holds only while tankers keep getting through. A real convoy confrontation is the one event that could reverse it within days.

What to watch: whether any tanker leaves Kharg; US Navy statements; and Wednesday’s EIA weekly data.

Divergence — is the strait getting more dangerous or less?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. The security reporting (UKMTO counts, Iran’s threats, the convoy) says risk is rising. The oil market says supply is fine. A PVM analyst told Reuters the pressure on prices is coming from the supply side, and Brent has fallen three weeks running.

Why they differ. Both are looking at real things. Ship attacks raise insurance costs and delay single voyages, but oil is still arriving, through rerouted cargoes and record US exports. Traders price barrels delivered, not incidents. The convoy report rests on unnamed sources reported by Fox, and a UANI adviser it quoted called the plan “farcical.”

What it means for you. Trust the price for now. Barrels are getting through, and your pump price should keep easing into Thursday. Treat the convoy as a tail risk, not a forecast. What would settle it: tankers actually leaving Kharg, or a US interception. Either would show up in Brent within hours.

Florida & Tampa Bay: the attorney general sues Leapfrog, the hospital-grading group, saying its safety grades misled patients; a federal judge has already found its method unfair

Florida & Tampa Bay New subject Single-sourced

Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier sued The Leapfrog Group and its chief executive, Leah Binder, on Friday in Seminole County, WUSF reported. Leapfrog is a nonprofit whose letter grades for hospital safety are widely quoted in hospital marketing and local news. The suit says Leapfrog called its grades expert-backed and peer-reviewed when they were not. It says hospitals that refused Leapfrog’s voluntary survey were given the worst scores without data. It also says the group used the ratings to raise donations without proper registration in Florida. Uthmeier says patients turned down surgery because of the grades.

Uthmeier is not starting from scratch. In March, after a five-day trial, US District Judge Donald Middlebrooks ruled for five Tenet hospitals in Palm Beach County. He found that Leapfrog penalised hospitals that skipped the survey by giving them the lowest scores on some measures, and that this was deceptive and unfair under Florida’s consumer law. Two days before the suit, 43 Florida hospitals had been given straight-A grades by Leapfrog.

Why it matters locally: Pasco and Pinellas families use these grades to choose hospitals, often for planned surgery. If the state wins, the grades could be limited or carry warnings in Florida. Hospitals that skip the survey are the most likely to see their grades change.

What to watch: Leapfrog’s response, which was not in the account read; whether Bayfront, AdventHealth or BayCare hospitals are named; and any injunction request. The label is Single-sourced: one newsroom, quoting the AG’s release and the March ruling.

Their lawyer says Ricardo Larrondo Mendoza and his daughter are asylum seekers and were flying to a family baptism in California (NBC, El País). Governor Bob Ferguson said he would ask every airline at SeaTac to call on ICE to leave. Seattle’s mayor called the tactics un-American. DHS and Democratic officials argued publicly, Fox News reported.

The governor’s request has no legal force over federal agents; it is pressure on the airlines, not a rule.

Below the line because it is one enforcement case with no policy change behind it so far; it would rise if DHS sets a new airport policy or an airline acts.

US financials The CFTC has sued Louisiana and Arkansas to stop them from shutting down federally regulated prediction markets. Louisiana is the 12th state it has sued.

A Louisiana state court on Thursday ordered more than 30 companies linked to trading CFTC-regulated contracts to stop operating in the state, without a hearing, the CFTC says. Chairman Michael Selig called the state’s emergency suit “naked lawfare.” Arkansas is bringing a state enforcement case against Kalshi that could carry hundreds of millions of dollars in penalties. The CFTC says federal law preempts both.

This is the same fight this brief followed in New York last week, now spreading. The Senate’s DEATH BETS Act, which bars contracts on war and death, is a separate federal track.

Below the line because the outcome rests with the federal courts, and these suits are one step in a process already being tracked.

Primary source CFTC release, Louisiana · CFTC release, Arkansas

US politics & policy Democrats are projected to hold at least 258 House seats after Chuck Hubbard beat Rep. Virginia Foxx in a North Carolina recount, the biggest majority either party has held this century.

NBC projects Hubbard won North Carolina’s 5th district by a fraction of a point. Trump carried the district by almost 18 points in 2024. Foxx conceded on Sunday. Florida & Tampa Bay Florida’s Cabinet approved rewording of what the state Hurricane Catastrophe Fund will repay insurers for living expenses after a storm. Some in the industry fear it narrows coverage; the fund says it does not.

The Cabinet voted on 17 November to define “additional living expenses” as reasonable temporary costs, beyond normal spending, when a covered event makes a home unlivable or a civil authority bars its use, Insurance Journal reported. The contract would drop its explicit wording on the 40%-of-insured-value cap and point to the statute that sets the cap instead. The fund’s legal director, Mary Linzee Branham, said the change only adds a definition.

The Cat Fund backs much of Florida’s private and Citizens market, so its reimbursement terms feed into reinsurance costs and, in the end, premiums in Pasco and Pinellas.

Below the line because the fund says nothing changes in practice, and no insurer has yet put a number on it.
