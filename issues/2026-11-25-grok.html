The White House is about to rebuild Trump’s struck-down global tariffs: new Section 301 duties on up to 16 economies, held to the caps in existing trade deals

US politics & policy New Independent corroboration

The Trade Representative is expected to release the findings of a Section 301 probe into “excess industrial capacity” and propose new import duties, three people told Reuters. The BBC says the announcement could come as early as today. The duties would come on top of tariffs already in place.

The probe covers China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Switzerland, Norway, Singapore, Bangladesh and Cambodia (Reuters). Rates would be set country by country. One source said existing deal ceilings would hold: 15% for the EU, Japan and South Korea, 19% for Malaysia, and the 20% cap on China while the truce lasts. That truce’s interim deal expires on 10 January (BBC). Mexico and Norway are unlikely to face immediate duties.

How it is known: AP and Reuters each had their own sources, and the BBC its own briefing. The White House and USTR did not comment. There is no document yet.

Why it matters: this is the legal replacement for the “Liberation Day” tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February. It runs alongside a forced-labour probe of 59 countries plus the EU, which already produced 10% and 12.5% duties in July (Reuters via BusinessLine). Section 301 has held up in court before, so these duties would be harder to undo. The reports landed during Tuesday’s selloff (next story).

What to watch: the USTR notice itself, with rates, exemptions and an effective date. Also any Chinese retaliation before the 10 January truce date.

Divergence — is it 14 economies or 16, and is it today?

What is disputed. A facts dispute, small but real. Reuters and AP say 16. The BBC says “at least 14”. The BBC says “as early as Wednesday”; AP says “in the coming days” and stressed the timing could change.

Why they differ. Reuters names 16 economies in the probe but says Mexico and Norway are unlikely to face immediate duties. That leaves 14 actually hit. The two counts measure different things. On timing, each outlet reports what its sources expect, and none has a document.

What it means for you. Read it as 16 investigated, about 14 tariffed at first. Expect it this week rather than on a fixed day. The USTR notice settles both points.

Dig deeper — how the caps would work

Reuters’ source said the White House would honour the ceilings in finalised trade deals. That would leave most allies roughly where they are now, at least at first. The open question is China. Its rate stays at the 20% cap only “as long as” the truce is extended; if it lapses, a higher excess-capacity rate would apply.

The probe also targets Chinese investment in Vietnam, Indonesia and Cambodia, which builds goods for the US market and avoids earlier tariffs (Reuters). The Taipei Times expects Taiwan’s rate to stay capped at 15%.

For US buyers, the July forced-labour duties are already in place, and these would come on top. The legal mechanism matters as much as the rate. A Section 301 action follows notice and comment, which makes it slower to impose and harder to challenge than the emergency-powers tariffs the Court rejected.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com · AP · BBC · Reuters via BusinessLine · DW India live

Chip stocks fall 7.9% in a day and Nasdaq futures are down about 2%, with Anthropic below its IPO price, as GDP and PCE land at 8:30

Macro & markets Follow-up Independent corroboration

Monday’s Nvidia rally reversed on Tuesday. The S&P 500 fell 0.50% to 7,201.09 and the Nasdaq 1.48% to 23,733.54. The Dow rose 0.85% to 50,018.70. Tech fell 2.8% and a semiconductor index 7.9%. Nvidia lost 5.9% (Reuters via NBC). Anthropic closed below its $83 IPO price for the first time, four sessions after listing (CNBC, MarketWatch).

Before the open, futures were down further. Nasdaq contracts were off about 2% (MarketWatch, Reuters), and chip maker Ambarella was down almost 40% premarket after weak guidance. SoftBank fell as much as 15% in Tokyo (CNBC).

Treasury’s 10-year closed Tuesday at 4.91%, down from 4.92% (Treasury par curve). The 30-year was 5.30%.

Why it matters: this is a heavy data day before Thanksgiving. At 8:30 ET the Commerce Department publishes the second estimate of third-quarter GDP and corporate profits, and October personal income and spending, which includes PCE inflation, the Fed’s preferred gauge (BEA schedule). Census’s October durable-goods orders are also due today. September orders rose 1.8%. So is October new-home sales, after September’s 676,000 annual rate (Census). This edition went to press before those prints, so none is used here.

What to watch: the PCE print against a market already pricing more Fed hikes, and whether the tariff notice (lead 1) lands during trading.

Divergence — what is driving the selloff: tariffs or doubts about AI?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. Reuters’ morning futures story has a web address that blames “fresh US tariff concerns”. Its current headline says “AI losses deepen”. A Cetera strategist called Tuesday ordinary profit-taking after the biggest rally in months (via NBC). CNBC points to “key-man risk” at Nvidia after Huang said he still sees no need for a succession plan.

Why they differ. The causes are timed together. The tariff reports landed Tuesday, the same day Nvidia gave back gains. Headline writers pick the newest catalyst. Strategists who own the stocks lean towards profit-taking.

What it means for you. The evidence favours AI doubts as the main driver. Chips fell 7.9% while the Dow rose, and a broad tariff shock would hit industrials too. Ambarella and Anthropic fell on their own news. The tariff notice is the test: if industrials and importers sell off once rates are public, tariffs become the second leg.

Hackers got into FinCEN, the Treasury office that holds banks’ suspicious-activity reports, hours after an unclassified CIA system was breached

US financials New Primary source

FinCEN says it is investigating “unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems.” It has taken some systems offline. The notice sits at the top of the Bank Secrecy Act e-filing site, the portal banks use to file suspicious-activity and large-cash reports. Filing still works, but some services may be unavailable. FinCEN is extending deadlines for affected filers and pointing urgent reports to a phone hotline (FinCEN notice).

The Verge first reported the notice on Tuesday, and The Record dates it to Tuesday evening. Neither Treasury nor FinCEN has said what was reached or who did it. The Record notes that Chinese state-backed hackers broke into Treasury in 2024.

Earlier on Tuesday, The New York Times reported a breach of an unclassified CIA system that handles open-source intelligence. A person familiar told AP that no classified information was involved and the problem was fixed. The Verge reports that the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency was also hit; the Pentagon declined to comment. The administration has said this month’s outages were not a coordinated attack (AP).

Why it matters: FinCEN holds the most sensitive financial data in government: every suspicious-activity report a bank files. If those were reached, the exposure covers named customers and live investigations.

What to watch: whether FinCEN says filed reports were accessed, and how long the deadline extension runs. Also whether the CIA, NGA and FinCEN incidents are tied to one actor.

Sources: FinCEN BSA E-Filing notice (primary) · The Verge · The Record · ABC News · AP via Federal News Network (CIA)

Intelligence officials tell Congress the woman in the Huang affair did pass information to China; the attorney general won’t say what he cleared; and export agents were guarding Lutnick

AI & tech industry Follow-up Independent corroboration

On Tuesday, FBI, CIA and ODNI officials briefed House and Senate intelligence committee staff on the case. That was the day after Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang (yesterday’s lead). The briefers found no evidence China’s Ministry of State Security told Alice Huang to start the affair. They did not dispute that she passed it information afterwards. They said the man introduced as her husband, Kang Wei, was the more significant asset. Staff were not shown a classified 30 March memo both committees have asked for (New York Times, via Fortune and Quartz).

At a news conference, Attorney General Todd Blanche declined to say whether Huang had ever been investigated. Asked about the export-control probe, he first said he did not know what the reporter meant, then called it “not new information” (ABC). Twelve days earlier he had called that probe ongoing. He called Nvidia “a good corporate citizen.”

The Atlantic reports that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick used agents from the Bureau of Industry and Security, the chip-export enforcers, as bodyguards on his jet and at his homes. Usually three or four were on duty at a time, out of fewer than 200 (via IBTimes, Mediaite). Commerce says the details were temporary and followed record threats.

Why it matters: Huang’s reinstatement rested on official assurances. The intelligence picture is murkier than those assurances, and the office meant to stop chip smuggling is thin.

What to watch: Huang’s closed-door House interview on 1 December and his Senate Banking testimony on 3 December.

Divergence — is there an open investigation touching Huang or not?

What is disputed. A facts dispute. Blanche now plays down any investigation, and the White House told Nvidia’s board Huang faced no charges (ABC, citing sources). But on 12 November Blanche himself said the export-control probe was ongoing. The Justice Department leads the Megaspeed criminal case, and Alice Huang ran Megaspeed.

Why they differ. These are not the same claim. “Huang faces no charges” is about one person. “The export probe is ongoing” is about Megaspeed and chip diversion. Blanche stood with a president who had vouched for Huang a day earlier. That explains the hedging better than any change in the facts. Separately, Commerce says the agents were a temporary emergency detail, while The Atlantic’s five sources describe a standing arrangement.

What it means for you. The evidence supports this reading: the Megaspeed probe is live, and nothing public shows Huang is a target. A filing in the Megaspeed case, or Huang’s answers under oath on 3 December, would settle it. On the agents, the number and dates are unknown. Commerce could settle that by publishing them.

A Brooklyn jury convicts Huawei of trade-secret theft, bank fraud and sanctions evasion, but acquits it of racketeering, the charge that tied the case together

US politics & policy New Independent corroboration

After a two-month trial, jurors found Huawei and three subsidiaries guilty on 10 of 12 counts on Tuesday (BBC). These include conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank and wire fraud, money laundering, conspiracy to defraud the United States and conspiracy to violate US sanctions on Iran (NBC, AP). Huawei was acquitted of racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice. Sentencing is set for 14 September; the company faces fines, forfeiture and restitution (NBC).

Prosecutors said the technology taken included source code, router manuals, antenna and robot-testing technology. The companies named as victims include T-Mobile, Cisco, Quintel and CNEX (NBC). Attorney General Todd Blanche said Huawei ran its US business “on the basis of lies and theft” (BBC). Huawei says it will appeal.

How it is known: AP, the BBC and NBC each covered the verdict and agree on the counts.

Why it matters: the case dates from the 2019 indictment and Meng Wanzhou’s 2018 arrest in Canada. It ends with a criminal judgment against China’s national champion, in the week the US readies new tariffs and fights chip diversion. Huawei is already barred from selling to US carriers, so the direct commercial effect is small. The legal record is what matters for sanctions and allies’ procurement.

What to watch: the size of the fine at sentencing and whether Beijing ties the verdict to trade talks before 10 January.

Divergence — a sweeping win, or a rejection of the government’s theory?

What is disputed. A significance dispute; the counts are agreed. Prosecutors call it proof of two decades of “fraud, theft and deception” (Assistant AG Duva, via NBC). Huawei’s lawyer says acquittal on racketeering, the one count tying the allegations together, “repudiated the central government narrative” (AP).

Why they differ. Each side is describing the part of the verdict that helps it. Racketeering required the jury to find Huawei operated as a criminal enterprise. Jurors convicted on the specific schemes but would not go that far.

What it means for you. Both are partly right. The specific crimes, including sanctions evasion, are now established in a US court, which is what affects banks and regulators. The company-as-criminal-enterprise claim is not. The appeal is the next test; the convictions stand meanwhile.

Sources: AP via PBS · BBC · NBC News

Amazon says Chinese-linked hackers set AI agents loose with one instruction, “obtain shell access”, and the agents found and used a zero-day on their own

AI & tech industry New Primary source

Amazon’s threat-intelligence team published the case on its security blog. On 10 September it found a public, password-free server running Cairn, an open-source AI penetration-testing framework. Its project files, prompts and AI output were visible to anyone. The prompts were in simplified Chinese, and script timestamps were set to China Standard Time.

Over about 48 hours from 8 September, the operator gave the agents a list of IP addresses at East Asian universities, outside mainland China, and the goal of getting a shell. The agents planned, ran and adjusted their own attacks. In one case they found an unknown flaw in an open-source medical-imaging AI application and took full control of the server (Amazon).

Ars Technica adds that Cairn is model-agnostic and runs on back ends including Claude Code and Codex. Palo Alto Networks has seen it used with Chinese models GLM and DeepSeek. Earlier this month Palo Alto reported 25 autonomous operations against at least 19 organisations in semiconductors, defence and civil society.

Why it matters: it is a documented case of AI agents finding and exploiting a new vulnerability with minimal human direction. That is the capability lab safety teams have warned about, now in criminal use with commercial and open models. It lands in a month of US government breaches (lead 3), though nothing links this campaign to them.

What to watch: whether labs whose models were used say anything about detection or account bans.

Sources: Amazon Threat Intelligence (primary) · Ars Technica

Bank profits fell 5.9% in the third quarter to $84.8bn as losses on bond holdings rose; the FDIC says capital and liquidity stay strong

US financials New Primary source

The FDIC’s Quarterly Banking Profile, released on Tuesday, covers 4,205 insured banks and thrifts. Third-quarter net income was $84.8bn, down $5.3bn (5.9%) from the second quarter but up 7.0% on the year. Return on assets was 1.28%.

Margins widened. The industry’s net interest margin rose 7 basis points to 3.39%. Deposits grew 0.9%, a ninth straight quarterly rise. Loans grew 1.9% on the quarter and 7.5% on the year. Past-due and nonaccrual rates edged up while net charge-offs edged down. The deposit-insurance fund’s reserve ratio rose 2 basis points to 1.50%.

The weak spot is bonds. The FDIC says unrealized losses on securities rose but remain well below the 2023 peak; it gives the figure in its charts, not the release. American Banker’s headline puts the rise at 48%. With the 10-year near 4.9% (Treasury), the bonds banks bought when rates were low are worth less, the same exposure that sank Silicon Valley Bank in 2023.

Why it matters: this is the regulator’s own scorecard for the core financial beat. Lending is growing fast while bond losses rise. More Fed hikes would make that pairing worse.

What to watch: the FDIC’s chart pack for the dollar figure, and whether any bank reports deposit outflows tied to unrealized losses.

Divergence — strong system, or growing hole?

What is disputed. A significance dispute about the same data. The FDIC leads with strong capital and liquidity and calls asset quality stable. American Banker leads with lower earnings and a 48% jump in unrealized losses.

Why they differ. The FDIC’s release is written to reassure depositors, and it measures losses against the 2023 peak. A trade paper for bankers measures them against last quarter. Both are accurate.

What it means for you. Read it as stable today, more exposed if rates rise again. The number that would change the picture is unrealized losses as a share of capital at mid-sized banks. That is in the FDIC’s data tables, which this brief has not yet read.

Sources: FDIC press release (primary) · American Banker · Treasury par yield curve (primary)

The Pentagon is weighing a loan of up to $15bn, about twice the company’s value, to a factory startup backed by Donald Trump Jr.’s fund

US politics & policy Follow-up Single-sourced

ProPublica reports, from interviews and Pentagon documents, that the Defense Department is considering lending up to $15bn to Hadrian Automation. The California startup uses robots and AI to make precision parts. The money would build a dozen automated factories for munitions, drones and other equipment. That would be about ten times the Pentagon’s largest loan in its recent lending push, and nearly double Hadrian’s value at its summer funding round.

1789 Capital, where Trump Jr. has been a partner since late 2024, took part in that round and holds what it calls a minority stake. Pentagon staff raised concerns about whether Hadrian can hire and expand fast enough. Former officials say the government would end up lending a contractor money to repay with Pentagon contracts.

The Pentagon says political connections play “absolutely no role” in funding decisions, the talks are early, and the figure is not $15bn. A White House official says Hadrian approached the Pentagon (via ProPublica and Hindustan Times).

Why it matters: this follows Monday’s letters from Rep. Jamie Raskin to four 1789-backed firms, Hadrian among them (yesterday’s short item). It would be a very large taxpayer exposure to a young contractor, decided weeks before Democrats take both chambers.

What to watch: any term sheet or approval before 3 January, and whether the new House majority subpoenas the loan file.

Divergence — is it a $15bn loan or not?

What is disputed. A facts dispute. ProPublica cites Pentagon documents laying out financing terms and risks for up to $15bn. The Pentagon disputes the figure, calls the talks “early stages,” and says multibillion-dollar deals with other firms have been agreed but not announced.

Why they differ. ProPublica has the documents; the Pentagon has the decision and an interest in making the loan look routine. Both can be true: a $15bn ceiling in planning papers, with no figure yet agreed.

What it means for you. Treat $15bn as the size under consideration, not a decision. Publication of the other unannounced multibillion deals would show whether this one is unusual. So would a signed term sheet.

Sources: ProPublica · Hindustan Times (Pentagon response)

Florida & Tampa Bay: Florida’s SNAP error rate is rising despite a $4m AI fix, and from October 2027 a high rate could put the state on the hook for 15% of food costs

Florida & Tampa Bay New Single-sourced

Preliminary monthly reports obtained by the Florida Phoenix show SNAP payment errors rising. This year the Legislature gave the Department of Children and Families $4m to hire a vendor and use machine learning to cut errors. DCF signed with Executive Information Solutions on 11 September, according to the department’s October report.

The stakes come from the 2025 federal tax-and-spending law. From October 2027, states with error rates of 6% or more must pay part of food benefits. Florida’s official 2025 rate was 12.97% (USDA), which would mean about 15% of benefit costs. The state already pays more for administration, about $50m a year, $38m of it budgeted this year. DCF’s request for 2027–28 has a $1 placeholder for food costs (Phoenix).

There is a twist. States above 13.3% get a delay to 2030, so a slightly worse 2026 rate could postpone the bill. On Friday, USDA let states recalculate error rates after a September court ruling (Phoenix).

Why it matters locally: 2.2 million Floridians were on SNAP in July, down 22% on the year. Pasco and Pinellas households feel any benefit cuts, and Tallahassee will fight over the cost-share.

What to watch: the 2026 rate, final in June, and whether the January session budgets for the cost-share.

Divergence — is the state fixing SNAP errors or not?

What is disputed. A significance dispute. Governor-elect Byron Donalds says the state is doing the work to remove waste and fraud (via Phoenix). The Phoenix says the monthly data show the opposite trend.

Why they differ. Donalds is describing intent and the new vendor contract. The Phoenix is describing preliminary numbers that are not final until June. Enrolment fell 22% in a year, which changes what errors are measured against.

What it means for you. The data on record favour the Phoenix: the rate is rising for now. Because preliminary rates are revised, the June final figure settles it. Under the 13.3% rule, a higher rate could even delay what the state owes.

Sources: Florida Phoenix
