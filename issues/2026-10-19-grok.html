Anthropic commits 5% of its shares to a public-benefit fund that grows with the company — subject to stockholder approval

AI & tech US financials New fact Conflict disclosed

Conflict disclosure

Anthropic makes Claude. This brief’s Grok editions still cover Anthropic on the Core AI beat under the same rule as prior issues: never soften coverage; disclose the industry conflict; source externally and to the primary. Holding the story would itself be an editorial choice.

The commitment (Sunday primary). Anthropic said it will commit 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, a wholly owned LLC, if stockholders approve. The board may add another 2% at its discretion for every 50% increase in company value above an unpublished baseline. Combined with co-founder pledges (80% of their equity) and an employee equity-donation match (~half of staff), Anthropic says “roughly 20%” of shares will be dedicated to the public good, plus any milestone add-ons (anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund, read).

What the fund can and cannot do. The company frames the LLC — not a 501(c)(3) — as giving “adjustability” between large nonprofit donations and “direct distributions to specific populations,” naming labour-market impacts as an example alongside an existing $200 million commitment to study and address them. “None of the Anthropic equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving, in the US or abroad.” Governance details, voting rights of the fund’s shares, and the valuation baseline are promised “in the coming months,” with a public-benefit report also forthcoming.

Market context (not in the post). On a $965 billion valuation tied to the May $65 billion raise (secondary reporting), 5% is roughly $48 billion of paper — a figure Anthropic’s post does not print. OpenAI’s foundation arrangement (foundation controls the group) is the structural foil: here the company would own the fund that holds the shares. Listing chatter in early October pointed at marketing as soon as the week of 9 Nov (etf.net / wires, secondary).

What to watch: stockholder vote date; whether fund shares vote; the unpublished baseline; how this sits beside last week’s chip-financing / IPO-valuation pressure covered in Issue 44.

OPM kills Time-in-Grade for the General Schedule — effective 19 Jan 2027

US policy Underreported Primary

The rule (FR 2026-21319, read). Final rule eliminating the Time-in-Grade (TIG) restriction on advancement into competitive-service General Schedule positions. Under the prior 5 CFR part 300 subpart F rule, a candidate generally could not advance to a covered GS-6+ job without 52 weeks in a position no more than one grade lower (two grades for some two-grade-interval lines). That requirement is removed. Occupational qualification standards and agency job-related requirements remain. Effective 19 January 2027. OPM says it adopts the 28 May 2026 proposal (91 FR 31669) substantially as proposed; conforming amendments hit 5 CFR parts 315, 316, 352 and 410.

Companion, same day. FR 2026-21320 finalises administrative-leave rules for workforce realignment, deferred-resignation programs, and RIF notice periods — effective 18 November 2026 (read opening; 30 comments on the June proposal).

Why it clears the bar. TIG has structured federal promotion timing for decades. Removing it is a structural HR rewrite for the competitive service, not a personnel anecdote — and no reachable wire carried it this morning. The repetition tell did not fire; the dull-title / near-term-effective-date tell did.
