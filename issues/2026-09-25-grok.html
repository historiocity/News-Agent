Senate rejected the Iran war-powers resolution 49–50 — it was July’s text, and a tie fails

Senate roll call 244, 24 Sep, 1:45pm ET: on adoption of H. Con. Res. 89 (Iran war powers) — YEAs 49, NAYs 50, Not Voting 1 (Alsobrooks). Concurrent resolution rejected. Required for majority: 1/2. A 49–50 is not “one vote short of a Vice-Presidential tie-break”; a concurrent resolution needs a majority of those voting, and 49 is short of 50. The Vice President does not break this kind of tie into passage.

Four Republicans voted Yea: Collins, Murkowski, Paul, Tillis. One Democrat voted Nay: Fetterman. The House had passed 89 on 23 Jul by 214–208 (roll call 282). A stronger House text — H. Con. Res. 93 (Moulton), 220–204 on 15 Sep — has not received a Senate floor vote. Privilege clocks, not substance, explain why July’s weaker count is the one the Senate answered.

As a concurrent resolution it does not go to the President and does not bind under INS v. Chadha. The binding route remains a joint resolution subject to veto. Nineteen US service members dead; diesel and petrol still the domestic price of the war — see the oil chain below.

What to watch: whether 93 ever gets a floor date; a Republican senator saying the legal position has changed (not merely that the war is unwise); any joint-resolution or appropriations limitation.

Dig deeper — why July’s resolution, and why 49–50 is not almost a win

War-powers concurrent resolutions become privileged on a schedule tied to receipt. 89 has sat since July; 93 arrived ten days ago. Leadership had no incentive to schedule the stronger text after the weaker one failed. Party split on the Senate roll call: the Yea list is almost the full Democratic caucus plus two independents and four Republicans; the Nay list is almost the full Republican caucus plus Fetterman. Check subtotals against the chamber (53 R / 47 D) before trusting a summariser’s party arithmetic — the standing 25 Sep discipline note after a prior wrong subtotal.

DOT final rule: race and sex DBE presumptions deleted; contract goals suspended until firms re-apply

Transportation Department final rule 2026-19688, published and effective 25 Sep, finalises the October 2025 interim final rule that eliminated race- and sex-based presumptions in the Disadvantaged Business Enterprise and Airport Concession DBE programmes. Every firm must now show social and economic disadvantage through an individualised personal narrative. Contract goals are suspended while Unified Certification Programs re-evaluate rosters; reevaluations must be complete by 24 December 2026 (one possible three-month extension). Non-responsive firms get a final written notice path with a 90-day grace to March 2027 then automatic disqualification.

Legal scaffolding cited in the rule: Mid-America Milling (E.D. Ky. 2024 preliminary injunction), Students for Fair Admissions, Executive Orders 14151 and 14173, DOJ/Solicitor General refusal to defend the presumptions. DOT received 637 comments on the IFR. Clarifications today: “similarly situated” → “non-disadvantaged individuals with comparable qualifications”; race/sex may appear in a narrative as factual experience but cannot be a presumption; Alaska Native Corporations / Tribes / NHOs keep a distinct statutory track; no automatic SBA 8(a) reciprocity.

This is federally aided highway, transit and airport contracting — most public construction money in the country has a DBE clause. For three months the programme runs without numerical goals.

What to watch: how many certified firms fail to re-certify by 24 Dec; state DOT litigation over the goal suspension; whether SBA’s 8(a) programme mirrors the narrative structure.

Dig deeper — why the FR sweep produced this lead

No reachable wire front page carried the final rule this morning. It appeared by reading the day’s RULE/PRORULE list and then the full-text endpoint — the standing FR discipline. The abstract alone does not convey the 24 Dec deadline or the goal suspension; the full text does. Estimated one-time transitional cost in the rule’s own analysis: roughly $95 million (narratives + UCP reevaluation), treated by DOT as outweighed by constitutional-compliance benefits.

OMB wants the ASPR 86 essential medicines made domestic or near-shore within eighteen months

OMB request for information 2026-19646, published 25 Sep: market research on new and idle domestic and near-shore capacity for finished dose forms, APIs and key starting materials on the ASPR 86 list of essential medicines. Responses due 26 October 2026. Stated objective: secure domestic production of those medicines and precursors within eighteen months, and reduce dependence on “Foreign Entities of Concern” (China, Russia, Iran, North Korea). Also asks about amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin, not on the 86 list, for supply-chain vulnerability. Feedback may shape near-term federal acquisition, including limited competition for industrial mobilisation.

Most essential-medicine APIs and KSMs are not made in the US. This is industrial-base policy with a dated clock, not a study for its own sake.

What to watch: which molecules attract idle-capacity responses; any follow-on FAR or offtake instrument; whether antibiotics outside the 86 list get pulled into a later action.

New Core · AI & tech Underreported

ITC institutes DRAM Section 337 case naming Micron, HPE, Lenovo and Super Micro

US International Trade Commission instituted investigation 337-TA-1523 on complaint of Netlist (filed 11 Aug): alleged infringement of four patents covering DDR5 RDIMM/MRDIMM technology by Micron Technology, Micron Semiconductor Products, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Lenovo (Group and US) and Super Micro Computer. Netlist seeks a limited exclusion order and cease-and-desist orders — i.e., a potential import ban on the accused memory products. Target date for completion to be set within 45 days of institution. Remedial orders, if any, face a 60-day Presidential/USTR review.

This is not a liability finding. It is an expedited ITC track that can wall off US imports of the very DRAM that seats AI servers. Super Micro and HPE sit in the data-centre build; Micron is the US memory champion. Physical constraint on AI capex, reached from the FR NOTICE list — no thread pointed here.

What to watch: target date; any early settlement with Samsung-style licensing (Netlist’s own framing); USTR disposition if an exclusion order issues.

Sources: FR NOTICE 2026-19697 (institution) · Netlist / PR Newswire 24 Sep · Bloomberg Law summary

New York sued Polymarket for illegal gambling; Polymarket sued New York the same day

NY Attorney General Letitia James and Governor Hochul filed in state court on 24 Sep accusing QCX LLC d/b/a Polymarket US of running an unlicensed gambling operation — prediction markets on sports and “markets on everything,” available to 18–20-year-olds despite New York’s 21+ mobile-sports rule, without a Gaming Commission licence or the associated tax base. Relief sought: injunction, forfeiture, restitution, treble fines. The same afternoon Polymarket sued James and state officials in Manhattan federal court to block state regulation, arguing prediction markets sit under federal CFTC derivatives oversight.

This is the same State theory used against Kalshi, Coinbase and Gemini earlier in 2026 — now aimed at the highest-profile event-contract venue. CFTC has been publicly supportive of prediction markets as a category while, separately, reporting described an ongoing marketing investigation into Polymarket. The live legal question is whether a state can regulate a CFTC-facing prediction market at all.

What to watch: temporary restraining order practice in either court; CFTC statement or filing; whether other AGs copy New York.

Sources: NY AG press release (primary) · AP / PBS · CNA on Polymarket countersuit
