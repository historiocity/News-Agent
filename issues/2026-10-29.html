"This Court declines to be a party to such process"

Courts US politics Continuation Independent corroboration

On Monday this brief set out two readings of the Justice Department's Alaska certification suit — ordinary enforcement, or a rehearsal — and said the test would be whether similar filings appeared elsewhere. They have, and the first court to rule has said no.

US District Judge David Carter, a Clinton appointee, denied the Department's emergency request for a temporary restraining order that would have made California stop processing ballots connected to 20,000 people the government says are "likely" noncitizens. The 26-page ruling, issued Wednesday evening, is not written in the register of a procedural denial.

"Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures and bend to the will of the Executive Branch," Carter wrote. And at the close: "Once a vote is segregated, lasting harm has occurred. If the Executive can discount votes and threaten an ongoing election due to unproven allegations, then our democracy is in danger. This Court declines to be a party to such process." He noted the government has an ordinary remedy available — prosecuting people for voting as noncitizens, which the Department says it has done dozens of times in two months.

The list itself is contested on the facts. A preliminary analysis by Los Angeles County found that many of the 20,000 do not have active voter registrations at all, and state and local officials argued that segregating ballots days before an election, with mail voting already under way, would be unlawful, infeasible, and would disenfranchise eligible voters.

The pattern this brief asked for is now three states in two weeks. The California suit was filed 15 October. Last week came the Alaska suit, seeking to block certification of a Senate result if the Republican incumbent loses. On Monday federal prosecutors asked Washington state to review a list of 400 registered voters. That is not a coincidence of duplicated names in one state, which was the reading the Department offered and this brief could not then rule out.

What to watch: whether the Department appeals before 3 November, which is the only way the California ruling gets tested in time to matter; what the Alaska judge does, having been reported as ruling quickly; and whether a fourth state appears.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. Quotations are from Carter's ruling as reported by NPR, which links the order; this brief did not read the 26-page ruling itself, the DOJ filings, or the LA County analysis. Forty thousand Homeland Security identities are in a searchable database

Cyber & security Immigration New subject Independent corroboration

A group calling itself the Department of Peace says it took the Department of Homeland Security's personnel files by subverting the department's single sign-on system, and has published the names, phone numbers and addresses of more than 40,000 DHS agents, operatives and associates — including people involved in undercover operations.

The data went to Distributed Denial of Secrets, which posted it in a database called Icepick, and to the journalist Ryan Ottignon and a Swiss hacker known as maia arson crimew, who together built a searchable front end. More than 21,000 records identify Customs and Border Protection employees; about 10,000 relate to ICE and Homeland Security Investigations. That is roughly a fifth of a workforce of a little over 220,000, and the set is not comprehensive.

The group named its motive and its target. "On July 17th, Markwayne Mullin said he was 'turning up the heat.' This is what happens to ICE when you do that," its spokesperson said — quoting the Homeland Security Secretary, whose signature also appears on the immigration-penalty ratification this brief led on four days ago. DHS said it is "aware of a potential breach and is actively working to address the situation swiftly and effectively."

The people who published it reject the word doxing. Ottignon's argument is that officials who conceal their identities while exercising public power have no claim to anonymity; DDoSecrets cofounder Emma Best called the leak "extraordinary but deceptive in its simplicity," because it links officials overseeing covert operations to personnel in deportation programmes. Other activists say it will make freedom-of-information requests usable, since those typically fail without names.

Whatever the argument, the exposed set includes undercover personnel and home addresses, and that is a physical safety question that does not turn on whether the disclosure is justified. It follows similar attacks on the FBI, which makes a federal pattern rather than an incident.

What to watch: whether DHS confirms the single sign-on vector, which would make this a systems failure rather than a credential theft; whether anyone named is harmed; and whether the department moves staff or suspends operations.

Confidence: Independent corroboration, but every substantive claim about the data originates with the people who published it. NPR spoke to Ottignon; DHS confirmed only that it is aware of a potential breach. This brief did not access the database, cannot verify the record counts, the single sign-on claim, or that undercover personnel are included, and did not try — opening it is not something this brief will do. Sources: NPR · 25 October

The refinery number arrived, and it points away from the inventory story

Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

On 21 October this brief explained the diesel price by a draw in distillate stocks. On 22 October it withdrew that explanation, because the next week's figure showed a build. Yesterday's EIA release, read this morning, gives the week to 23 October: distillate stocks 102.8 million barrels, down 1.46 million, and refinery utilisation 92.8 per cent, down 0.6 points.

Taken alone the stock draw looks like a vindication of the withdrawn claim. It is not, and the reason is the shape of the two series. Distillate stocks over nine weeks: 104.2, 106.3, 107.9, 107.4, 105.2, 105.1, 102.6, 104.3, 102.8. That is oscillation inside a four-million-barrel band with no direction. A series that goes up and down has no trend to explain a price that has gone one way, and a reading that fits one week of it will stop fitting the next — which is exactly what happened a week ago.

Utilisation does have a direction. 98.0 per cent in late August, 92.5 by 25 September, and 92.8 now — a fall of more than five points, held for five weeks, and this week's reading is the lowest since 25 September. Refineries have been making less of everything that comes out of the middle of the barrel since the start of September, and the price of the product has risen while stocks have gone nowhere.

This is a better-supported reading, not a proved one. Utilisation falls for ordinary reasons — autumn maintenance turnarounds happen every year at about this time — and this brief has not established how this autumn compares with previous ones, which is the test that would separate a seasonal dip from a constraint. The lesson of 21 October was not "pick a different series", it was "do not convert one correlation into a mechanism."

At the pump both fuels fell and the gap widened again. Diesel $6.3347, down 1.52 cents; regular $4.2258, down 2.13. The spread is $2.1089 — a fifth consecutive record, now set on a day when diesel itself got cheaper.

What to watch: utilisation against the same weeks in 2024 and 2025, which this brief owes and has not done; whether the 4 November refinery hearing or any Title III award from Wednesday's determination touches throughput; and next Wednesday's release, which now reaches the reader on Thursday.

The methane rule is being rolled back, and the pitch is the price of fuel

Energy & grid Regulatory New subject Independent corroboration

The EPA is proposing to scale back the 2023 rules limiting methane emissions from oil and gas drilling sites. The President announced it while campaigning in Lubbock, Texas, on Tuesday, saying the revision would save the industry billions and spur domestic drilling. The proposal is in today's Federal Register as a reconsideration of the standards of performance for new, reconstructed and modified sources.

Methane is roughly 80 times more warming than carbon dioxide over twenty years, which is why the 2023 rules existed. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin: "Behind every barrel of oil and every cubic foot of natural gas produced in the U.S. are millions of workers, businesses, and the heartbeat of this nation's economy. The Biden EPA destructively targeted all this." The American Petroleum Institute's Dustin Meyer said the industry looks forward to "a more workable approach… that advances continued emissions reductions".

The argument against is economic rather than only environmental, and it is the interesting one. The Environmental Defense Fund's Jon Goldstein puts it simply: "Methane is natural gas, or natural gas is methane. And so if you're capturing more natural gas, you're not allowing it to leak out, you're not allowing it to be flared, you have more product that you can sell. And so that offsets a lot of the costs." A leak-control rule is also a production rule, and the captured volume is saleable.

The politics are stated openly in the reporting: the proposal comes as Republicans face a backlash over high fuel prices going into the midterms. That is the thread this brief has run for six weeks, now appearing as the stated motive for an environmental rollback. Whether it can affect a pump price is a different question: the constraint this brief can actually measure is refinery throughput, in the story above, and nothing in a drilling-site emissions rule touches that.

What to watch: the comment deadline and whether it is short; the EPA's own estimate of forgone gas capture, which is the number that tests Goldstein's argument; and whether any part of the proposal is defended on price grounds in the document rather than on a stage.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. The announcement, quotations and the 80-times figure are NPR's; this brief identified the proposed rule in today's Federal Register by title and did not read it, so the description of its scope rests on the reporting, and no comment deadline is given here because none was read. Sources: NPR · FR 2026-22082

Six peptides the FDA's own scientists opposed are getting a path to the counter

Health Regulatory New subject Independent corroboration

HHS has announced it will exercise enforcement discretion for six peptides made by small compounding pharmacies — that is, it will not act against pharmacies that make them, without changing their legal status. The six, with the uses they are promoted for: BPC-157 (ulcerative colitis), Epitalon (insomnia), KPV (wound healing), MOTS-c (metabolic health), Semax (migraine) and TB-500 (wound healing). None has been through the FDA's approval process.

The disagreement inside the agency is on the record. In July an FDA advisory panel voted in favour of expanded access to these six through compounding pharmacies. At the same meeting, the FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding the existing restrictions. The policy follows the panel. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has publicly supported peptides and said he has used some himself.

The stated rationale is harm reduction: these peptides already circulate on a grey market as "research-use-only" products bought online from overseas suppliers, and a compounding pharmacy is a safer source than that. It is a real argument, and it is the argument for regulating a supply rather than for endorsing a product — the evidence for safety and effectiveness remains, in the reporting's word, sparse.

The practical effect may be small and the reason is structural. The policy covers 503A compounding pharmacies — the small ones making patient-specific preparations — and not 503B outsourcing facilities, which produce at volume. Joshua Fritzler, president of Olympia and Wesley Pharmaceuticals in central Florida, expects to compound one of the six, in small quantities, once his smaller facility is ready: "We won't be able to meet demand… I don't think all the [503]As in the country could meet the demand."

So the likely outcome is a legal channel too small to displace the grey market it was meant to replace, with federal enforcement discretion now attached to products the agency's scientists said should stay restricted.

What to watch: whether 503B facilities are added, which would change the scale entirely; whether adverse-event reporting is required as a condition; and whether any of the six enters an actual approval pathway.

Confidence: Independent corroboration. NPR; this brief did not read the HHS policy document, the July advisory committee record, or the FDA scientists' recommendation, and has not verified the clinical claims attached to any of the six — they are listed as promoted uses, not as effects. Sources: NPR

The long end sold off six days before the refunding

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal Continuation Primary source

Wednesday's Treasury print, read by enumerating the column headers and taking cells by position: ten-year 5.05%, up 6 basis points; twenty-year 5.41, up 7; thirty-year 5.37, up 7 — and two-year 4.67, down 3. The long end sold off while the front end rallied, which is a steepening of nine basis points in the two-to-ten spread in a single session.

This brief has reported the ten-year at or around 5.00 for three sessions and called it a floor question. It is answered for now: 5.05 is the highest close this brief has recorded since 5 October's 5.31 was on the way down, and the move was concentrated where supply is heaviest. The twenty-minus-ten hump widened to +36 basis points, inside the +34 to +37 range it has held all month.

A front end rallying while the long end sells off is the shape markets make when they are pricing the issuer rather than the policy rate: lower short rates expected, more term premium demanded. The Treasury quarterly refunding is on 4 November, the day after the midterms, and it is the event that will test whether this is positioning or a view.

What to watch: the refunding size announcement on 4 November; whether the ten-year holds above 5.00 through the election; and whether the hump breaks its month-long range in either direction, which is the signal this brief has been treating as structural.

Civil service OPM has rewritten the federal personnel framework, and the comments warned about politicisation.

A final rule from the Office of Personnel Management "modernises the framework governing personnel management and strategic human capital management in Federal agencies". Four things: it streamlines human capital planning and reporting, strengthens the authority of agency Chief Human Capital Officers, updates employee survey requirements, and aligns the framework "with Executive branch priorities for a high-performing Federal workforce".

The preamble records what commenters said. They asked OPM to abandon the rule, citing administrative burden, "potential for politicization of the civil service", and anticipated harm to scientific and diversity-related roles. OPM's published response addresses a different objection at length — a claim that the rule lacked a cost-benefit analysis, which OPM rebuts in detail, noting the rule "governs the internal planning, review, and survey processes agencies use to manage their own workforces" and falls below the $100 million threshold for a full impact analysis.

Strengthening a political appointee's authority over human capital policy is the kind of change whose effect is invisible in the rule and visible in hiring three years later. The commenters' concern is not answered by the cost-benefit rebuttal, because it is not a cost-benefit concern.

Below the line, and it is today's negative check. It is the largest story not led on, and it is here rather than above because planning-and-reporting rules produce their effects slowly and this brief read the response to comments rather than the regulatory text.

Confidence: Primary document, preamble only. Sources: FR 2026-22093

Regulatory The Upper C-band rule is final, four days after the clearinghouse deadline that had already expired.

The FCC has published its final rule on the Upper C-band, 3.98–4.2 GHz, the proceeding expanding flexible use of the 3.7–4.2 GHz band. This is the transition whose reimbursement machinery this brief wrote about on 25 October, when the notice announcing the clearinghouse selection criteria was published with a candidate-question deadline of 23 October — three days before the notice appeared.

The order here is the substance behind that administration: it is what creates the costs the clearinghouse will adjudicate, by moving incumbent satellite users and compensating them, and by funding radio altimeter retrofits on aircraft in the adjacent band. Clearinghouse proposals are due 9 November, which is eleven days away and remains the operative date for anyone wanting the job.

Below the line because this brief has not read the rule — only its title and its relationship to the notice already covered. The money involved makes it worth returning to, and it is on the owed list.

US politics The day after the housing order, USDA counts rent from a granny flat toward your mortgage.

A USDA final rule amends the Single Family Housing Guaranteed Loan Program to deal with the income-producing accessory dwelling unit — the garage conversion, the basement flat, the unit over the barn. The programme guarantees mortgages for moderate-income buyers in rural areas.

It lands the day after the executive order this brief led on yesterday, which established a Task Force on Manufactured Housing Innovation and directed proactive cancellation of mortgage insurance. Two housing supply measures from two departments inside twenty-four hours is a programme, not a coincidence — and both work on the same margin, which is making an existing structure count for more rather than building a new one.

Whether rental income from an accessory unit may be counted toward qualifying for a loan is one of the quieter determinants of whether people can buy, and it is set in exactly this kind of rule rather than in legislation.

Below the line because this brief read the title and not the rule, and therefore cannot say in which direction USDA has moved — counting more such income or less. That is the whole content, and stating it unread would be guessing.

Confidence: Identified, not read. Sources: FR 2026-22038 · 28 October

Geopolitics Israel at nearly 90 per cent counted: still no winner.

With close to 90 per cent of votes counted, Israel's election still has no clear winner. Yesterday this brief reported the position at 72 per cent counted — Netanyahu's bloc 54, Gadi Eisenkot's alliance 48, the Arab parties 18 — and the arithmetic that neither bloc reaches the 61 seats needed without the eighteen that neither will ask for.

An additional eighteen points of counting has not changed that. The interesting number remains the Arab parties' 18, a historic result, and the reason the deadlock is arithmetic rather than merely close: there are two available majorities and a political convention against forming either.

Below the line because the count has advanced and the story has not. It returns above the line when the final tally is certified or when the President gives someone the mandate to form a government, whichever comes first — and the second of those is the real event.
