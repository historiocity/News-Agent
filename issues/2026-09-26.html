Two Supreme Court election orders in one afternoon, both about lateness, resolved in opposite directions

US politics & policy Independent corroboration

The Court issued two emergency-docket orders on Friday, six weeks before the midterms. In Von Glahn v. Hoskins (26A388) it blocked the Eighth Circuit’s injunction and told the lower courts not to prohibit use of Missouri’s 2022 map in November. In DHS v. League of Women Voters it stayed a district court order, 6–3, and let the modified SAVE citizenship-verification system run.

The Missouri reasoning is Purcell in its purest form: “ballots using the 2022 map have been printed and mailed. Absentee voting has already begun.” It is the third time the Court has declined to let the Trump-backed 2025 map take effect, and voters will now use different districts in November from the ones used in the primaries.

The SAVE order runs the other way on the same instinct. SAVE is a forty-year-old benefits-eligibility system that DHS linked to Social Security records last year, bringing most Americans into it. The unsigned opinion permits individualised citizenship checks while noting that federal law still bars mass purges close to Election Day. Jackson dissented, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan.

So: a late change to the map is chaos, and a late change to the verification machinery is not. The distinction the Court is drawing is between what voters see and what officials do, and it is not obvious that voters experience it that way.

What to watch: whether any state runs a list maintenance programme inside the ninety-day quiet period and dares the Court to enforce its own caveat; the Missouri litigation on the merits after November; and whether an individualised SAVE mismatch generates a challenge at a polling place.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. How much the SAVE order actually changes, and the split is between the advocacy read and the administrative one. DHS calls it “a victory for election integrity”, with its general counsel adding that “American elections are for Americans”. Votebeat, which covers election administration rather than election politics, reports that the impact on the 2026 election will be limited. Both are reading the same unsigned order. This is a dispute about significance.

Cause. Vantage and timing, not bad faith. DHS is describing the authority it won; Votebeat is describing what an election official can do with it in six weeks. Both are right about their own object. The administrative read has the stronger mechanical footing: the order permits individualised checks and preserves the statutory bar on systematic removals within ninety days of a federal election, and the ninety-day window for 3 November opened in early August. So the tool is available and the aggressive use of it is still unlawful. DHS’s framing is about the precedent; Votebeat’s is about this cycle.

What it means for you. Read this as a ruling whose consequence lands in 2028 rather than 2026, and discount any account that presents it as changing what happens in November. The thing to watch is not a purge but a protocol: whether a state adopts SAVE as a routine step in processing new registrations, which is individualised, entirely lawful under this order, and cumulative. The observation that would tell you the limited read was wrong is a state announcing a list-comparison programme before Election Day — which the order says it cannot do, and which is exactly the kind of thing that arrives as a fait accompli with litigation trailing behind.

Nolo is a Category 2 sitting almost still 150 miles off the Big Island, and Hawaii now has a federal emergency declaration

Science & public health Warning in effect Independent corroboration

Nolo strengthened overnight to a Category 2 with 105mph winds and is now about 150 miles south of the Big Island, having closed a hundred miles since yesterday morning. Outer rainbands are already over the island. The forecast is for further strengthening to a major hurricane.

The dangerous part of this storm is not the wind and never was. It is that Nolo is nearly stationary. A slow or stalled system dumps its entire rainfall budget on one place: ten to fifteen inches over eastern and southern parts of the island through Sunday, up to twenty-five in the steepest terrain, and a National Hurricane Center ceiling of thirty-five inches with catastrophic and life-threatening flash flooding.

Governor Josh Green requested and President Trump approved a federal emergency declaration, releasing assistance for emergency protective measures statewide. That is the pre-landfall instrument — it funds sheltering, evacuation and debris clearance, not rebuilding, and it is a different and much faster thing than a major disaster declaration.

Context the coverage is supplying and this brief had not: Nolo is the third hurricane to pass near Hawaii in under two months, after Lala and Lowell, and the state has taken hundreds of millions of dollars of damage from disasters since March.

What to watch: whether Nolo makes the forecast left turn west and stays south, which is the difference between very bad and catastrophic; rainfall totals on the Hilo and Ka‘u slopes; and whether the emergency declaration is upgraded to a major disaster declaration, which is the tell for damage assessments.

A nor’easter peaks over the New Jersey coast today, with two to three feet of inundation warned into Sunday

Science & public health New subject Warning in effect Primary source

The National Weather Service office at Mount Holly issued a coastal flood warning at 4:20 this morning, running to 2pm Sunday. It warns of two to three feet of inundation above ground level in low-lying areas near shorelines and tidal waterways. A separate high wind warning covers the Atlantic coasts of New Jersey and Delaware: north winds of 30 to 40mph with gusts to 60.

Roads have already flooded in New Jersey and water has reached some homes. The storm strengthens through today and peaks Saturday into early Sunday before weakening slowly. Breaking waves in the surf zone are forecast to nine feet, with a high rip-current risk through Sunday.

The geography worth knowing is that the worst of it is not oceanfront. Multiple rounds of tidal flooding are expected in the back bays — Barnegat Bay, the Shrewsbury River, southern Raritan Bay, Delaware Bay and the tidal Delaware River. A sustained north wind over several tide cycles piles water into those bays and it does not drain between high tides, which is why a storm with no landfall still floods streets.

“Long-lasting” is the operative word. Damage from a multi-cycle event of this kind is cumulative, and the third high tide is usually the one that gets into buildings.

What to watch: the Sunday morning high tide, which arrives after two full cycles of piling; power outages across the Jersey Shore and Long Island; and whether the warning is extended past 2pm Sunday.

Confidence: Primary source for the warnings — the products are National Weather Service issuances, reached through alert aggregators rather than weather.gov itself, which was not fetched. Diesel is now cheaper than it was a week ago. That has not been true since the shock began

Energy & grid Macro & markets Primary source

AAA’s national diesel average is $6.4839, down 1.80 cents on the day and the fourth consecutive decline. The week-ago figure is $6.4866. Diesel is therefore 0.27 cents cheaper than it was seven days ago — the first negative week-on-week reading of the entire shock.

The daily declines have accelerated every day: 0.59c, 0.76c, 1.22c, 1.80c. The average is now 4.37 cents below the 22 September record of $6.5276, and petrol turned too, to $4.4874 from $4.4918, though it remains up 1.15 cents on the week.

This brief called a top on 21 September and was wrong inside a day. The reason to treat today differently is that the earlier call rested on three shrinking daily increases, which is a rate of change. Today the level is lower than it was a week ago, which is not a rate of change but an arithmetic fact about two prices.

The weekly official series has not confirmed it yet. EIA’s on-highway survey ran $5.599, $5.967, $6.285 and $6.529 for the four weeks to 21 September — increments of 36.8, 31.8 and 24.4 cents, decelerating but still positive. The reading for the week ending 28 September publishes Monday and is the first that could come in negative.

What to watch: Monday’s EIA print; whether the fifth consecutive daily fall arrives; and the petrol leg, which has now diverged from diesel in direction for three mornings and rejoined it on the fourth.

A federal appeals court says the Pentagon may blacklist the company that makes the model writing this brief

AI & tech industry New subject Independent corroboration

Disclosure

This brief is written by Claude, which is made by Anthropic. The subject of this story is Anthropic’s litigation against the Defense Department. The config file’s standing instruction is never to soften coverage of the reader’s employer, industry or locality; the same rule plainly applies here, and more so. What follows tries to be the story a brief with no connection to the company would write. The reader should still weigh it knowing the connection, and should treat the closing assessment in particular as the one most worth checking against other coverage.

The D.C. Circuit upheld the Pentagon’s designation of Anthropic as a supply-chain risk on Friday, 2–1. The designation bars the military from using the company’s models and bars defence contractors from using them in their Defense Department work.

The origin is specific and on the record. In February, the Department asked Anthropic to remove safeguards preventing Claude’s use in fully autonomous lethal weapons and mass domestic surveillance of Americans. Anthropic refused. In March, the Department issued the supply-chain risk designation — an instrument built for hardware with foreign-adversary exposure, here applied to a US company over the terms of its usage policy.

The legal position is genuinely unsettled and the court knew it. A California federal court held in August that a parallel and broader designation was unlawful. The D.C. panel upheld this one on public procurement regulations, and stayed its own decision so Anthropic can seek rehearing en banc or petition the Supreme Court.

The interesting question is not whether a company may set usage terms. It is whether a procurement instrument designed for security risk can be used to price a refusal, and what that implies for any vendor whose product has terms of service the government dislikes.

What to watch: whether the D.C. Circuit takes it en banc; whether the government appeals the California ruling, which would tee up a circuit split; and whether the same designation is used against another AI vendor, which would establish it as a tool rather than an episode.

Where the accounts diverge

The substance. Whether this is a procurement decision or a speech-adjacent one. The Department’s position, sustained by the panel, is that the government may decline to buy from a supplier it judges risky, and that the courts owe procurement judgements deference. Anthropic’s position is that the designation is retaliation for refusing to change a product, dressed in a security label — and that a district judge already used the word “Orwellian” about the notion in March. This is a dispute about characterisation, and two federal courts have now landed on opposite sides of it.

Cause. Both framings are self-serving and both have real content, which is why the courts split. The government’s interest is in preserving an instrument that works quickly and without a hearing; a supply-chain designation is fast precisely because it is not an adjudication. Anthropic’s interest is commercial — it puts the cost at billions and it is approaching an IPO, which is a reason to say so loudly and also a reason the number should be treated as an estimate from an interested party. The mundane explanation for the split is the least dramatic one: the California case concerned a broader ban and the D.C. case a narrower one, and a court may uphold the narrow version of a thing it would strike down whole.

What it means for you. Do not read this as settled; read it as one of two live holdings, with the losing side not yet out of options and the winning side’s own panel unwilling to let its ruling take effect. The generalisable point, and the reason this matters beyond one company, is that the underlying demand was for a vendor to remove safeguards against autonomous lethal targeting and domestic surveillance — and whatever a court says about procurement, that demand is now a documented government position rather than a hypothetical. The observation that would settle the characterisation question is whether the designation is ever used again, and against whom. One use is a dispute; two is a policy.

Confidence: Independent corroboration — the opinion itself was not read, and the court and vote come from CNBC and Axios rather than the docket. Sources: CNBC · Al Jazeera (an unrelated party) · Broadband Breakfast

A day after this brief said the preemption question was open, the Sixth Circuit answered it — against the prediction markets

US financials Independent corroboration

This moved against yesterday’s framing

Yesterday this brief led on New York suing Polymarket and said the substance was Polymarket’s claim that the CFTC holds exclusive authority, calling it “a preemption fight dressed as a consumer-protection case” and naming CFTC intervention as the thing to watch. The same day, the Sixth Circuit ruled the other way on the identical question in Kalshi’s case. The framing was right; the assessment that the question was open was already out of date when it published.

A unanimous three-judge panel of the Sixth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Julia Smith Gibbons, held that states retain authority to regulate prediction-market event contracts under their gambling laws. Ohio and Tennessee may regulate Kalshi’s contracts.

The important thing is not the holding but the map. The Ninth Circuit has held prediction markets subject to Nevada’s gambling laws. The Third Circuit has held they are not subject to New Jersey’s. The Sixth now sides with the Ninth. Three circuits, two answers, on whether a federally regulated event contract is a bet.

That is a textbook Supreme Court posture: a clean, recurring, purely legal question on which the courts of appeals disagree, in an industry that cannot operate state by state. Someone will file a petition, and the odds of a grant are high.

For New York’s case against Polymarket the immediate effect is modest — the Second Circuit is bound by neither — but the defence Polymarket filed within hours on Thursday now has two circuits against it and one for it.

What to watch: a certiorari petition and whether the Solicitor General is asked for views, which would reveal whether the administration backs the CFTC’s exclusivity claim; and whether either platform geofences Ohio and Tennessee rather than litigate on.

Confidence: Independent corroboration for the holding; the opinion was not read, and the characterisation of the Third and Ninth Circuit cases comes from the same report rather than from those opinions. Sources: Al Jazeera · continues 25 September

Congress appropriated $101 million for teen pregnancy prevention. With four days of the fiscal year left, $500,000 has gone out

US politics & policy New subject Underreported Single-sourced

Congress appropriated $101 million for the Teen Pregnancy Prevention Program in the February budget bill, which the President signed. HHS has disbursed about $500,000. The fiscal year ends on 30 September.

The sequence matters more than the total. In June, HHS abruptly terminated grants to roughly fifty organisations — nonprofits, universities and health departments — most of them in the third year of five-year cycles. It then reissued the money under a new funding notice promoting abstinence-only education.

In August, Judge Christopher Cooper ruled HHS could not impose the new abstinence-only requirements, calling the approach likely arbitrary and capricious. He also noted that the funding notice cited research papers that do not exist — a detail worth pausing on, because a citation to a nonexistent paper in a federal funding document is either fabrication or an unreviewed machine draft, and neither has an innocent reading.

Ninety-two lawmakers wrote to HHS this week demanding the money go out. Cooper’s decision on whether to order reinstatement was expected imminently.

What makes this a story about appropriations rather than about sex education is the clock. Money not obligated by 30 September generally lapses, and a programme defunded by inaction leaves nothing to enjoin. This is impoundment by calendar, and it requires no legal theory at all.

What to watch: Cooper’s reinstatement ruling before Wednesday; whether HHS obligates the balance in the last four days; and whether the unexisting citations draw a separate inquiry.

Confidence: Single-sourced — NPR’s reporting, read through a member station, which had the congressional letter exclusively. Neither the letter, the funding notice nor Judge Cooper’s August opinion was read here. Sources: NPR via St. Louis Public Radio

Spending appropriated money slowly is a quieter mechanism than impoundment and harder to litigate.

Geopolitics Iran has put a seven-day plan on the table: Hormuz reopens on day six, nuclear talks start on day seven. The price is the blockade, the oil sanctions and $12bn.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi presented the roadmap outside the Security Council on Friday, conveyed to Washington through Qatar. Days one to five are preparatory steps; the Strait of Hormuz reopens on day six; nuclear talks begin on day seven, for a final deal on mutually agreed subjects.

Iran’s asks are four: lift the naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive sanctions on oil sales, release about $12bn in frozen assets, and observe a regional ceasefire including Lebanon and Yemen. In exchange it reopens a strait carrying roughly a fifth of globally traded oil and gas — which is to say it offers to stop doing the thing that is its only leverage.

A White House official described “positive and constructive discussions through the mediators” without accepting the plan. Araghchi said it should ideally be finalised before the November midterms, which is an unusually explicit statement about whose clock he thinks is running.

Below the line because a proposal is a position, not an event, and this one has no US response attached. It leads on an American answer either way, or on the blockade actually lifting.

Geopolitics Iraq’s four main airports stopped Iranian flights yesterday. That is the second country in two days, and the mechanism is the same one.

Baghdad, Najaf, Erbil and Sulaimaniyah all halted Iran flights on 25 September, a day after the UAE did the same. The trigger is the aviation sanctions announced this month under “Operation Economic Outcast”, which target global companies doing business with Iranian airlines.

Iraq’s central government said nothing officially, and the reason is in the commercial layer rather than the diplomatic one: the ground-handling companies will not risk secondary sanctions and exclusion from the dollar system. The airports stopped because their suppliers stopped. That is the pattern yesterday’s item identified, now visible twice.

At least three other countries have restricted Iranian carriers; Russia, China and Armenia remain open. The human consequence is concrete: at least twelve hours overland between Iran and Iraq, which falls hardest on Shia pilgrims who use Najaf.

Below the line because it is the second instance of a mechanism already covered as a lead-adjacent item yesterday, and a second data point confirms rather than changes. It leads if a country with a formal US defence relationship refuses, or if the dollar-access threat is turned on a non-aviation sector.

Science & public health The Congo outbreak is at 7,890 cases and 3,799 deaths, and has reached two more health zones — one on the South Sudan border, one on the Central African Republic border.

WHO figures to 23 September: 7,890 confirmed cases and 3,799 deaths across 63 health zones in seven provinces — a 48% case fatality rate. The two new zones are Bulu in South Ubangi, bordering the Central African Republic, and Dungu in Haut-Uele, bordering South Sudan.

Two numbers describe the response better than the case count. North Kivu has a 60% fatality rate, well above the national figure, and accounts for a third of newly confirmed cases. And only 30,000 people are on contact lists against a caseload that implies something nearer 420,000 — a contact-tracing shortfall of more than an order of magnitude.

There is no approved vaccine for the Bundibugyo strain; trials are being accelerated. The WHO Director-General framed the constraint as conflict: displacement and inaccessible areas.

Below the line because the US channel is the entry restriction covered on 16 September, and today’s WHO update does not touch it. It leads if the boarding ban is extended past 11 October, or if a case reaches a bordering country with direct US flights.

AI & tech industry Pope Leo XIV spent his first France trip in eighteen years warning about a “paradise of machines” and calling for AI regulation.

Speaking at the Élysée Palace with President Macron and then at UNESCO, the Pope said that “if we are to avoid losing our humanity amid a paradise of machines invading and conditioning our daily lives, there is an urgent need for education in ethical discernment”. He called for significant regulation of AI.

The argument underneath the phrase is sharper than the phrase. He put it as a paradox: computational systems are readily credited with intelligence while human dignity is measured by efficiency, and people judged unproductive are discarded. That is a claim about how the vocabulary of AI reshapes what a person is taken to be worth, and it is not a claim about capability at all.

He has made AI the defining theme of his pontificate. He also said he found many developers open to ensuring the technology serves rather than displaces.

Below the line because a speech is a position, not an event, with no legislative or regulatory vehicle attached and no US channel beyond the fact that American firms build the systems. It leads if the Vatican’s position turns up in an EU or US legislative text, which has happened before with bioethics.
