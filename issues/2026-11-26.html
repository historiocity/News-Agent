A judge has stopped the FCC from going after ABC's licences: “This is not a close case”

US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

US District Judge Loren AliKhan issued a preliminary injunction on Wednesday barring the Federal Communications Commission from taking further action against ABC's local television stations, and denied the commission's motion to dismiss Disney's lawsuit. “This is not a close case,” she wrote. The FCC may not continue its early review of ABC's station licences while the case proceeds. She found that Disney's lawyers had “sufficiently cast the early license renewal proceedings as a retaliatory act”.

The sequence she describes is short and dated. ABC's licences were not due for renewal for several years. In April, FCC chairman Brendan Carr ordered the company to file early-renewal paperwork — something the commission virtually never does. At the same time the president was publicly pressing ABC to fire a late-night host, and the network did not comply. Carr said the review related to an ongoing FCC inquiry into Disney's diversity, equity and inclusion efforts; former FCC officials said that did not make sense and Disney argued it was a pretext.

The judge's own words on the mechanism are the part to keep. “No one doubts that the commission may enforce the law in the manner it sees fit. But in doing so, defendants may not retaliate against or punish plaintiffs and other broadcast license holders for disfavored speech.” And: Carr “has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders”, and “it is no coincidence that President Trump criticized Mr. Kimmel's skit on social media and the very next day defendants initiated the early license renewal proceedings”.

Responses. ABC: “an important victory for the First Amendment”. An FCC spokesperson: the decision is “wrong on the facts and wrong on the law”, and the commission “will continue to use our lawful authority to hold broadcasters accountable to their public interest obligations”. The FCC's lone Democratic commissioner, Anna Gomez, who publicly supported the suit: the ruling shows “this FCC is a paper tiger whose threats crumble when challenged by those willing to defend their rights”, and she urged other broadcasters to “take note” and follow Disney.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether an early licence review is regulation or retaliation. The commission's position is that it has lawful authority over broadcasters who use the public airwaves and was exercising it. The court's finding is that the authority is real and the use of it here was retaliatory, which are not in conflict. A dispute about motive — normally the hardest kind — that a court resolved because the timing was documented to the day.

Cause. The reason this was “not a close case” is not that regulating broadcasters is improper; it is that the commission did something it virtually never does, immediately after the president complained about a programme. Reach for the ordinary explanation first and it fails on the calendar: an early-renewal order years ahead of schedule, issued the day after a presidential social media post about a comedy sketch, with a stated rationale about diversity policies that former officials at the same agency said made no sense. That is why the judge could write about marching orders in a published opinion rather than inferring intent. What the injunction does not do is decide the merits — it preserves the position while the case runs, and the commission says it expects to prevail. What it does establish, today, is that a licence holder who sues can stop this, which is precisely the point Commissioner Gomez made in urging others to try.

What it means for you. Nothing changes about what is on television. What changes is the cost of the threat: a broadcaster now has a district court opinion to cite. Believe the injunction, the quoted passages, the April early-renewal order and both sides' statements — two newsrooms carry them and the opinion is public. This brief has not read the opinion itself, only reporting that quotes it. What would settle the direction: whether the FCC appeals and how fast; whether any other broadcaster files a similar suit, which is the test of Gomez's advice; whether the DEI inquiry proceeds separately; and whether the early-renewal mechanism is used against anyone else.

What to watch: an FCC appeal; suits by other licence holders; the separate DEI inquiry; and whether early-renewal orders are issued elsewhere.

The same commission, the same day, authorised a million satellites

AI & tech industry New subject Underreported Independent corroboration

On Wednesday the FCC authorised SpaceX to deploy one million satellites — “orbital compute modules” forming a constellation called Starmind, intended to run data centres in orbit for artificial intelligence workloads on Earth. Chairman Brendan Carr: “America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence… opens a bold new frontier.” The order requires at least one authorised space station by 2033, with more added gradually through 2040.

The physics objection is specific and comes from a named astrophysicist. Adam Becker called the prospect “a fantasy”: “It's not going to happen.” The case for orbital data centres is solar power and the cold of space; the problem is that space is a vacuum, which traps heat in an object the way a thermos does. Becker's point is that there are “basic physical limits” to radiating that heat away, and that spreading the load across many small satellites could in principle carry enough radiating material but would be expensive and difficult. He also warns of atmospheric pollution from launches and re-entries, and of in-orbit collisions producing “a cascading set of space junk collisions” and “a cloud of debris” in low Earth orbit, where weather and telecommunications satellites sit.

The objectors were not only scientists. The American Astronomical Society, the satellite operator Viasat and Amazon's Kuiper Systems all asked the commission to deny the application. The AAS said it was “disheartened and dismayed”, and that putting data centres in low Earth orbit would undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and “the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky”. The FCC said it received a “substantial number” of comments including petitions to deny, held that the constellation complies with its orbital debris rules and serves the public interest, and dismissed topics outside its authority — among them environmental concerns.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this will ever be built. The commission's position is that authorisation serves the public interest and the debris rules are satisfied. The scientific position is not that it is dangerous but that it is impossible: a vacuum will not let you dump that much waste heat. A dispute about facts, and an unusual one, because the regulator has approved something its critics say cannot exist.

Cause. Read the order's own timetable and the dispute shrinks. One authorised space station by 2033, more gradually through 2040. That is not a million satellites; it is a licence to begin, with seven years before the first milestone. Reach for that before treating either the announcement or the fantasy charge as settled: authorising a constellation and building one are different acts, and the FCC has authorised constellations before that were never completed. What is substantively decided today is narrower and more consequential: the commission explicitly set aside environmental objections as outside its authority, and found the debris plan adequate over the objections of the astronomical community and two commercial competitors. That finding, not the satellite count, is what a future applicant will cite. And set it beside this issue's first lead. The same chairman, on the same Wednesday, was found by a federal judge to have taken a president's rhetoric as his marching orders in a licensing matter, and announced that America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence in another. This brief draws no causal link between the two and notes that both are licensing decisions by one commission on one day.

What it means for you. If you look at the night sky or rely on weather satellites, the thing to watch is the debris and brightness record, not the launch count. Believe the authorisation, the 2033 and 2040 milestones, the named objectors and the FCC's own statement that it dismissed environmental topics. Treat “one million satellites” as the ceiling of an authorisation, not a plan with funding. This brief has not read the authorisation order. What would settle whether it is real: whether SpaceX files a launch schedule; the thermal design, which is the claim Becker says cannot be met; whether the AAS or Viasat seek review; and whether the first authorised station flies before 2033.

What to watch: any petition for reconsideration or appeal; a published thermal design; launch filings; and the first milestone in 2033.

The president's accounts traded Nvidia 44 times this year, and his vice president says nobody was investigating

AI & tech industry Continuation Underreported Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief led on the attorney general's refusal to say whether Jensen Huang had been cleared. Two answers arrived, and a third fact arrived that nobody had asked for.

First, the denial. Vice President JD Vance, in Dayton on Wednesday: “We were told that he was under no investigation… the accusations against him were based on anonymous reporting, not on actual evidence.” He said the attorney general was among “multiple people” in government who gave him those assurances, and that people should not lose their jobs over anonymously sourced accusations. Asked on Tuesday whether Huang had been investigated, the attorney general had replied: “For what?” — and then, pressed: “The FBI, law enforcement, looks into things all the time. That doesn't necessarily become a criminal investigation.” Sources say the president asked Vance to help get Huang reinstated.

Second, what is actually under investigation, which is a different entity. The woman involved is Alice Huang — no relation — an executive at Megaspeed, an Nvidia customer. The Commerce Department opened a probe into Megaspeed in 2024 and conducted an on-site check with Singaporean law enforcement in June 2025, interviewing her and identifying her as the company's chief executive. The probe examined whether Megaspeed diverted US semiconductors to entities in China in breach of export controls. The Justice Department has since taken the lead on a criminal investigation into Megaspeed. On the espionage question, intelligence officials told congressional staff on Tuesday that there is no evidence she was a Chinese spy or intelligence asset, and no evidence she began the affair to collect intelligence — but that she is believed to have passed information along after it began.

Third, the fact nobody asked for. BBC Verify, reviewing the president's financial disclosures to the Office of Government Ethics, found his investment portfolios bought or sold Nvidia shares at least 44 times this year. Between January and September they purchased between $5.2m and $17m of Nvidia stock and sold between $4.4m and $12m; they held between $12.5m and $58m at the end of 2025. Disclosures report ranges, so no more precise figure is possible. The recent turmoil is not covered by the disclosures. The White House says the portfolio is independently managed by third parties and that neither the president nor his family “has any ability to direct, influence or provide input” on it. Nvidia is more than 6% of the S&P 500; its shares rose 31% on Monday on Huang's return, adding about $1 trillion in market value, and remain below their pre-scandal level. The president said he had “made the deal” to “convince” the board. A presidential historian at the Miller Center: “no precedent for this… It's beyond anything we've ever seen in the presidency.”

Republicans are still not satisfied. Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick, a former FBI agent on the House Intelligence Committee, wants to hear from the FBI and CIA before Huang testifies next week: “What briefings did they give? When did they give them?” He also wants the attorney general and the commerce secretary. “Anytime you do an investigation, you follow the facts wherever they lead… You leave no stone unturned.”

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether there is anything left to investigate. The administration's position, now stated by the vice president and the attorney general, is that Huang was under no investigation and the accusations rest on anonymous reporting. Fitzpatrick's position, from inside the same party, is that the question is not whether Huang was a target but what the agencies knew and when they told him. A dispute about scope, and both sides may be accurate.

Cause. The two claims are compatible and separating them is the whole job. “Jensen Huang is not under investigation” and “the Justice Department is running a criminal investigation into Megaspeed over chip diversion to China, and the woman he was involved with is its chief executive and is believed to have passed information” can both be true at once. Reach for that before reading the denials as a cover-up or as exoneration: the subject of an export-control case is usually the company, not the counterparty's romantic partner, and the attorney general's own formulation — the FBI “looks into things all the time” — concedes activity short of a named target. What the denials do not address is the question Fitzpatrick asked, which is about warnings rather than charges. And the 44 trades are a separate matter again. They are not evidence of anything improper — the disclosures are the government's own, the trades predate the turmoil, the White House says a third party manages the account, and a stock that is 6% of the index sits in most diversified portfolios, including pensions. What they establish is a structural fact: the president intervened to restore the chief executive of a company his portfolios trade, and the only reason anyone can say so with figures is that the disclosure system worked.

What it means for you. Next week is when this becomes checkable: Huang behind closed doors on Tuesday, in public on Thursday. Believe the Vance and Blanche quotations, the Megaspeed probe timeline, the congressional briefing's conclusions and the BBC's trade counts — the last are from published disclosures. Do not treat “no evidence she was a spy” as the end of it: the same briefing says she is believed to have passed information, and that is an export-control question rather than an espionage one. What would settle it: whether the FBI and CIA brief Congress before Thursday; what the Megaspeed criminal investigation charges, if anything; whether Huang invokes the Fifth; and whether the disclosures covering October and November show any trading during the turmoil.

What to watch: next week's hearings; FBI and CIA briefings; the Megaspeed criminal case; the next tranche of financial disclosures; and whether the board answers who in government advised it.

New tariffs on China, the EU and a dozen others, built on a law the courts have not yet struck down

Economy & supply chains New subject Single source

The administration announced on Wednesday that it will impose a fresh round of tariffs on goods from China, the European Union and a dozen other economies, accusing them of overproduction in some industries at the expense of American companies. China would rise to 20%, in line with its existing agreement — and could double to 40% if talks collapse. The EU would be taxed at 15%, in compliance with the 2025 US–EU deal, rising to as much as 20%. Japan, South Korea and Switzerland: 15%, with Korea potentially 25% and the other two 20%. India: 17% if a trade pact is finalised, potentially 25%. Several smaller Asian economies are included; Mexico and Norway were considered and left out for now. The measures are subject to public comment and a final action could come as early as January.

The legal route is the point of the exercise. The administration's earlier country-by-country tariffs, imposed using emergency powers, were struck down as unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in February. This round follows a probe begun in March under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a process widely seen as more resistant to legal challenge. The rates are not expected to exceed levels already agreed in existing deals.

Reaction was muted where it mattered. China's foreign ministry called the prospect “political maneuvering” and said the two countries should maintain their trade truce. A European Commission spokesman: “we strongly reject the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity”, adding that the bloc expects any new levies to abide by the tariff agreement. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer: “A lot of these deals we made actually solve a lot of the problems we see in excess capacity… They know what we're proposing. I think a lot of this is baked in.” By Thursday morning the Chinese position had hardened: Beijing says it reserves the right to take “measures” in response. Vietnam, separately, is seeking a trade deal with Washington as the tariffs threaten its clothing and footwear exports.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is an escalation. The trade representative's position is that it is not — the rates sit inside deals already struck and partners have been told. The Chinese and European statements reject the premise while declining to threaten retaliation. A dispute about significance that all three parties appear content to leave unresolved.

Cause. The legal history explains the shape of the announcement better than the economics do. A tariff wall built on emergency powers was struck down in February; Section 301 requires an investigation, a finding and a comment period, which is slower but survives. Reach for that before reading the rates as a negotiating position: the administration is rebuilding an agenda it lost in court, using an instrument designed in 1974 for exactly this kind of finding, and setting rates that mostly ratify deals already signed. That is why nobody is retaliating — there is little new to retaliate against yet, and the headroom clauses (40% for China, 25% for Korea and India, 20% for the EU) are the actual leverage. The politics cut the other way and are not hidden: Democrats won the midterms this month partly on the cost of living and on disapproval of the president's handling of the economy, and this is a tax on imports announced three weeks later.

What it means for you. Nothing is in force: this is a proposal with a comment period and a January target. Believe the rates, the headroom figures, the Section 301 basis and the February Supreme Court ruling. This brief has not read the USTR notice and does not know which products are covered — which is the difference between a headline rate and a price change you would notice. What would settle the effect: the product lists in the Federal Register notice; whether the comment period changes anything; whether China's truce holds past January; and whether Mexico and Norway are added.

What to watch: the Federal Register notice and product lists; the comment period; January's final action; the US–China truce, which expires in January; and whether Mexico is added.

The lead azide was detonated, the monitors read zero, and the soil has not been tested yet

US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Yesterday this brief wrote that the single most important thing to watch was whether ambient monitoring was in place and whether its results were published, because absent that there would be no way to know whether the mitigations worked. Monitoring was in place and the results were published. Two hazy plumes rose over the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant on Wednesday morning. EPA monitoring tools showed airborne lead holding at 0.0 through the day, and the Army announced an all-clear just after 6:30pm Eastern — nearly nine hours after the detonation — saying government mapping had detected zero airborne lead. The shelter-in-place advice for residents within 20 miles was lifted; FEMA had offered lodging through Friday to those who left.

The Army's own next sentence is the one that matters: “Post-detonation surface sampling is underway, and results will be released once complete.” Air is the fast measurement and the favourable one. Lead that leaves the air arrives on the ground. Researchers quoted stress that surface and soil testing still have to be rolled out in the coming days. The US Department of Agriculture says about 350 farms could be affected based on the expected plume direction, and advises farmers to work with veterinarians to monitor livestock. The Iowa Department of Agriculture's advisory: power wash the outside of barns and buildings, fencing, livestock feeders and portable water tanks, trailers, grooming supplies and other farm equipment after the dust particles settle.

Two statements worth setting against each other. The acting Army secretary and others publicised the 0.0 readings and thanked the president for providing the “fix” to Middletown's lead problem. The Health and Human Services secretary wrote: “We will continue working with Iowa officials and our federal partners to assess potential health risks, share findings, and respond to residents' needs… Iowa families deserve clear answers about what the monitoring shows, what remains under investigation, and what they should do to protect their health.” And the medical director of the Iowa Poison Control Center: there is no safe amount of lead in the human body, and “even small amounts in children or patients or pregnant individuals can cause long-term health effects”.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Whether this is over. The Army's position is that the operation worked: zero airborne lead, all-clear, normal activities resumed. The researchers', the agriculture departments' and the health secretary's position is that the airborne measurement is the first of several and the ground has not been tested. A dispute about timing rather than facts — nobody disputes the 0.0 readings.

Cause. Zero airborne lead nine hours after a detonation is a real result and should be read as one: the sand barriers, water and gel blocks appear to have done what they were for. Reach for that before assuming the readings are spin. But the measurement answers a narrower question than the announcements imply. A plume that deposits lead on the ground produces low airborne readings precisely because the material has landed — which is why the Army's own release pairs the all-clear with surface sampling still under way, and why the agriculture advisory is about washing barns and feeders rather than about air. The 350 farms in the plume path are the population for whom the relevant measurement has not yet been taken. Note also what nine hours means: the all-clear came long after the event, from mapping rather than from a published monitoring network, and this brief has still not seen the dispersion modelling it asked for last Friday.

What it means for you. If you are inside the twenty-mile ring, the operative advice is the agriculture departments': wash exterior surfaces after the dust settles, and have livestock checked. Believe the 0.0 airborne readings, the all-clear time, the 350-farm estimate and the poison control director's statement on lead. Do not treat zero airborne lead as a finding about soil; the Army does not, and says so. What would settle it: the surface sampling results and when they are released; whether soil testing covers the full plume path including the four- and eighteen-mile distances residents named last week; whether EPA publishes its monitoring data rather than describing it; and whether the dispersion modelling is ever released, which is now a three-item owed list on this one story.

What to watch: surface and soil sampling results; publication of EPA monitoring data; the dispersion modelling; livestock findings on the 350 farms; and whether any follow-up testing is scheduled.

American refineries are running almost flat out and the distillate is not piling up

Energy & grid Continuation Underreported Primary source

The weekly petroleum status report landed this morning, and it landed incomplete. Three of the six series this brief tracks updated to the week ending 20 November; three did not.

What updated. Refinery utilisation rose to 97.5%, from 95.7% the week before — a jump of 1.8 points, and the third-highest weekly reading of 2026 out of 47. The year's maximum is 98.0% on 28 August; the minimum 88.6% on 20 February. Distillate stocks rose to 105,616 thousand barrels, up 715 on the week — which is 28th of 47, squarely mid-range, and still near the year's low of 99,259 set on 30 October. Distillate product supplied — demand — rose to 3,917, up 132, 21st of 47.

What did not update: the two price series the crack spread is built from. WTI still reads $90.78 and ULSD New York Harbor $4.265, both for the week ending 13 November. So the crack spread this file has tracked since early November still stands at $88.35 and cannot be recomputed today. Retail diesel, which updated on Tuesday, reads $5.695 for the week to 23 November, down 24.2 cents in a week.

The combination is the finding. Refineries are running within half a point of their 2026 peak, demand is unremarkable, and distillate stocks rose by seven-tenths of a percent. Flat-out production, ordinary demand, and almost no build.

Divergence

The point in dispute. There is no reported dispute, because nobody reported this — which is why it runs. The reading implied by the falling pump price is that the distillate squeeze is ending. The reading implied by the stock figures is that it is not: a 97.5% utilisation week that adds 715 thousand barrels is not rebuilding anything. A dispute about facts, between two parts of the same release.

Cause. Take the boring possibilities first, because two of them are real. Utilisation near its annual peak in late November is partly seasonal — autumn maintenance ends and refiners run hard into winter — and a single week's stock change is noise at this scale. And 715 on a base of 105,616 is well inside normal weekly variation. Reach for those before concluding anything is wrong. What survives them is the level rather than the change. Stocks sit 28th of 47 weeks and barely above the year's low, after a month in which refiners have been running at or near capacity. If the squeeze this file has tracked since early November were resolving, the inventory would be visible by now, and it is not. Which suggests the fall in pump prices is being delivered by crude — WTI was $90.78 a fortnight ago against $107.17 on 23 October — rather than by distillate supply recovering. That is a hypothesis this brief cannot test today, because the two series that would test it did not update.

What it means for you. Cheaper diesel at the pump is not the same as more diesel in the system, and this morning's numbers are consistent with the first without the second. Believe the three updated figures and their ranks — read from EIA's own tables this morning and ranked across all 47 weeks of 2026. Believe nothing about the crack spread today: the inputs are a fortnight old and this brief will not compute a spread from stale prices. What would settle it: next week's WTI and ULSD readings; whether stocks build or draw at this utilisation; and whether utilisation holds above 97% once seasonal maintenance effects are past. If stocks are still flat at 97% utilisation in a fortnight, the squeeze is structural rather than seasonal.

What to watch: next week's WTI and ULSD updates; the stock trajectory at high utilisation; whether utilisation passes 98.0%; and the retail series, which is now running ahead of the wholesale ones.

No analyst commentary has been read, and this brief found no report on these figures in any of its eight front-page sources. Sources: EIA refinery utilisation · EIA distillate stocks · 25 November

Lukoil's overseas empire has been sold to an American consortium, and the US government took a stake

Energy & grid New subject Underreported Single source

A consortium led by the US investor Todd Boehly has agreed to buy Lukoil International — the entire overseas portfolio of Russia's second-largest oil producer — from its sanctioned Russian parent. The consortium includes the US International Development Finance Corporation, so the deal will give the US government an equity stake in the entity. The price was not disclosed. Eldridge, Boehly's firm, says the transaction will “secure critical global energy assets under American and allied ownership”.

What is being transferred is substantial. Upstream operations in 11 countries producing around 350,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day; oil and gas fields; three refineries in Europe; 2,000 retail filling stations across 16 countries in Europe, North America and Central Asia; and an international trading business.

The structure is the part to understand. The assets are being acquired by share swap: restricted shares in the Russian parent and other Russian companies are exchanged for Lukoil International's equity. Eldridge says this “avoids any direct cash payment to Russia under the transaction”. Other partners are UAE-based Catena Investment Holding and the US investment bank Xtellus Partners. The transaction requires conditional approval from the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control.

It took more than a year and two failed attempts. Lukoil put the assets up for sale after the Treasury sanctioned it, and Rosneft, to pressure Moscow over the war. A bid by the Swiss commodities trader Gunvor, now Centalion, collapsed when Treasury would not approve it. A Carlyle agreement in January, excluding the Kazakh assets, also fell apart. A research fellow at RUSI: the deal “demonstrates the efficiency of sanctions… Western sanctions are forcing one of Russia's largest oil companies to relinquish international assets accumulated over decades”, and moving refineries and filling stations to non-Russian owners will “reduce Moscow's trade influence over important energy infrastructure and remove a source of future earnings”.

Divergence

The point in dispute. What this proves. The sanctions-efficiency reading, stated on the record by a RUSI researcher, is that pressure worked: Russia is losing assets it spent decades assembling. The reading nobody in this report offers is the obvious counter: a forced sale at an undisclosed price, structured to avoid paying cash, is a transfer of value whose terms are unknown, and the buyer's consortium includes the government that imposed the sanctions. A dispute about significance that is one-sided in the reporting and should not be in the reading.

Cause. The share-swap structure explains both the delay and the shape. Sanctions bar paying a sanctioned entity, so a cash purchase was never available — which is why Gunvor's bid failed on Treasury approval and why this one exchanges restricted Russian shares for the international equity instead. Reach for that before reading the structure as a dodge: it is the only form such a sale can take, and OFAC still has to approve it. What is genuinely unusual is the DFC's participation. A US development finance agency taking equity in a former Russian oil business is not a sanctions enforcement action; it is an acquisition of strategic infrastructure, and the Eldridge statement says so in those words. The buyer is also notable: Eldridge manages more than $75 billion but in sports, entertainment, credit and real estate, and its principal is best known as a part-owner of the Dodgers and Lakers. That is not a disqualification — the DFC's presence suggests who is actually underwriting the strategic logic — but it is the reason to want the price.

What it means for you. If you buy fuel in Europe or Central Asia, the owner of the forecourt may change; nothing else does yet. Believe the asset list, the structure, the consortium members and the OFAC condition. The price is not disclosed and this brief has no basis to say whether it is fair, nor any valuation of the restricted shares being swapped — which is the single number that would make this assessable. The fight over the immigration agenda is not in Congress; it is across the street

US politics & policy New subject Single source

Democrats take control of Congress in January and have promised to constrain the immigration crackdown. The decisions that will determine most of it are at the Supreme Court, and they are expected by late June. Three appeals are coming: the asylum ban; the vast expansion of mandatory detention of migrants; and the policy of sending people in the country illegally to third countries rather than their homelands — including some with poor human rights records. That last one is the question this brief wrote yesterday had gone unanswered when Kilmar Abrego Garcia left for Spain rather than test it. It is being answered anyway, by other litigants.

The record at this court is mixed, which is the reason to be careful about predictions. Its unprecedented push to end birthright citizenship was rejected. The court limited the use of the 1798 Alien Enemies Act for rapid removals. But in June, six-justice majorities allowed the administration to end Temporary Protected Status and to limit asylum claims made at the border.

Both sides are on the record. The ACLU's Cecillia Wang, opposing in two of the three cases: “In all three of these cases, the Trump administration is trying to rewrite laws enacted by Congress, in service of a radical anti-immigrant agenda that has hurt not only immigrants, but the rule of law. These Trump policies are not only illegal but also unpopular.” A White House spokeswoman: “If the American Civil Liberties Union cares more about criminal illegal aliens than they do about American citizens, then they should change their name.”

And the congressional half is weaker than the rhetoric. A senator-elect has vowed to “rip ICE apart” and the incoming speaker says Democrats will “put an end to Donald Trump's violent mass deportation machine”. But Republicans have already front-loaded ICE funding for years, and the architect of the crackdown was named White House chief of staff this month.

Divergence

The point in dispute. Where the agenda will be decided. Democratic leaders are describing a legislative fight they are about to win the majority for. The reporting's argument is that the appropriations were made in advance and the policies are already in litigation, so the operative venue is a 6–3 court. A dispute about significance, and the calendar favours the second reading: the new Congress sits on 3 January and the decisions land by late June.

Cause. Front-loaded funding is the mechanism and it is the least discussed part of this. A majority can refuse to appropriate; it cannot easily un-appropriate money already granted for several years, and this brief reported GAO's finding on Monday that ICE holds $45 billion available through fiscal 2029. Reach for that before treating January as a turning point: the money is spent in the sense that matters. What a majority can do is subpoena, investigate and legislate — and this file has spent a week on an executive order designed to blunt the first of those. Against which, the court's record cuts both ways and should not be reduced to its composition: the same bench rejected the birthright citizenship push and limited the Alien Enemies Act, while permitting the TPS termination and the border asylum limits. It has not behaved as a rubber stamp, and anyone predicting these three outcomes from the 6–3 split is predicting beyond the evidence.

What it means for you. If you are affected by asylum rules, mandatory detention or third-country removal, the dates that matter are argument and decision at the Supreme Court, not the congressional calendar. Believe the three cases, the late-June timing and the quoted positions. This brief has not read the petitions and does not have the case names or argument dates — which is what it would need to tell you when each is heard, and is owed. What would settle the direction: the argument dates; whether the court grants certiorari in all three or narrows the questions; and whether any decision addresses removal to a country with no connection to the person, which is the one this file has been waiting on since Tuesday.

What to watch: certiorari grants and argument dates; whether the third-country question is reached; the June decisions; and whether the incoming Congress attempts to legislate before them.
